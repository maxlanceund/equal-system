# Reflections on the Dream of Red Mansions

The love we call family, the love we call romance——all of it comes with debt attached.

Those who seem to have lived richly, who have loved many times and left many traces——to be envied for their freedom is to envy their accumulated debts without seeing the burden. To be entangled with so many people is, in the end, suffering. Lovers can be let go; familial bonds are harder——they cut slowly, over years, grinding down the heart.

Life, at its core, is suffering. The joys of family, the warmth of marriage, the blessings of children——all of these are, in truth, stories of much pain and little peace. The more pain you endure, the more you call it love. There is no afterlife; there is only debt in this one. To see through family early is a kind of mercy.

Family is a transaction never agreed to: you receive life, and in return you owe gratitude, patience, silence in the face of anger and reproach. What is that if not suffering?

Why do monks and Daoists renounce the world? Because they have seen clearly that human bonds are ropes of pain, and the sooner they are cut, the lighter the load. A layperson need not renounce everything, but the same clarity of mind is worth having——not to become cold, but to stop being drained by others' suffering.

There was a news story some time ago: a 70-year-old man in Japan threw his 90-year-old paralyzed mother into the sea. He had cared for her for over 30 years——his whole later life bound to that bloodline. At the time I felt nothing. Later, after I had tasted what it meant to be worn down to the bone, I understood his exhaustion completely.

So forget about "many children bring blessings" or passing on property. For the poor, more children bring more suffering. Look at Lü Dongbin——in his Yellow Millet Dream, he had wives, office, prestige; then in a moment, the axe fell. He was tested ten times by Zhongli Quan and remained unmoved. He received the Way and wandered the world, free of the bitter sea.

This body is nothing but a bag of filth. If you stand in a latrine, you understand: what we call "beauty" is just temporary. Age comes, and the body becomes a prison. Some people live half a lifetime and never see this.

Live for no one. After the Jia family was ruined, Jia Xichun became a nun. When asked, she said, "What Jia family? What Zhen family?" And Jia Bao, after passing the civil service exam, knelt in the snow to his father, draped in red, then followed a monk and a Daoist into the white distance. If someone presses you to do what you do not want, you can simply walk away.

The Dream of Red Mansions teaches a hard lesson: those who cling to family are destroyed; those who cling to the world suffer. Lin Daiyu buried flowers and burned her drafts——her love ran so deep it wounded her body and mind. She embodied an uncompromising ideal, and her death marks the turning point of the Jia house. The daughters of the Grand View Garden died, fled, or took the tonsure. In the end, nothing remained.

Miaoyu's tragedy is the deepest: a woman too pure to use a cup touched by others, who ended up degraded to the dust. Fate broke her pride, then broke her dignity. She will remain in that living hell of struggle.

The author Cao Xueqin wrote of an era, a tragedy, to its limit. Every word deserves rereading. Some readers have seen in the novel the oppression of women, bound by economics, custom, and thought. Qingwen, when cast out, had even her undergarments confiscated——total economic dependence. Women were once bound by law to men. Now they can be scholars, doctors, independent——and if Cao Xueqin lived today, he would be glad.

He called his own work "a pile of nonsense." But generations of readers have taken it seriously. Writing this, I felt a strange mirroring: I saw Cao Xueqin, writing by lamplight at night; I saw others who read him on long journeys, turning the pages again and again.

Literature surpasses love and family. To have no children is not a loss; to have no partner is not an emptiness. If there is true love, cherish it; if not, live out your years alone. Those who lament at the end of life, regretting they never married, never knew what they truly wanted. For those who think for themselves, worldly attachments are optional——and for those who cling to them, the spectacle is almost comic.

This is not to say "do not marry." It is to say: there is something beyond that——thought, literature. And it is not worth debating with those who would not understand.

Some see through, and walk away. Others sink deeper.

The ending of the Dream:

Jia Zheng saw a figure from a distance. It was Jia Bao. Startled, he called out: "Is that you?" Bao remained silent, showing neither joy nor sorrow.

Then two figures appeared——a monk and a Daoist——and said: "Your mortal ties are finished. Come." They took him by the arms and walked away. Jia Zheng ran after them, slipping on the snow, but could not catch them. He heard the three singing:

> I dwell on the Blue Ridge Peak,  
> I wander through the Vast Void.  
> Who travels with me? Who follows?  
> Endless, boundless——back to the Wilderness.

Jia Zheng listened and ran, but when he turned the hill, they were gone——and before him lay only a wide white expanse, no one in sight.

> To speak of bitterness is absurd.  
> All dreams are the same——do not mock the waking.

There is more to say about the Dream of Red Mansions—much more. The realm of daughters is gone, but the poetry and songs remain.
