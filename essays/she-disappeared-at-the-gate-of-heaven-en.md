# She Disappeared at the Gate of Heaven

The sky was impossibly blue back then, with layers of creamy clouds. And yet? I remember how she looked, and now there is only darkness.

I am a dull person. It is the seventh day of the Third World War, and I think the world has proven that death is like a terminal that can erupt at any moment.

Occasionally I wonder why I survived. But there is no way to know now.

I walk through rain that is not water. On the ground, cities that once scraped the sky have become ruins. All the wealth accumulated over centuries vanished in an instant.

Tactical nuclear missiles leveled nearly every city. The death toll doubled exponentially. Blood rain falls from the sky—the horrifying weather caused by the nuclear incineration of a city of ten million. That rain is human blood.

Japan no longer exists. The United States no longer exists. Russia no longer exists. All countries are gone.

I look at the exposed steel rebar, the severed limbs scattered everywhere, the black smoke rising. And I suddenly realize: the laughter of yesterday will not return today.

I walk through the rubble. Nuclear dust covers the entire world. The heads of state may still be alive in their bunkers, but it no longer matters. No one believes in governments anymore.

The disaster began when two countries suddenly announced they would accelerate the development of some superweapon. An arms race, obvious enough.

So be it—let us destroy each other. If we can't have it, no one can.

And so the war began. Tens of thousands of super-nuclear missiles, at a single command, all launched at one another.

I heard the roar of countless stealth drones overhead, the howl of super-nuclear warheads from afar, and the deafening blasts tearing through the air.

The war had begun!

First, the dense cities of Japan—Tokyo, Osaka—vaporized in an instant. Tens of millions of people evaporated. Then AI-operated drones swept across the land like scythes, harvesting human lives. Countless swarm drones carrying miniaturized nuclear warheads continued to annihilate all living forces.

Then all two hundred countries suffered the same fate simultaneously…

A single command could destroy the world. One minute to kill twenty million people. Ten minutes to melt an entire city.

Nuclear submarines, nuclear missiles, nuclear aircraft, nuclear tanks, nuclear aircraft carriers—almost every weapon was loaded with enough nuclear warheads to destroy a city outright.

Humanity finally realized: death is like a firework show. After the explosion, nothing is left. And so, in absolute madness, let the nukes annihilate everything.

From the skull of a primitive man, an iron arrow had pierced through and remained lodged there. Thousands of years later, humans discovered the arrowhead and realized with excitement: war was passed down to us by our ancestors.

Slaughter. Killing. Madness. Destruction. Death.

A deeply misanthropic madman stood on a hundred-meter-high building. Seeing the blazing fireball erupt in the distance, he shouted: "That is a sun descending upon the earth!" He laughed maniacally, then leaped.

A weary middle-aged man, rushing to work, sat in his car. He saw that distant sun and understood. His expression shifted from fear to a smile. Then he let go of the steering wheel and watched quietly. The fireball grew larger.

A billionaire sat in his lavish corner office. He felt the terrifying heat approaching. Looking up, he saw a nuclear bomb exploding, its center temperature reaching tens of thousands of degrees. His eyes were filled with blinding white light. He clenched his fist in defiance—then relaxed it, leaned back into his leather chair, and let go.

"Everyone in the world is going to die!"

"This money is worthless!"

"Oh God, why are you punishing us like this?"

"I want to go home!"

Before the nuclear blast hit, everyone understood: everything they had clung to was meaningless. Because the firework has exploded, and there is nothing after. All the wealth, status, power, treasures, gold, diamonds, banknotes—all of it meaningless, because the whole world is about to explode.

"Explode! Explode! All you bastards, die with me! All the bastards who ever bullied me, hooray!" A depressed, long-haired student, a victim of prolonged bullying, stood on the school rooftop, raising his arms in triumph.

His bullies collapsed to the ground. Regret and fear filled their eyes. Before them, this once-dim figure stood tall, cheering in the face of nuclear annihilation. Their usual bravado had vanished.

"You ask where I am?"

"Good question."

I am also a student at this school. I never bullied anyone. I keep to myself, have few friends, and get pushed around for being weak—but I don't care. I have grown used to this pain. I didn't go to the rooftop to watch. I was reading a book in the classroom.

That book was the Book of Revelation. My heart trembled uncontrollably—because the myths written in it had become reality. The Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse had arrived. The final judgment was upon all. Sinners would be cast into the abyss forever.

My hair was even longer than the bullied student's. Everyone was running in chaos and panic. I sat still, watching coldly, unmoved. The school was in utter disarray—I didn't care.

I read the book, my heart full. The explosions outside were the perfect accompaniment. The clatter of desks, the school sirens, the government broadcasts—all converged into my favorite symphony: Beethoven's *Fate*. But this *Fate* was far more intense, even frantic—interwoven with salvation, judgment, finality, and rest. It was beautiful. I relished it. I could now see the Son of God, nailed to the cross by human violence, descending upon the world with His storm of authority.

I sat in the back row, looking out the window—my reflection was there, and beyond it rose the cities, the countless screams, and that impossibly bright, impossibly fierce, impossibly terrifying fireball, casting an orange-red glow. And my reflection sat right in the center of that fireball.

"I like you."

Suddenly a girl ran up to me and confessed. Her hair was tied in a small ponytail, she was very cute, with a small curved mouth. For a moment I didn't know how to answer. Then I said gently: "Then sit beside me."

She actually moved her desk and chair and sat down beside me.

Girl: "I want to lean on you."

Me: "Go ahead."

Girl: "I want to kiss you."

Me: "Go ahead."

But I felt nothing—no sensation at all. Not even the kiss. I turned in surprise and looked at her. She smiled mysteriously, then vanished.

I laughed too. "I see—she is my beloved."

I leaned my cheek lazily on my hand, my eyes melancholy, watching the window. Countless nuclear warheads were approaching from all directions, countless drones descending, countless wars breaking out, nuclear missiles falling like rain. In my eyes, the sky was gradually plunging into silent darkness.

"I was so foolish to love her. So foolish. She didn't love me."

I blinked. Suddenly, the girl reappeared before me. She smiled and took my hand. This time I felt it. Without hesitation, I took her hand and ran out of the classroom.

Jehovah stood at the horizon, bathed in the entire starry sky, clothed in white. He met my gaze, and my heart surged. He said nothing. Where His gaze fell, scorched earth turned to meadow.

The barren world was reborn, teeming with life. Countless good souls entered the eternal paradise. The fountain of heaven flowed into the earth, and Eden descended.

I turned around. She was gone.
