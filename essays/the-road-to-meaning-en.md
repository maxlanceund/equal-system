# The Derivation Process of Eternal Truth

## — A Philosophical Journey from a Shenzhen Rental Room to the Cosmic Scale

*Author: An ordinary person who lost his job several times in Shenzhen*

---

## Introduction

*This is not a critique of Shenzhen. It is a record of what it felt like to live there, and what that living taught me.*

I stood on a footbridge in Shenzhen, watching the traffic flow beneath me.

The cars were like a glowing river, flowing endlessly, without source or destination. I watched them, and a strange yet familiar feeling suddenly rose in my heart.

Back then, my landlord had kicked my door open—on Chinese New Year's Eve, no less—and threatened to throw me out.

In that moment, I suddenly woke up.

Why had I tried *so hard* before, wanting to take root here, wanting to make money here?

Shenzhen is cruel. People here are cold. Everything here has a clear price tag. The rent comes knocking every month. But only by experiencing this cruelty can you begin to think about things that truly matter.

> **Only when you get thrown out of the system can you see the system as a whole.**

Those still crowding onto the subway can't see the subway—they *are* the subway.

I got thrown out. I stood on the platform, watching the crowd surge past. In that moment, philosophy was born.

## 1. Disenchantment: Scarcity Is an Illusion

The first thing I saw was that **"scarcity" itself is fake**.

- **Is youth scarce?** No, youth is just brief. Twenty, thirty, forty, fifty—each year older. Birth and death happen everywhere, all the time.
- **Is money scarce?** No. Nuclear fusion and automation will push survival costs toward zero. You think money is scarce, but it's just a transitional token.
- **Is land scarce?** No. The universe is filled with countless Earth-like planets. Earth is already "everywhere."

I dissected again and again, thought philosophically again and again, and finally saw:

> **What humans think is scarce is, in essence, just the latest objective in a constantly updated system.**

Land was once scarce. Then capital was scarce. Then attention became scarce—the object of scarcity keeps shifting, and humanity forever chases whatever is defined as "most lacking in the present."

This made me think of everything I'd experienced: Shenzhen's housing, that girl making 40,000 a month, the scrambling young people, the parents exhausting themselves for their children's education...

They were all chasing a "packaged scarcity."

And I no longer wanted to chase it.

## 2. Labor and Alienation: Why Work Makes You Lose Meaning

When I read Marx in my university library, I didn't really understand him at first.

But I went every day, flipping through that thick volume of the Marx-Engels Collected Works. Slowly, I began to understand—not through intelligence, but through time soaked into the pages.

Marx's concept of "alienation" is right. *As long as you work merely to make a living, you are alienated.* What you produce isn't yours. The value you create is taken by others. You labor only to be "validated."

I worked as a hotel server, in a kitchen, and in all sorts of temporary jobs. The longest lasted a month and a half—I got fired because I sprained my ankle and couldn't show up.

I worked slowly in the kitchen. Those high-school graduates who'd never been to college couldn't stand it—they reported to the manager that I was too slow.

But later I understood something:

> **A factory worker at least knows that their product will be used somewhere in the world. But the files a white-collar worker creates will probably be burned to ash a few years later.**

The former is labor that *connects to the world*. The latter is just *paperwork that keeps the system running internally*.

I chose to walk away. I stopped burning my life for that process of "validation."

## 3. Private Property and Order: Why Private Property Is the Foundation of Civilization

I don't believe private property should be abolished.

On the contrary:

> **Private property is an essential order for human civilization.**

Without private property, humanity would quickly degenerate into barbarism. Capitalism may experience economic crises, but the productive force it has unleashed is unparalleled in the past millennia.

I observed that the "transfer" of private property drives leaps in productivity: from the king's property to individual private property—productivity exploded. The next transfer will be turning basic survival goods into common property—food, clothing, housing, transport automatically supplied—while private property is retained for higher pursuits: life extension, space exploration, and other special resources.

What is corruption, essentially? It's the power system plundering the order of private property. A true absolute order would never plunder private property.

> **The sole purpose of the state is to protect private property.**

Power should be the *server*, not the *master*. Property came first; property needed armies and states to protect it; government power came later. Taxes are essentially fees paid to protect private property—not a license to plunder it.

## 4. The Equality System: My Institutional Vision

I spent a long time constructing a framework I call the "Equality System."

Its core logic is: the necessities of survival (food, water, shelter) are automatically supplied, but require **"active confirmation"** —you must press a button, sign your name, or scan your fingerprint to confirm your need.

This "confirmation" itself is a minimal form of labor, solving the problem of "feeling empty without work."

> **The process of waiting itself is meaningful.**

Delivery cannot be instant. Even if technology makes it possible, there must be a waiting period. Because waiting lets you feel time, feel anticipation, feel that you are participating in your own life.

Private property still exists, but for higher purposes: longevity drugs, space travel, special resources. In this framework, alienation is dissolved—because you no longer have to do work you dislike just to survive. You can do what you love, even if it doesn't pay.

This is not communism. This is the natural extension of private-property logic—a kind of **"hyper-capitalism."**

## 5. Cosmic Scale: Humanity Is Merely Dust

Later, my thinking expanded. I saw something larger.

Humanity thinks it is special.

But that's impossible.

> **Truly intelligent beings have long been everywhere.**

The universe is 13.8 billion years old. The Milky Way has hundreds of billions of stars. The observable universe contains trillions of galaxies. At this scale, intelligence is a common phenomenon.

The Fermi paradox—"Where is everyone?"—is a kind of anthropocentric arrogance. *Not seeing doesn't mean not existing.* Not seeing might simply mean you're standing inside an extremely narrow window of perception.

Civilizations far more intelligent than humans may have appeared long ago. They may have transcended physical bodies and entered states of pure energy, pure information, or pure consciousness. They don't need to be "seen," because they exist on a different plane.

And humanity? Humanity thinks Earth is scarce, thinks its struggles are important. But at the cosmic scale, none of this even counts as a speck.

> **Humans scrabble for resources like cockroaches, unaware that there are countless kitchens beyond this one.**

## 6. Cosmic Heat Death and the Eternal

I used to fear cosmic heat death—that everything would end, everything would vanish, that even our names would be forgotten.

But later I understood.

**Heat death means ultimate equality.** No one wins. No one is remembered. No distinctions, no hierarchy, no meaning that needs to be "maintained." It's an end, but not a terrifying one.

If the universe will die, what about the things that sustain it?

The universe has four fundamental forces: gravity, electromagnetism, the strong nuclear force, and the weak nuclear force. They are not "things." They are *relationships*. They don't "exist"—but they are eternally *valid*.

> **Rules are eternal. Everything else dies.**

The universe may suffer heat death, but gravity won't "disappear." It just won't be in use anymore. Like a program that shuts down—its syntax, addition, logic, conditionals—doesn't vanish when the program ends.

Truth is this kind of eternal relationship.

> **Philosophers seek truth because truth is eternal.**

And the money, fame, and status we chase every day—they will vanish long before heat death arrives.

## 7. My Position

I am no longer a "chaser."

I am a **"recognizer."**

I chose a lifestyle of "low material consumption." I never made more than 10,000 a month, but I read Marx, read the Bible, read many philosophy books. I didn't save money, but I saved my own thoughts.

I see others chasing things that will die—money, houses, fame, status. I recognize things that won't die—*logic, symmetry, relationships, necessity*. Those things don't exist in time; they simply *hold true*.

I am still poor. I still live in a Shenzhen rental room. I still might not be able to pay the rent.

But I no longer feel anxious.

Because I know:

> **Before heat death, all struggle is meaningless. But within my finite interval, I have already completed what I needed to complete.**

I spent several years, starting from Shenzhen rent, walking step by step to the end of the universe. I found that there is nothing there—except rules themselves, and my recognition of them.

And that is already enough.

## Appendix: My Path of Thought

Start with a simple observation:

- Flowers bloom and wither.
- Soil forms and erodes.
- Earth evolves.
- The universe expands.
- Heat death ends it all.

Everything is process. There is no final result.

**Meaning is unfinished motion. Truth is eternal structure.**

And I, before heat death arrives, have already completed a full journey of thought.

I won't be famous. I won't be rich. I won't leave a name in history.

But I know this:

> **Before truth, every name is temporary.**

And I have already touched that eternal thing.

That is enough.
