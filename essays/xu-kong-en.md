# Xǔ Kōng

Violence began in winter.

A child in oversized, ragged clothes was being beaten with a switch by the one called mother. His tears had flooded, his pain had run dry. No one would save him.

Onlookers stood around, watching coldly, savoring the violence that would not touch them. The child sobbed in agony, but the woman did not stop. The switch drew blood, and he could only curl up, clutching his head.

"Haha, serves him right," an old man said, pointing and fanning the flames.

The mother grew even more furious, striking harder, grabbing the child's clothes and dragging him as she beat him. The pain tore through his senses until he went numb, able only to be dragged along. More people gathered, craning their necks like ducks, following the spectacle.

"Stop, please stop," he cried out, the only plea he could make.

No one answered.

He knew the pain would not end there, and there was nothing he could do about it.

He was beaten half to death that day. From then on, his mind would always remember that powerlessness.

When he came of age, he left that house of pain. The wind was quiet, the sky blue, his steps light.

That night, he found a remote mountain ravine and settled into a warm cave. There was no electricity, but he had paper and pen, and he could write. He began to ponder: "What is the meaning of life?" But the more he thought, the more he felt that life's greatest meaning was meaninglessness. As a child, he had suffered violence and pain without reason. He came to understand that often, people are the source of suffering. He did not want to forgive anyone. Yet in reality, he treated everyone with excessive politeness. If he were a good man, his life would be bitter; if he were a bad one, he could not fully commit. He became a complicated, extreme person. He was misanthropic, weary of everything—this was the only thing he could do. He had longed for freedom, yet realized he had never been free. He had yearned for distant places, yet understood they too were cages. He had once hoped for wealth, a wife, children, but in the end, he only saw the world full of quarrels and hostility. He came to understand that pain had shaped him, filling a heart that had once known neither suffering nor sorrow, as if to prove that he alone could understand pain. His heart grew heavier with each day of that ascetic life. He did not know what he was, and no answer could console him.

Days in the mountains passed quickly. Sometimes he heard birdsong, sometimes he saw deer, occasionally he glimpsed the Milky Way. On rainy days, he wrote in peace. Nothing could be more monotonous than this life, and he lived it day after day, year after year. He drew water from the mountain, cooked from the mountain, living a simple, quiet, and free life. He almost forgot the pain of the past, and he needed that feeling. When the moonlight fell on his face, he felt utterly at ease. This was all he wanted. It was little, yet it was more than enough.

Later, no one knew how many years had passed. She never saw him again, and he never thought of her. Because pain, when remembered, is unbearable. He had become nothing but sorrow. He wrote a poem about his mother, trying to cover the deepest wound with the memory of her kindness. When he finished, he burned it, but the ashes scattered everywhere. He thought he had let go, yet still the nightmares came.

He began to study medicine, reading Daoist and Buddhist scriptures. He often visited mountain temples and Daoist retreats, knowing that some pain could only be entrusted to the divine, and only a mind without attachment could truly let go.

He went to a temple.

He asked the monk: "What is the meaning of life?"

The monk said: "Are you insane?"

He asked: "What happens to this body after death?"

The monk said: "You're an idiot."

He asked: "Why do I always feel pain?"

The monk said: "I don't know."

He left.

In the blink of an eye, he was in his thirties, his hair long, his beard unkempt.

One day, a lady entered the mountains, sorrowful beyond measure, as if she meant to hang herself beneath a tree.

He asked: "Why do you seek death?"

She said: "I do not have much time left. I want to leave early, so as not to burden anyone."

He laughed suddenly: "Hah, completely useless. You won't find liberation that way."

The lady looked at him and ceased her attempt at suicide. She asked: "Why? This body is in agony. Why can I not find release?"

He answered: "This is nothing but escape, like my hiding here in the mountains—it is useless. Change your view, and liberation will come of itself."

She ran to him, her bright eyes wide open, studying him. His face was like frost; his eyes like sunken wood. She knelt and said: "Please, teach me."

He helped her up and said: "Go gather two bundles of firewood for me. Tonight, I will teach you the way to liberation."

She went into the mountains and gathered the wood with difficulty. By nightfall, she had brought back two bundles, exhausted. She dragged them to the cave, but her eyes remained bright and eager. He warmed a basin of water for her, gave her his towel to wipe away sweat, and offered her water. She drank it all in large gulps.

Her eyes full of hope, she asked: "Now, will you tell me the way to liberation?"

He served her a bowl of rice and said: "Finish this first."

She ate ravenously, along with the vegetables in the pot. He watched her and knew that illness was consuming her; her time was indeed short. The truth was that the "way to liberation" was a lie. He had sought it all his life and still did not know it—how could he teach her?

She finished her meal and sat upright, waiting for him to speak.

He began to invent the teaching: "You suffer because you do not yet understand the root of your pain."

She nodded thoughtfully.

He continued: "Your time is short—not because fate is unjust, not because this is a curse. It is the natural way of this impermanent world. You cannot escape it, but you do not have to add to your own suffering. There is no 'you' that suffers; the one that cries out is not you."

She listened, her breath steady.

"From now on, there is no 'you.' Whatever name you bear, whoever's wife, mother, or daughter—none of that is you. The one suffering is not you. Let it go. I am giving you a new name. It has no meaning. You do not even need to remember it."

He touched her forehead and said: "Xǔ Kōng. From now on, you are Xǔ Kōng. It means nothing at all. You only need to know that you are Xǔ Kōng—you are no one. All worldly ties have nothing to do with you. Let them all go."

Now tears began to flow, releasing the grief and bitterness she had long suppressed. She sobbed loudly. She had once been a woman of rare beauty, had borne children for a wealthy merchant, had struggled as a mother, believed in happiness, lived in comfort. Yet when illness struck overnight, all her finery and ease became meaningless. Every thing she had clung to became a torment in her pain. Now, before her stood a penniless, bearded, gaunt man living in the wilderness, with no desire for her body—only telling her to let go, to take a new name, to leave everything behind. At last, she felt she had found something she could truly rely on—not material support, but the reassurance of a peaceful departure.

Long after, her tears dried.

He had her tie her long hair into a bundle with a hemp cord, then cut off half and throw it into the fire. She did as he said. As the hair crackled in the flames, she said goodbye to the self that had once clung to all worldly desires. She became Xǔ Kōng.

After that, he took her to gather herbs, which eased her pain. She grew accustomed to life in the mountains, chanting and meditating. She was no longer a lady wailing in suffering, but a true practitioner. Soon her level surpassed his; she understood scriptures and doctrines quickly, and over time, he found himself learning from her. As her name foretold, she had realized emptiness.

One night, as they were about to sleep, Xǔ Kōng asked him a question that caught him off guard.

Lying sideways under her blanket, she looked at him and smiled: "Did you ever like me?"

He sat on his cotton mat, his expression unchanged: "Why do you ask?"

She smiled again, her cheek resting on her hand: "You liked me—I am sure of it."

He looked into her eyes. They were calm and broad as the sea. At that moment, he understood: she had surpassed him utterly. She had attained wisdom, free from attachment and aversion, accepting everything with equanimity. Facing such a person, there was no room for deception. After a long silence, he said at last: "Yes. I liked you."

She showed no emotion, simply nodded, pulled the blanket up, and closed her eyes to sleep. He said nothing more, extinguishing the fire. All was dark, save for the sound of insects.

But as the night deepened, he was tormented. It felt as though ants were gnawing at his whole body. His mind filled with images of desire. He grew afraid. When moonlight poured into the cave, he quickly put on his clothes and went outside.

He gasped for air. Now, he had become trapped. Xǔ Kōng had become an obstacle. He wanted to… that terrible thought. "She has already seen through me. I cannot let go of her. She fills my heart. I am no true practitioner. I must leave. She no longer needs me. I should go." He looked at the bright moon, uncertain where to settle his heart.

He took out paper and pen and wrote:

"Xǔ Kōng, I must leave. No reason."

He planned to leave the note on the hearth and depart. But when he returned, he found her awake. Her eyes met his. He turned away instinctively, unable to face her. But she was unflinching; she smiled: "Look at me. Look at my body. You like it, don't you?"

He turned and fled, unable to stay, knowing he was far beneath her. She chased after him, her voice mournful: "Stay. Don't leave me."

He ran into the forest, gritting his teeth. Rain began to fall, turning the ground to mud. He ran until he had no strength left, collapsing under a tree. He struck his own face twice, then wept. Why could he not suppress the wretched desire? His clothes soaked, his breathing grew heavy, his head spun, and he fell unconscious beneath the tree.

In his dream, Xǔ Kōng comforted him, feeding him warm congee.

When he woke, he realized it was not a dream. She was beside him, feeding him congee. Seeing her, he exhaled. At last, he understood his problem. What was leaving but another form of escape?

Her beautiful eyes watched him. Her delicate hand brought the spoon to his mouth. "I think you understand," she said.

He said nothing, lowering his gaze. "Yes. I was just running away. Running from desire?"

He sighed deeply: "I am far beneath you now, Xǔ Kōng."

She nodded: "Before, you; now, me."

She helped him sit up and asked: "Tell me, what did it feel like?"

"Racing heart. My mind full of you… wanting… wanting you…" He could not finish, swallowing the congee.

Her voice came, clear and hollow: "It will pass. Do not dwell on it. Slowly become calm. That is not you."

Gradually, his racing heart subsided, his heat fading. Yes, that was not him—that was his body.

She placed her hand on his chest, feeling the warmth inside, and asked: "Now what do you feel?"

"It's rising again," he said, struggling to resist.

She kept her hand there for an hour, until the pulse fully calmed.

She asked: "Where is that racing heart now?"

He answered: "Gone."

She nodded: "Good. Those things are not you. They merely rise briefly and pass quickly."

With her repeated help, he gradually cooled, the thoughts and desires fading, leaving his body clear and light.

They lived together in that cave for eight full years. Their faces barely aged; those years passed like the snap of a finger.

In the early spring of the ninth year, Xǔ Kōng said she wished to bathe and change into her finest clothes. She let down her long hair, which she had grown for eight years, and soaked in a warm pool, naked before him. She immersed herself, enjoying the warmth. Fragrance filled the air as she ran a herbal paste through her hair.

He knew this was the day of farewell. He looked at her; she was still smiling, as she had eight years before.

"This is life. These eight years were a miracle. Tonight, she will enter nirvana, her soul returning to the heavens. We will be parted forever. It is the destined end, and the most dignified one. Xǔ Kōng now is far calmer than I ever was. I am but her witness."

He smiled, shaking his head, remembering all that had passed. Resolved, he walked to the edge of the pool, drew near her, and kissed her forehead and her lips. They laughed together—a farewell ritual.

After two hours of bathing, he dried her body, dressed her in the flowing robes, and pinned up her hair with a jade clasp. She looked new, radiant, pure. She smiled all the while, happy.

He took her hand and walked with her around the cave. The weather was fine that day, with a brilliant sunset and a cool western wind. Her hair flew in the breeze; she glanced sideways at him, as if reading something in his face, then smiled and turned her gaze toward the sun. The great red orb slowly sank beneath the horizon, trailing brilliant clouds, while the pale moon rose.

He felt her warmth fading. She rested her head on his shoulder. At last, she closed her eyes, her breathing gently slowing. He sat with her in silence, and she murmured her last words: "It feels so good… with you here… so light."

As night fully descended, Xǔ Kōng's breath ceased. She had returned to the eternal quiet. He touched her now-cold hand; she had passed peacefully.

He did not weep, felt no emotion. He was over forty.

Three days later, he cremated her. The fire burned through the night. Part of her ashes he scattered around the cave; part he kept with him.

He looked back one last time. The bamboo grove before the cave was lush and swaying in the spring wind. He shouldered his pack and set out on a distant journey.

"Xǔ Kōng"—the emptiness you gave yourself.
