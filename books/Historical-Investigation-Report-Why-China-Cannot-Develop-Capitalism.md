> **Abstract**  
> This report conducts a structural investigation into whether China can develop capitalism, based on a causal chain derived from modern history. The conclusion is that China cannot take the capitalist path — not as an economic efficiency issue, but as a judgment formed by historical experience. All data cited in this report are drawn from publicly available historical documents, academic research, and official statistical sources. No unverified materials are used.

## 1. Investigation Background and Purpose

China was repeatedly impacted by external forces in modern times. From the Opium War in 1840 to the end of the War of Resistance in 1945, China experienced over a century of upheaval and casualties. During this period, China — a predominantly agricultural nation — faced a world that had already completed industrialization.

This report attempts to answer one question: Can China develop capitalism? This is not an economic policy question, but a question of historical experience. This report examines this proposition by tracing a causal chain — capitalist expansion → opening of Japan → Japanese industrialization → militarism → invasion of China → Chinese casualties → national awakening — to assess whether it holds.


## 2. Investigation Methods and Scope

This report employs a combination of historical document analysis and causal chain deduction. Data sources include the ten-year research findings of the Party History Research Center of the CPC Central Committee, the original text of the Treaty of Shimonoseki, Tao Xingzhi's Diaries, the Japan Statistical Yearbook, East Asian economic history research literature, People's Daily Military Channel, Xinhua History Channel, and other publicly available sources. The time scope covers 1853 (the arrival of the Black Ships) to 1945 (the end of the War of Resistance).


## 3. Investigation Findings

### 3.1 How Capitalist Expansion Entered East Asia

In 1853, the U.S. Black Ships sailed into Edo Bay, demanding that Japan open its borders with the force of 4 steam warships and 63 cannons. In 1854, the Treaty of Kanagawa was signed, and Japan was forced to end its long period of isolation.

> Japan did not "choose" industrialization voluntarily. It was after seeing the gunboats of external powers that it rapidly turned toward Westernization to avoid colonization.

The Meiji Restoration began in 1868, and Japan comprehensively adopted Western technology, military systems, education, and political institutions. Between 1866 and 1873, Japan's annual industrial growth rate reached 32.2% — far exceeding that of contemporary Western nations (UK 3.3%, USA 5%, Germany 3.8%).

### 3.2 How Japan Industrialized and Turned Toward Militarism

After completing its industrialization, Japan quickly turned to external expansion. In the First Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895, Japan defeated the Qing Empire and obtained an indemnity of 230 million taels of silver through the Treaty of Shimonoseki — equivalent to approximately three times the Qing government's annual revenue, and approximately 6.4 times Japan's own annual fiscal revenue at the time. This massive indemnity was used by Japan to expand its military and advance industrialization.

Japan continued its expansion: defeating Russia in 1905, annexing Korea in 1910, occupying Manchuria in 1931, and launching a full-scale invasion of China in 1937.

> Industrialization itself does not inevitably lead to aggression. But when a nation simultaneously adopts Social Darwinism and the international logic of "might makes right," industrial capacity is transformed into a tool for military expansion.

### 3.3 The Impact and Casualties Suffered by China as an Agricultural Nation

In modern times, China was a large agricultural nation that had not yet completed industrialization. Facing an already industrialized Japan, China was at a disadvantage in both equipment and institutions.

During the War of Resistance, Chinese military and civilian casualties exceeded 35 million, including over 20 million deaths and over 10 million wounded; military casualties exceeded 3.8 million. Japanese forces killed or captured exceeded 1.5 million. Direct economic losses amounted to approximately USD 100 billion (1937 value), and indirect economic losses to approximately USD 500 billion.

In addition, modern China's total foreign indemnities amounted to approximately 1.045 billion taels of silver (approximately 1.375 billion silver yuan), of which the Boxer Protocol indemnity was 450 million taels of silver, with principal and interest totaling 980 million taels.

> These figures are not abstract statistics. They represent specific families, communities, and ruptures of intergenerational memory. China is one of the nations that paid the heaviest price in the history of capitalist expansion.

### 3.4 How Suffering Gave Rise to National Subjective Awakening

> Historical lessons are sometimes not "learned" but "endured."

After experiencing a series of impacts, China gradually arrived at the following conclusions:

- Learning from the West could not truly protect itself
- Dependence could not secure equal status
- It had to walk a path different from the logic of capitalist expansion

> The path China walks today was not derived from the capitalist system — it grew out of the struggle against it.


## 4. Analysis and Discussion

### 4.1 Comparative Perspective: Why Did Other Countries Not Take the Same Path?

Capitalist expansion impacted many countries, but not all of them turned to militarism. Thailand was not colonized and maintained flexible diplomacy; Ethiopia was invaded but not occupied, and did not respond with militarism. This indicates that capitalism provided the tools of industrialization, but how those tools were used was determined by the receiving nation itself.

> The harm China suffered came not only from an industrialized neighbor, but from a neighbor that chose the logic of "might makes right."

### 4.2 A Hidden Premise: "Being Opened" for Large Countries vs. "Being Annexed" for Small Countries

In the historical chain of capitalist expansion, a premise is seldom mentioned: the size of the impacted country determines how it bears the cost. Small countries can be annexed and reduced to colonies; large countries, too large to be easily annexed, endure repeated shocks and repeated openings.

> China was large enough that it experienced not a one-time annexation, but repeated shocks and openings over more than a century. This became one of the deep conditions for China's eventual choice of "another path."

### 4.3 An Open Question: What If China Had Already Been Industrialized?

If China had already completed industrialization in the 19th century — if it had already possessed a complete industrial system and organizational capacity before being impacted — would it have suffered the same fate? This question has no answer, because it did not happen.

> But it suggests this: China's subsequent industrialization occurred under the shadow of shocks and openings, and the way it was accomplished — without colonial accumulation or the logic of might makes right — is itself a choice worth noting.

### 4.4 The Cost Never Considered: The Asymmetric Shock of Industrialization

When Western powers used gunboats to open China's doors, they considered markets, resources, and trade routes. They never considered what an unindustrialized country would endure — how its social structure would be torn apart, how many generations would live through continuous upheaval.

> An industrial nation can endure war because it has an industrial base to rebuild. When an agricultural nation is drawn into an industrial war, its support system is not industry — it is land, families, and human labor. When these are destroyed, recovery is not measured in years — it is measured in generations.

### 4.5 A Question Never Asked: Against Whom Would Japan Turn Its Power?

The West opened Japan and exported to it technology, military systems, and educational frameworks. They helped Japan complete its industrialization, but throughout the process, they never seriously asked: after this country completes its industrialization, against whom will it turn its industrial capacity?

After the First Sino-Japanese War, Japan had already extracted a massive indemnity from China — far exceeding its annual fiscal revenue at the time. This proved one thing: using force to extract resources from China was more efficient than any trade. This "proof" itself was one of the reasons Japan later chose China as its target.

> The West's role in this causal chain was not that of an uninvolved observer, but a participant that created conditions without bearing the consequences. When it exported industrialization to Japan, it never considered what direction it would take. It never asked itself: what kind of country are we creating? This "not considering" is itself a form of irresponsibility.

### 4.6 Western Inaction: Data and Historical Facts

Western powers between 1931 and 1941 were not "unaware" of what Japan was doing — they chose not to intervene, not to sanction, not to cut off supplies. This "inaction" itself is a structural form of complicity.

> On September 17, 1931 — the day before the Mukden Incident — the U.S. Secretary of State and the Japanese Ambassador to the United States reached a secret understanding: the United States would not interfere with Japan's actions in Manchuria, and Japan would confine its actions to north of Jinzhou. Only when Japan took Jinzhou and advanced south did the U.S. issue a "Non-Recognition" note, but no actual action was taken.

On September 21, 1931, China appealed to the League of Nations. The League dispatched the Lytton Commission, which confirmed that the Mukden Incident was an act of Japanese aggression and refused to recognize Manchukuo. However, after adopting the report in February 1933 with 42 votes in favor and Japan's 1 vote against, the League took no substantive sanctions against Japan. Japan immediately announced its withdrawal.

> The League's ineffectual response sent a signal to all nations tempted to expand through aggression: aggression can be tolerated.

Between 1937 and 1940, total U.S. exports to Japan reached USD 986.7 million, of which military materials accounted for USD 703.9 million. According to Tao Xingzhi's Diaries, the U.S. share of Japan's strategic material imports in 1937 was 54.4%. In 1938, U.S. scrap iron exports to Japan increased 9-fold compared to 1937. Over 80% of Japan's oil and over 90% of its aviation gasoline depended on U.S. supply.

> Before 1941, the United States continuously exported strategic materials to Japan, "in effect aiding Japan's invasion of China."


## 5. Conclusion

### 5.1 Observer's Note

> Before writing this report, the author spent some time in a marginal position within a small organization. He saw power concentrated in the hands of a few, with most people quietly existing on a roster — neither participating in discussions nor being assigned tasks. He saw that even those who did not participate were counted in the year-end summary — because the system needed to maintain the completeness of its roster.
>
> This experience helped the author understand a simple structure: in any system, what truly determines its direction is not those who propose solutions, nor those who passively accept them — but those who choose to observe from the margins and remain clear-eyed.

### 5.2 Final Conclusion

> China cannot develop capitalism. This is not an economic efficiency issue — it is a judgment formed by historical experience.

| Dimension | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| External variable | Capitalist expansion did exist as an external variable in modern Chinese history, and produced far-reaching consequences |
| Internal condition | As an unindustrialized agricultural nation, China endured the cost of repeated shocks and repeated openings |
| Historical boundary | A nation harmed to this extent by external expansionary logic has no reason to voluntarily choose to establish its own internal operating mechanisms on the same logic |

> This is not a decision that can be changed through debate. It is a structural judgment based on historical experience.
