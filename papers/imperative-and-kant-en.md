# The Command Body and Kant: Toward the Empirical Path to Autonomy

> **Core Thesis**: Kant formulated the categorical imperative—that human beings should use their own reason to legislate for themselves, thereby achieving autonomy. He pointed the direction and asserted that "ought implies can." But he did not elaborate on why, if "can" is always possible, autonomy remains so difficult to realize in practice. This paper argues that autonomy encounters a "structural friction" in the empirical world—the Command Body. The Command Body compresses the time and space available for rational scrutiny by controlling survival resources, thereby making the realization of "can" more arduous than Kant anticipated. Yet the Command Body does not negate the possibility of autonomy; it merely marks the gradient of the path. Recognizing this gradient is itself the first movement of reason, and the first step toward autonomy.


## Opening

Have you ever seen a dog being trained?

The dog is not persuaded. It is shaped into obedience through food, punishment, and repetition.

Kant used "command" as a core concept of moral philosophy. He distinguished hypothetical imperatives from categorical imperatives, and argued that human beings should use their own reason to legislate for themselves.

He pointed the direction.

But what if, before reason could speak, a "structure" had already taken its place by controlling survival resources? What resistance would this "ought" encounter in the empirical world?

Following the direction he pointed, I saw something—the Command Body.


## I. Kant's Categorical Imperative and "Ought Implies Can"

Kant distinguished two types of imperatives.

**Hypothetical imperatives** are conditional: "If you want to achieve X, you must do Y." They depend on your personal ends.

**Categorical imperatives** are unconditional: "You must do A." They depend on no external condition, only on reason itself.

For Kant, genuine morality must be based on categorical imperatives. You follow not an external authority, but a command given by your own reason. Kant called this state **autonomy**—giving law to yourself. Its opposite is **heteronomy**—accepting legislation from an external authority.

Kant's crucial insight did not stop there. He made a further, essential claim:

> **"Ought" implies "can" (Sollen impliziert Können).**

This means: if you "ought" to do something, it follows that you "can" do it. Moral responsibility does not depend on the improvement of external conditions. You do not need to wait for the world to become better, for oppression to vanish, or for survival to be secured before you fulfill your moral duty.

Kant's conclusion was: human beings should use their own reason to legislate for themselves. And since "ought" implies "can," you are already "able" to be autonomous, right now.

> Kant's command comes from reason.
> The Command Body comes from dog-training.


## II. Where Kant Stopped

Kant said: human beings should use their own reason to legislate for themselves. And "ought" implies "can."

This was his conclusion. He stopped there.

What he did not ask was: if "can" is always possible—if, regardless of external conditions, we are always "able" to be autonomous—then why is autonomy so rare in practice?

Kant might have answered: because people are not rational enough, or because they are dominated by desires.

But if you look closely, you will see that most people do not fail to follow the categorical imperative because they are "not rational enough." They fail because they do not have the **time** to pause and engage in rational scrutiny. They are pushed forward by a "structure" that controls survival resources, and must react within extremely short timeframes—otherwise they lose their means of subsistence.

Kant described the "ought." He demonstrated the "can." But he did not discuss in detail: **Is the difficulty of executing "can" in the empirical world constant?** If an external structure compresses the time and space for rational scrutiny, then while "can" remains possible, it may become extremely difficult.

This is not to say Kant was wrong. At the transcendental level, Kant is entirely correct. But when the transcendental "can" enters the empirical world, it encounters friction.

This gap is the unfinished ground Kant left behind.


## III. The Command Body

Kant's "command" comes from reason itself—unconditional, autonomous.

But there is another "command." It comes from an external "structure." This structure maintains obedience by controlling survival resources: food, shelter, healthcare, social recognition—all controlled by it. Those who obey receive resources; those who do not, lose them. It does not require you to understand the command, only to execute it.

Kant's is "you should." This command is "you must."

I call this command the **Command Body**.

> Dog-training was not invented by anyone.
> It grew out of hunger and fear.

### The Relationship Between the Command Body and "Heteronomy"

One might ask: what is the difference between the Command Body and what Kant calls "heteronomy"?

Kant's "heteronomy" refers to a state in which an individual's will is determined by external factors—desires, emotions, social pressure, tradition. It is an **individual-level** concept: your behavior is not determined by reason, but pushed by something else.

The Command Body is not that individual state itself. It is the **institutionalized structure** that makes that state a universal phenomenon. It controls survival resources and maintains obedience, making "heteronomy" the only option for most people, most of the time.

Kant described the individual state of heteronomy. What he did not describe is: when countless individuals are all in a state of heteronomy, what macro-structure do these heteronomous acts aggregate into? And how does that structure, in turn, reinforce individual heteronomy?

The Command Body is an answer to that question. It is not a blind spot in Kant's vision, but the empirical manifestation of his concept of "heteronomy" at the institutional level.

|  | Kant's Command | Command Body |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Source** | Reason itself | External "structure" |
| **Mode of constraint** | Rational persuasion | Control of survival resources |
| **Nature** | Autonomous | Heteronomous |
| **Form of command** | "You should" | "You must" |
| **Goal** | Freedom | Maintenance of system operation |
| **Attitude toward rational scrutiny** | Requires rational participation | Compresses the time and space for rational scrutiny |


## IV. What the Command Body Compresses: A Kantian Analysis

Kant said: you should use your own reason to legislate for yourself.

But this implies a **precondition**: you need **time** to scrutinize your maxim. You need to pause and ask yourself: "Can the maxim on which I act become a universal law?"

The Command Body operates precisely by compressing this very possibility. It creates urgency by controlling survival resources, so that you cannot complete a full rational examination before acting. You can only "act first, think later."

The gap between "act first, think later" and "think first, act later" is precisely the operational window in which autonomy occurs. When the Command Body compresses this window toward zero, autonomy becomes increasingly difficult at the experiential level.

### Does this mean that "complying with the Command Body" equals "non-autonomy"?

No.

This is a common error—conflating external behavior with inner maxim.

Kant's categorical imperative addresses the **maxim** on which you act—its validity depends on whether it can pass the test of universalizability, not on whether your behavior is outwardly compliant or resistant.

If you are compelled to work excessive overtime under coercive conditions—but your inner maxim is: "I choose to comply with this demand in order to protect my family's survival, and I regard my family as my obligation"—then this maxim has been subjected to rational scrutiny, and your action remains autonomous. Kant does not require you to become a rebel; he requires you to become a **clear-eyed follower** who subjects even acts of compliance to rational examination.

> Kant asks: can your maxim withstand the test of universalizability?
> Not: is your behavior outwardly compliant with or resistant to some authority?

### What, then, does the Command Body actually do?

The Command Body does not cancel your capacity for autonomy. It does not cancel the fact that you "can" be autonomous.

But it does something else: it **compresses the time and space available for you to engage in rational scrutiny**. It ensures that, in the vast majority of situations, you cannot complete the act of "pausing to scrutinize" before you act.

Therefore, the relationship between the Command Body and the categorical imperative is not one of "mutual exclusion"—not a choice between "either obey the Command Body or execute the categorical imperative." The real tension is:

**The categorical imperative requires you to "think first, then act"; the Command Body forces you to "act first, think later," or "think while acting."**

Autonomy remains possible—you can scrutinize after the fact, or remain clear-eyed while acting—but it becomes harder. It requires more vigilance and more effort than Kant anticipated.

This is not a problem of "cannot." It is a problem of "cost."


## V. "Recognition" Is the Beginning of Autonomy

What, then, should we do?

One wrong answer is: wait until the Command Body has been contained, and *then* begin to be autonomous.

This answer falls precisely into the trap Kant warned against—shifting moral responsibility onto external conditions, making autonomy something that "will begin at some future moment." This is equivalent to saying: "Because external conditions are imperfect, I do not need to be autonomous now." This is precisely a variant of what Kant called heteronomy—except that here, the external authority is named "social structure."

But another answer is equally wrong: to pretend the Command Body does not exist, to pretend the path is flat, to pretend the window for rational scrutiny is not being compressed.

Kant's "ought implies can" is correct. But this does not mean: "you can do it effortlessly." It only means: "you can do it"—regardless of external conditions.

**The difficulty of "can" does not negate "can" itself. But it marks the gradient of the path.**

> You cannot say: "Because the slope is steep, I cannot walk."
> But you cannot pretend the slope does not exist, either—walking as if the ground were flat.

What, then, is the right way?

**Recognizing the Command Body *is* the first movement of reason.**

When you pause and become aware that there is a "structure" compressing the time and space for your rational scrutiny—this "awareness" itself is the first step toward autonomy. Because:

1. You are using reason to judge the operation of an external structure.
2. You are using reason to recognize the compression it exerts on you.
3. You are using reason to decide: even within this compression, I can still scrutinize my maxim.

Recognizing the Command Body is not a "precondition" for autonomy—as if you must first recognize it, then contain it, and only then begin to be autonomous. No.

**Recognizing the Command Body *is* the beginning of autonomy.** Because you are already thinking, already scrutinizing, already judging. Your reason is already in motion.

Kant pointed the direction. The Command Body marks the gradient of the path. You do not need to wait for the path to be leveled before you walk. You only need to know, as you walk, that you are on a slope—and not use "being on a slope" as an excuse for "not being able to walk."


## VI. Conclusion

The opening question was: if Kant's "ought implies can" is correct, why is autonomy so difficult to realize in practice?

The answer is not "Kant was wrong." The answer is:

**The difficulty of "can" is compressed by an external structure.**

The Command Body does not cancel your capacity for autonomy. But it compresses the time and space available for rational scrutiny. It prevents you from completing the act of "pausing to scrutinize" before every action. Autonomy remains possible, but it becomes more costly, more scarce, and more demanding of vigilance.

This is not a matter of "not trying hard enough." It is a matter of maintaining rational scrutiny *within* the operation of the Command Body.

Kant answered the question: "Where should we go?"
The Command Body answers: "Why is the path harder than expected?"
Together, they form the complete picture.

> Kant said: human beings should use their own reason to legislate for themselves.
> The Command Body does not negate this.
> It only makes the path steeper.
> And recognizing that it is steep—is the first step onto the path.
