# The Imperative and Kant: Answering an Unfinished Question

> **Core Thesis**: Kant proposed the Categorical Imperative — that one should use one's own reason to give oneself the law, to achieve autonomy. But he did not explain: why do most people not follow this imperative, but instead obey another? This paper argues that a "living thing" — **The Imperative** — has been operating all along. It maintains obedience by controlling the means of survival, forcing individuals to obey before they can even understand the command. Under conditions of survival scarcity, one cannot simultaneously execute the Categorical Imperative and obey The Imperative — the two are mutually exclusive. Therefore, to realize Kant's moral ideal, one must first identify and contain The Imperative.

## Opening

Have you ever seen a dog being trained?

A dog is not persuaded. It is shaped into obedience through food, punishment, and repetition.

Kant made "command" a central concept in moral philosophy. He distinguished between hypothetical and categorical imperatives, arguing that one should use one's own reason to give oneself the law.

He stopped there.

The question he left behind is: why do most people not follow the Categorical Imperative, but instead obey another command?

Following the direction he pointed, I saw a "thing" — The Imperative.

## I. Kant's Categorical Imperative

Kant distinguished between two kinds of commands.

The **Hypothetical Imperative** is conditional: "If you want X, you must do Y." It depends on your personal ends.

The **Categorical Imperative** is unconditional: "You must do A." It does not depend on any external condition — it comes from reason itself.

For Kant, true morality must be based on the Categorical Imperative. What you follow is not an external authority, but a command given by your own reason. Kant called this state **autonomy** — giving the law to oneself. Its opposite is **heteronomy** — accepting legislation from an external authority.

Kant's conclusion: one should use one's own reason to give oneself the law.

> Kant's command comes from reason.
> The Imperative comes from dog training.

## II. The Question Kant Did Not Answer

Kant said: one should use one's own reason to give oneself the law.

This was his conclusion. He stopped there.

He did not continue to ask: if one "should" do this, why do most people not do it?

Kant described the "ought" — one ought to follow the Categorical Imperative, ought to achieve autonomy, ought to obey only the law given by one's own reason. He assumed humans are rational, free, and capable of self-legislation. But he did not ask: what is stopping most people from doing so?

In Kant's framework, if you do not follow the Categorical Imperative, it is because you are not rational enough, or because you are ruled by desire. He attributed the problem to individual failure.

But if you look closely, you will see: most people do not follow the Categorical Imperative not because they lack rationality, but because they are forced to obey another command.

That command comes from parents, tradition, social expectations, survival pressure — from an external "structure" that controls the means of survival.

Kant did not discuss this "structure." He described the "ought," but did not explain "why not."

This gap is the question Kant left behind.

## III. The Imperative

Kant's "command" comes from reason itself — unconditional, autonomous. He placed it on a very high ground.

But there is another "command." It does not come from reason, not from oneself — it comes from an external "structure." It maintains obedience by controlling the means of survival — food, shelter, healthcare, social recognition — all controlled by the system. Those who obey receive resources. Those who disobey lose them. It does not ask you to understand the command — only to execute it.

Kant's is "you should." This command is "you must."

> Dog training was not invented by anyone.
> It grew out of hunger and fear.

I call this command **The Imperative**.

|  | Kant's Command | The Imperative |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Source** | Reason itself | External "structure" |
| **Mode of constraint** | Rational persuasion | Control of survival resources |
| **Nature** | Autonomous | Heteronomous |
| **Form of command** | "You should" | "You must" |
| **Goal** | Freedom | System maintenance |
| **Can it be questioned?** | Yes (rational discussion) | No (obedience is default) |
| **Cost of exit** | Low | High (loss of survival) |

The reader might ask: is The Imperative the same as what Kant called "heteronomy"?

No.

Kant's "heteronomy" refers to the individual will being determined by external factors — desire, emotion, social pressure, tradition. It is an **individual-level** concept — your action is not determined by reason, but pushed by something else.

The Imperative is not the state of "the individual being externally determined." It is the external "structure" that **makes this state a universal phenomenon**. It controls survival resources, maintains obedience, and makes "heteronomy" the only option for most people. Kant described heteronomy. But he did not describe the "structure" that produces it. The Imperative is that "structure."

The Imperative was not designed. It was not invented by anyone. It grew out of conditions of survival scarcity. When an individual cannot secure the means of survival on their own, they must obey the "structure" that controls those resources. For thousands of years, most people have been in this position.

> The hardest part of dog training is not getting the dog to obey —
> it is making the dog believe that obedience was its own choice.

Kant did not see this "structure." He believed that reason alone could set one free. But he did not notice that before reason could speak, a command had already taken its place.

## IV. The Conflict Between the Categorical Imperative and The Imperative

Kant said: one should use one's own reason to give oneself the law.

But he did not notice one thing: before reason could speak, a command had already taken its place.

The Imperative does not issue a "Categorical Imperative." It issues another kind of command — "Obey. Do not question. Or you will not survive." It does not negotiate with you. It directly controls the means of survival. Those who obey receive resources. Those who disobey lose them. Under conditions of survival scarcity, you cannot stop to ask "what should I do?" — you must survive first, and only then can you think about other things.

> The trainer has stopped.
> The dog is still standing there.

This creates a "structural" conflict.

|  | Categorical Imperative | The Imperative |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Source** | Reason itself | External "structure" |
| **Form of command** | "You should" | "You must" |
| **Goal** | Freedom, autonomy | System maintenance |
| **Prerequisite** | You need time to think | You must obey first to survive |

The prerequisites of these two commands are completely opposed. The Categorical Imperative asks you to stop, to use reason to judge whether your maxim could become a universal law — this requires time, space, cognitive ease. The Imperative does not give you that space. It demands immediate execution. Disobey, and you lose survival resources.

> You cannot choose between "must obey" and "obey yourself."

You cannot act under both sets of prerequisites at once. Kant assumed that humans are already free, rational, and capable of self-legislation. He did not notice that before becoming that kind of person, most people have already been shaped into obedient subjects by The Imperative.

This is not because you "did not try hard enough" — it is a "structural" contradiction. The state you are in directly forecloses the possibility of executing the Categorical Imperative.

## V. Conclusion

The opening question was: why do most people not follow Kant's Categorical Imperative, but instead obey another command?

Now it can be answered.

Because The Imperative has been operating all along. It cannot issue a "you should" — it can only issue a "you must." It does not give you the space to ask "what should I do?" — it simply presses you into the position of "you must survive."

Not that you didn't want to. You just didn't have the chance.

Kant believed that humans are free, rational, and capable of self-legislation. This was his endpoint. But he did not see that before reaching that endpoint, most people had already been trained.

It is not your fault. The thing that trained you is still there.

Kant pointed a direction. The Imperative stands on that "structure." Until it is identified and contained, you cannot reach the place Kant spoke of.

Kant answered "where we should go." The Imperative answers "why we haven't arrived yet." Together, they form the complete picture.

> Kant said one should use one's own reason to give oneself the law.
> But before reason could speak, the human had already been trained.
