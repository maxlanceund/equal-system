# Survival Compulsion and U.S. Industrial Decline (Academic Version)

<br>

### Thesis

**Is U.S. industrial decline really China's fault?**

The core argument of this paper is: **No.** The root causes of U.S. industrial decline lie in its internal capital logic, financialization, technological progress, and policy failures — not in the rise of Chinese manufacturing. Attributing the decline to China is a "fallacy of external attribution" that serves domestic political agendas.

At the same time, China's status as the "world's factory" was not achieved through "unfair competition." It was earned through the near-limitless labor intensity, meager wages, depleted health, and fractured families of an entire generation of Chinese workers — a direct manifestation of **Survival Compulsion** within global production networks.

**Chinese workers are not "stealing" anyone's jobs. They are exchanging their blood and sweat for the most basic possibility of survival.**

<br>

### Abstract

This paper deconstructs the popular narrative that "Chinese workers stole U.S. manufacturing jobs." By analyzing the labor intensity, health costs, and family separation of Chinese workers, it reveals the true cost of their so-called "competitiveness." Through historical data, it demonstrates the long-term structural trend of U.S. manufacturing decline and argues that attributing it to China is a logical fallacy.

The analytical framework consists of three concepts:

- **Survival Compulsion**: Chinese workers endure extreme conditions not because of an inherent "diligence" but because they lack viable alternatives. This is determined by their structural position in the global value chain.
- **External Attribution**: U.S. politicians simplify complex domestic structural issues into "China stole jobs" — a classic scapegoat mechanism.
- **Structural Shift**: Globalization and automation are the fundamental causes of the sustained decline in U.S. manufacturing employment, a trend that predates and is independent of China's industrial rise.

**Keywords**: Survival Compulsion; External Attribution; U.S. Industrial Decline; Global Production Networks; Scapegoat Economics

<br>

### I. Introduction: A Misunderstood Causal Chain

In 2025, U.S. manufacturing employment fell to **8.0%** of total employment — the lowest in history. Simultaneously, U.S. political rhetoric has persistently blamed this decline on China's manufacturing expansion following its accession to the WTO. "China stole our jobs" has become a recurring campaign slogan.

However, this narrative rests on a neglected premise: **Where does the "competitiveness" of Chinese workers actually come from?**

This paper examines the issue across three dimensions:

- First, what survival costs do Chinese workers bear?
- Second, what are the true causes of U.S. industrial decline?
- Third, who are the real winners?

<br>

### II. Survival Compulsion: The Real Situation of Chinese Workers

#### 2.1 Working Hours: Far Above Legal Limits

In 2025, the average weekly working hours for employees in Chinese enterprises was **48.6 hours**, far exceeding the statutory limit of **44 hours per week** stipulated by the Labor Law. Manufacturing frontline workers generally follow a "12-hour workday, single rest day" schedule. Manufacturing employees work an average of **5.68 days per week**, with **61.46%** working six days or more. Among frontline manufacturing workers, this figure reaches as high as **74%**.

Annualized calculation: 48.6 hours/week × 52 weeks ≈ **2,527 hours/year**. In comparison, U.S. manufacturing workers average approximately **1,700 hours/year**.

**Chinese manufacturing workers work roughly 800 more hours per year than their U.S. counterparts.**

#### 2.2 Wages: High Intensity, Low Return

In 2025, the average annual salary for production and related personnel in China's industrial enterprises above designated size was **80,739 RMB** (approximately **$11,000 USD**). For migrant workers in manufacturing, the average monthly income was **5,126 RMB** (approximately **$6,100 USD/year**).

In comparison, the average hourly wage for U.S. manufacturing workers is approximately **$25-30 USD**, with an annual salary of over **$70,000 USD**.

**Chinese manufacturing workers earn roughly one-sixth to one-seventh of their U.S. counterparts.**

Tesla provides a more intuitive comparison: Shanghai factory workers earn approximately **$14,000-$15,000 USD/year**, while U.S. factory workers earn about **$82,500 USD/year**. Chinese workers earn just **17%** of U.S. wages but are more than **twice as productive**.

#### 2.3 Health: Exchanging Bodies for Survival

In 2024, China reported **11,289** new cases of occupational diseases, including **7,251** cases of pneumoconiosis and **2,227** cases of occupational ear, nose, and throat diseases.

National Health Commission monitoring data shows that over **24 million** workers are exposed to occupational hazards, covering more than **1.815 million** job positions. Among approximately 70 million occupational health examinations, **76,700** suspected occupational disease cases and **1.156 million** occupational contraindications were identified. Cumulatively, approximately **1.05 million** occupational disease cases have been reported, of which about **90% are pneumoconiosis**.

#### 2.4 Family: The Cost of 60 Million Left-Behind Children

According to the Seventh National Population Census, China has **66.93 million** left-behind children aged 0–17 — meaning nearly half of the country's children are spending their childhood in a state of "mobility" or "left-behind" status.

In 2025, China's floating population reached **358 million**, with approximately **117 million** children of migrant workers — **one in every two children is affected by population mobility**. A large number of workers return home only once a year, exchanging "family separation" for meager income.

#### 2.5 Section Summary

The "competitiveness" of Chinese workers does not come from "unfair competition" but from their **greater capacity to endure — longer hours, lower protections, more separation.**

This is not a moral badge of "industriousness." It is the reality of **Survival Compulsion.**

### III. External Attribution: The Truth of U.S. Industrial Decline

#### 3.1 The Long-Term Decline in Manufacturing Employment

U.S. manufacturing employment as a share of total employment:

| Year | Share |
|------|-------|
| End of WWII | **38%** |
| Early 1950s | **>30%** |
| 1990 | **16.2%** |
| 2024 | **8.1%** |
| 2025 | **8.0%** |

**This is a long-term trend spanning over 70 years — predating the rise of Chinese manufacturing by decades.**

In absolute numbers: U.S. manufacturing employment was **17.2 million** in 2000, falling to **12.7 million** by September 2025 — a loss of **4.5 million jobs in 25 years**. In 2025 alone, manufacturing employment shrank by **156,000 jobs**, the largest annual decline since the pandemic.

#### 3.2 Real Causes: Automation and Globalization

Federal Reserve data indicates that the primary drivers of manufacturing employment decline are **globalization and automation**:

- Between 1977 and 2001, U.S. manufacturing employment declined by **41%**.
- Between 2000 and 2010, **87%** of manufacturing job losses were due to **productivity gains** (i.e., automation), with trade accounting for just **13%**.

The share of service sector employment rose from about 57% in the 1950s to over **86%** in 2025 — Americans did not "lose jobs"; they shifted from factories to hospitals, schools, restaurants, and office buildings.

#### 3.3 Policy Failures and Capital Logic

- **Capital Outflow**: Multinational corporations pursue profit maximization, relocating production overseas. China is merely one destination among many.
- **Financialization**: Returns on Wall Street far exceed those in the real economy, naturally channeling capital toward "money-making" finance.
- **Educational Mismatch**: The U.S. vocational education system has failed to keep pace with industrial upgrading, leading to a shortage of skilled workers.
- **Aging Infrastructure**: The average age of U.S. infrastructure exceeds 50 years, driving up logistics costs.

#### 3.4 Section Summary

**Blaming China is using a simplistic "scapegoat" to conceal a complex structural transition.**

Was U.S. industrial decline caused by China? **The data says no.** It is a 70-year-long domestic structural decline whose timeline does not coincide with China's industrial rise.

### IV. Structural Shift: Who Takes the Real Profits?

Take the example of a **$1,000 USD** iPhone:

| Entity / Component | Share of Profit/Cost |
|-------------------|----------------------|
| **Apple Inc.** | **58.5%** |
| Raw Material Costs | 21.9% |
| Korean Suppliers | 4.7% |
| Other U.S. Companies | 2.4% |
| EU | 1.1% |
| Japan | 0.5% |
| Taiwan, China | 0.5% |
| **Mainland China Workers (Assembly)** | **Only 1.8%** |

Based on the iPhone 17 Pro Max price of **9,999 RMB**, Chinese workers receive approximately **180 RMB**.

**Apple takes nearly 60% of the profits, while the Chinese workers who assemble the phone with their own hands receive less than 2%.**

Who are the real winners of globalization?

- **Multinational capital** — took the lion's share of profits.
- **U.S. consumers** — enjoyed cheap goods while blaming workers for job losses.
- **Chinese workers** — contributed the most, received the least, yet are accused of "stealing jobs."

<br>

### V. Conclusion

Through three layers of analysis — **Survival Compulsion** (the real cost for Chinese workers), the **Fallacy of External Attribution** (the domestic roots of U.S. decline), and **Structural Shift** (the inequality of global profit distribution) — this paper attempts to answer a question grossly distorted by political discourse:

**Is U.S. industrial decline really China's fault?**

The answer is no.

Chinese workers have not stolen anyone's jobs. They are exchanging their blood and sweat for the most basic possibility of survival, within a global structure that offers them few alternatives. The decline of U.S. industry is a long-term consequence of domestic capital logic, policy failures, and technological change — unrelated to the labor of Chinese workers.

Attributing blame to China is both an evasion of reality and a disregard for the sacrifices of Chinese laborers.

**The real way forward is not to find a scapegoat, but to face one's own structural issues directly.**

### A Final Note

This paper does not criticize any government or national policy or system. It argues only one thing: **Chinese workers did not take away American jobs.**

Chinese workers endure longer hours, less rest, wages roughly one-sixth of what U.S. workers earn, and inadequate working conditions — not because they are "willing," but because **Survival Compulsion** leaves them with few other options.

To put it plainly: **the Chinese people do not have the same conditions as the American people.**

If, after reading these facts, you still believe that the situation of Chinese workers is something to be admired, that only means you have not yet understood the meaning of the word "cost." If you have seen the documentary *American Factory*, you will remember the scene where Chinese workers pick up broken glass with their bare hands — that is not a symbol of diligence or courage. It is a demonstration of what happens when survival compulsion overrides individual freedom.

The data has already shown that Chinese workers receive very little of the value they produce. Most of the profit is taken by capital.

**What can be said has been said. The rest is silence.**
