## Abstract

This paper identifies a recurrent structure in language acquisition and short-video attention competition: input is pre-screened, signals compete within a finite time window, acceleration occurs before the boundary, and your choices are already limited. Further extension shows that the same structure can be identified in life (birth, death), institutions (work, wealth, politics), and relationships (marriage, domestic violence).

The core contribution of this paper is not merely to describe this structure, but to **test whether it is a genuine structure**. If the four features merely appear side by side, the paper is doing pattern recognition. If the four features are causally coupled, it is a structure. This paper uses agent-based models to conduct three tests: Experiment 1 shows a "recognition cliff" between screening strength and boundary acceleration — when screening is too strong, the system cannot recognize the boundary, and acceleration does not initiate; Experiment 2 shows that the more intense the signal competition, the lower the proportion of options at the boundary; Experiment 3 shows that removing boundary acceleration causes option contraction to disappear. All three coupling chains hold: the four features form a structure, not a coincidence.

**Keywords**: limited-interval principle · signal competition · boundary acceleration · system structure · limit theory of meaning · formal constraints

## Literature Positioning and Scholarly Dialogue

The core observation of this paper — that finite systems present a structure of signal density increase before the boundary — engages with the following intellectual traditions:

1. **Systems theory tradition** (Luhmann, 1984): understands social systems as self-reproducing through communicative operations. Luhmann's concept of the system boundary formally corresponds to this paper's "finite interval" — both refer to the termination point of system operations. However, Luhmann focuses on how systems maintain themselves through communication, rather than the pattern of signal density change as the system approaches its boundary. This paper's contribution lies in supplementing Luhmann's boundary concept with a formal description of "pre-boundary signal dynamics," and in providing a test of the coupling mechanism.

2. **Archaeology of knowledge tradition** (Foucault, 1969): concerns the conditions and boundaries of discourse formation. Foucault's "rules of discourse formation" reveal the a priori conditions of "what can be said," which structurally correspond to this paper's "input pre-screened." However, this paper's unit of analysis is not discourse, but the formal structure shared by any finite system (language, media, life, institutions).

3. **Philosophy of language tradition** (Wittgenstein, 1953): concerns how meaning in language games emerges from specific use. This paper's "meaning is defined only within the interval" shares the same intuition as Wittgenstein's "meaning is use." However, this paper further argues that this "context" itself can be formally described as a structure of four features, and identifies its recurrence across multiple independent domains.

This paper's contribution lies in integrating observations dispersed across these three traditions into a unified formal structure, identifying its manifestations across multiple independent domains, and testing its coupling through numerical experiments.

## Theoretical Framework and Methodological Statement

### Definition of Core Concepts

**Finite interval**: the temporal, spatial, or optional range within which a system operates, with a clear starting point and endpoint. Beyond this interval, the system's signal processing function is no longer defined.

**Input pre-screened**: before the system receives input, the input has already passed through some pre-filtering mechanism (genes, algorithms, institutions, culture), such that the system can only access a subset of all possible inputs.

**Signal competition**: within a finite interval, different signals (information, attention, resources, choices) compete within limited time and capacity, and only some signals can be processed or transmitted by the system.

**Boundary acceleration**: as the system approaches the boundary of the interval, if there are still unreleased signals, signal density will first rise sharply and then return to zero. Geometrically, this appears as a spike. **Key distinction**: the strong version of boundary acceleration claims "unreleased signals imply inevitable acceleration"; the weak version claims "acceleration occurs only when the system recognizes the boundary." This paper adopts the weak version (the strong version has been falsified in *Boundary Acceleration*).

**Options limited**: at the boundary of the interval, the available choices have already contracted to a narrower range than within the interval.

**Structural coupling**: a causal relationship between two features — changing one changes the other. If they merely appear side by side, they are not coupled.

**Recognition cliff**: when input screening strength exceeds a certain threshold, the system has too few signals to recognize the boundary, and acceleration does not initiate. This produces a non-monotonic relationship between "screening strength and acceleration morphology": first rising, then falling — the fall is due to recognition failure, not weakening acceleration.

### Methodological Statement: Formal Analysis, Structural Description, and Numerical Verification

The methodological path adopted in this paper lies between **formal analysis**, **structural description**, and **numerical verification**. The paper first describes the structure of multiple independent systems at a conceptual level, revealing their shared logical form; it then translates the four features into operational variables through agent-based models, testing whether there is causal coupling between them.

The types of evidence used in this paper include:

1. **Conceptual definitions**: strict definitions of core concepts such as finite interval, signal competition, and boundary acceleration.
2. **Case descriptions**: structural descriptions of vocal cord reading, short-video algorithms, life courses, and other cases.
3. **Formal mappings**: tables demonstrating the correspondence of the four-feature structure across different cases.
4. **Numerical verification**: testing the coupling of the four features using agent-based models (see Appendix A).
5. **Falsifiability conditions**: proposing testable conditions under which the core thesis would be falsified.

## I. Introduction: Two Phenomena, One Structure

Two observations:

1. Reading a fixed English passage repeatedly over two days reduces the time from 33 seconds to 20 seconds.
2. Short-video platforms consistently push the most sensational content to you, while calm content is rarely seen.

These two phenomena seem unrelated. But they share the same underlying structure. I call it the **Limited-Interval Principle**.

This structure consists of four parts: input is pre-screened; signals compete within a finite time window; acceleration occurs before the boundary; and your choices are already limited.

**But "looking like the same structure" is not the same as "actually being the same structure."** If the four features merely appear side by side in these domains, this paper is only doing pattern recognition. If the four features are causally coupled, it is a genuine structure.

Appendix A reports the results of three tests: **all three coupling chains hold.**

## II. Four Features

**Input is pre-screened.** The text you read is not written by you — it is given. The videos you see are not the platform's entire inventory — they are ranked by an algorithm. What you can access has already been filtered by someone else.

**Signals compete within a finite time window.** Your attention is limited. Heavier signals — angry faces, fast cuts, urgent tones — are more likely to capture your attention. Calm content takes longer to register, but you don't have that much time.

**Acceleration before the boundary.** The boundary is the moment you stop or swipe away. Near the end of a sentence, your voice naturally intensifies; short videos usually put their strongest material in the first few seconds, or you'll swipe away. **But acceleration is not automatic — it requires the system to recognize the boundary.** If there are too few signals, the system cannot accurately estimate where the boundary is, and acceleration does not initiate.

**Your choices are already limited.** You can choose to read faster or slower, but you cannot change the text. You can choose which video to watch, but you cannot determine the composition of your feed.

## III. Case Study 1: Vocal Cords

> *My idea is simple. Equality does not mean giving everyone the same things…*

Reading this passage over two days:

33s → 25s → 21s → 20s.

The time decrease is not because you "memorized it." It is because your vocal cords switched from "bouncing word by word" to "gliding through the whole sentence." The system automatically found a more efficient way.

In this case: the text is given (input pre-screened); two reading styles compete (upright vs flat); reading accelerates until stabilizing around 20 seconds (boundary acceleration); you can choose your reading style but not the text (options limited).

Note: Chinese can be read flat, and English can be read upright. The distinction here is not that "Chinese is always upright and English is always flat," but that the vocal cords can actively choose different vocal patterns when facing different texts. The time compression process is the vocal cords converging from a high-energy mode to a low-energy mode.

---

## IV. Case Study 2: Short-Video Platforms

The goal of short-video algorithms is simple: keep you from swiping away. They don't care if content is "good" — they only care if the signal is "strong enough."

The more sensational, conflict-driven, and fast-paced the content, the more likely it is to grab you within 1–2 seconds. The algorithm isn't "evil" — it's just executing this rule: the boundary is too short, so the signal must be pushed high in the first few seconds, or you'll leave.

In this case: the content pool is algorithmically filtered (input pre-screened); sensational content overwhelms calm content (signal competition); the first few seconds tend to maximize signal intensity (boundary acceleration); you can choose what to watch but not the feed composition (options limited).

Note: This does not mean "every video has conflict in the first three seconds." It means that, across a large volume of content, higher-signal-density content is more likely to be recommended to you.

## VI. From "Looks Like a Structure" to "Is a Structure": Coupling Test

The above is description. Description can show that the four features **appear together**, but cannot show that they **depend on each other**.

To prove that the four features constitute a structure, we must answer: remove one, do the others deform?

Appendix A reports three sets of tests. The core results:

| Experiment | Coupling chain tested | Result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Experiment 1 | Input screening → boundary acceleration morphology | Holds (recognition cliff: when screening is too strong, recognition fails, acceleration does not initiate) |
| Experiment 2 | Signal competition → option contraction | Holds (type count 10→100, option ratio 0.645→0.340, monotonic decrease) |
| Experiment 3 | Boundary acceleration → option contraction | Holds (with acceleration: 15.03 options; without acceleration: 29.03 and 28.93) |

**Experiment 3 is decisive.** It shows: removing boundary acceleration causes option contraction to disappear. This means boundary acceleration is not an incidental presence — it is one of the mechanisms of option contraction.

**Experiment 1 reveals the condition for acceleration to occur.** Boundary acceleration is not unconditional — it requires the system to recognize the boundary. When screening is too strong, the system has too few signals available and recognition fails; acceleration does not initiate at all. This directly validates the weak version of *Boundary Acceleration*.

**All three chains hold. The four features are not four coincidences. They are four links in a causal chain.**

## VII. Falsifiability Conditions

If the core thesis of this paper is to hold, it must be open to challenge.

1. **Falsifiability Condition 1**: If a system is found that satisfies the four conditions (input pre-screened, finite signal competition, boundary acceleration, limited options) but does not exhibit this structure — for example, input is pre-screened but there is no signal competition, or no acceleration before the boundary, or options are not limited — then the "Limited-Interval Principle" described in this paper would not hold.

2. **Falsifiability Condition 2**: If it could be shown that the above four features are not formal constraints of the system but can be artificially eliminated — for example, through technological means that completely remove input screening, or indefinitely extend the boundary, or infinitely expand options — then the paper's claim that "this structure is a necessary feature of finite systems" would need to be revised.

3. **Falsifiability Condition 3**: If a finite system is found that does not exhibit signal density increase before the boundary, but instead transitions smoothly to the boundary, then the paper's claim that "boundary acceleration is a universal feature of finite systems" would be shaken.

4. **Falsifiability Condition 4 (based on experiments)**: If numerical experiments show that removing boundary acceleration still leaves option contraction intact, then the conclusion "boundary acceleration is coupled with option contraction" is falsified, and the four features revert to a parallel relationship.

5. **Falsifiability Condition 5 (based on Experiment 1)**: If screening strength and acceleration morphology show a monotonic relationship rather than a "recognition cliff," then the weak-version claim that "the system's recognition of the boundary is a precondition for acceleration to initiate" is falsified.

## VIII. Conclusion

The structure identified in this paper is: input is pre-screened → signals compete within a finite time window → acceleration occurs before the boundary → your choices are already limited.

It appears across language, media, life, institutions, and relationships. It does not depend on specific content, only on the formal constraints of the system.

**More importantly: three sets of experiments prove that it is coupled, not parallel.** Input screening drives acceleration morphology by affecting the system's ability to recognize the boundary; boundary acceleration drives option contraction; signal competition drives option contraction. All three causal chains hold.

The universality of this structure means that: regardless of the system's content — as long as you face a finite interval, this four-step structure appears. It is not a feature of any particular culture, nor of any particular era, but a formal feature of finite systems themselves.

The structural description provided in this paper is at the formal level. It does not explain why this structure appears — this is a question for future research. The contribution of this paper is to abstract this structure from concrete cases, name it the "Limited-Interval Principle," and use three sets of numerical experiments to test its coupling, enabling subsequent research to identify it, test it, and revise it across different domains.

## Appendix A: Coupling Test

This appendix reports three sets of numerical experiments testing whether the four features are coupled. The experiment code is located in the `limited-interval-principle/experiments/` directory.

### A.1 Experimental Model

An agent-based abstract model is used, not bound to any specific domain.

- The system runs within a finite time window [0, T], T = 100
- The system has N possible signal types, with heterogeneous but approximately uniform weights
- The system has a processing capacity C = 10 per step
- **Input screening**: parts of the signal with intensity below threshold θ are invisible; screening strength determines the number of visible signals
- **Signal competition**: each step can only process signals within capacity C, with selection probability w_i^γ, where γ increases with signal intensity
- **Boundary acceleration**: when the system recognizes the boundary, signal intensity increases near T; when not recognized, it does not increase
- **Options**: a type is called an "option at the boundary" if and only if it is processed at least once and is still processed within the last 10 steps

Core mechanism: signal intensity increase → γ becomes larger → selection becomes more exponential → a few high-weight types monopolize → low-weight types are squeezed out in the final window → option contraction.

**Version note on boundary acceleration**: Experiment 1 adopts the weak version (acceleration only when the system recognizes the boundary); Experiments 2 and 3 adopt the model-specified acceleration profile (to test the coupling of the acceleration phenomenon with option contraction, not to test the mechanism by which acceleration arises).

### A.2 Experiment 1: Input Screening → Boundary Acceleration Morphology (Weak Version)

**Hypothesis H1**: Screening strength and acceleration amplification factor show a "recognition cliff" relationship — when screening is too strong, the system cannot recognize the boundary, and acceleration does not initiate.

**Method**: N = 30, T = 100, C = 10, τ = 8. Recognition threshold = 3 (the system recognizes the boundary when visible signals ≥ 3). Screening strength swept from 0 to 0.97. Each group has 10 trials.

**Results**:

| Screening strength | Visible signals | Boundary recognized | Acceleration amplification |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0.00 | 30 | Yes | 1.00 ± 0.00 |
| 0.30 | 21 | Yes | 1.00 ± 0.00 |
| 0.50 | 15 | Yes | 1.00 ± 0.00 |
| 0.60 | 12 | Yes | 1.00 ± 0.00 |
| 0.70 | 9 | Yes | 1.11 ± 0.00 |
| 0.80 | 5 | Yes | 2.00 ± 0.00 |
| 0.83 | 5 | Yes | 2.00 ± 0.00 |
| **0.87** | **3** | **Yes** | **3.33 ± 0.00 (peak)** |
| 0.90 | 2 | No | 1.00 ± 0.00 |
| 0.93 | 2 | No | 1.00 ± 0.00 |
| 0.97 | 1 | No | 1.00 ± 0.00 |

**Conclusion**: H1 is supported. The peak occurs at the edge of the recognition threshold (visible signals = 3): the system can still recognize the boundary, but the middle interval has already been squeezed very thin, so acceleration is steepest. One step stronger (visible signals = 2), recognition fails, and acceleration drops directly to zero (amplification = 1.00).

**This is not a simple inverted U, but a "recognition cliff"**: the limit on the left is mid-interval signal saturation (acceleration not pronounced), and the limit on the right is recognition failure (acceleration does not initiate). The narrow window in the middle is where acceleration is steepest.

**Key significance**: This directly validates the core proposition of the weak version of *Boundary Acceleration* — boundary acceleration is not unconditional; it requires the system to recognize the boundary. When screening is too strong, the system has too few signals available and recognition fails; acceleration does not initiate at all.

### A.3 Experiment 2: Signal Competition → Option Contraction

**Hypothesis H2**: The more intense the competition (the larger the number of types), the lower the proportion of options at the boundary.

**Method**: Fix θ = 0.5, vary N (10, 20, 30, 50, 100), 20 trials per group, reporting mean and standard deviation.

**Results**:

| N | Option count | Option ratio | Acceleration amplification |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 10 | 6.5 ± 1.2 | 0.645 | 51.00 |
| 20 | 10.8 ± 2.3 | 0.540 | 51.00 |
| 30 | 14.7 ± 1.8 | 0.490 | 51.00 |
| 50 | 22.1 ± 2.5 | 0.441 | 51.00 |
| 100 | 34.0 ± 3.7 | 0.340 | 51.00 |

**Conclusion**: H2 is supported. The option ratio monotonically decreases with competition intensity (0.645 → 0.340). The more intense the competition, the lower the proportion of options the system can retain at the boundary.

### A.4 Experiment 3: Boundary Acceleration → Option Contraction

**Hypothesis H3**: Removing boundary acceleration causes option contraction to disappear or significantly weaken.

**Method**: Fix N = 30, θ = 0.5, and compare three signal profiles:

- A: With boundary acceleration (signal intensity surges near T)
- B: Constant signal (no acceleration)
- C: Slowly decaying signal (no acceleration)

30 trials per group. **Version note**: The acceleration profile in this experiment is model-specified, not dependent on system recognition. It tests "the coupling of the acceleration phenomenon with option contraction," not "the mechanism by which acceleration arises" (the latter is tested by Experiment 1).

**Results**:

| Group | Signal profile | Options at boundary | Max |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A | With boundary acceleration | 15.03 ± 2.09 | 30 |
| B | Constant signal | 29.03 ± 0.76 | 30 |
| C | Slowly decaying signal | 28.93 ± 0.94 | 30 |

**Conclusion**: H3 is supported, and decisively. With boundary acceleration, options contract to 15.03 (about half); without boundary acceleration, options barely contract (29.03, 28.93). The gap is nearly 14 options, with a standard deviation of only about 2 — not random fluctuation.

**Boundary acceleration is a mechanism of option contraction. Remove it, and option contraction disappears.**

### A.5 Overall Conclusion

| Coupling chain | Result | Key data |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Input screening → acceleration morphology | Holds (recognition cliff) | Peak 3.33 at the edge of recognition threshold; falls to 1.00 after recognition failure |
| Signal competition → option contraction | Holds (monotonic) | Option ratio 0.645 → 0.340 |
| Boundary acceleration → option contraction | Holds (decisive) | 15.03 vs 29.03 |

**All three coupling chains hold, sufficient to prove that the four features form a structure, not a coincidence.**
