# On the Boundaries of Meaning: A Verificationist Framework

## Abstract
This paper puts forward a "Limit‑of‑Meaning Thesis": meaning is a signal generated by finite systems through closed‑loop operations within a finite empirical interval.
Outside this interval, meaning is undefined—not reduced to zero, but no longer defined.
Grounded in the verifiability criterion, any claim that extends meaning beyond the interval relies on unverifiable premises and must therefore be excluded from operable discourse.
Tracing the intellectual lineage from Wittgenstein’s *Tractatus Logico‑Philosophicus* and logical positivism, this paper shows how the framework avoids both metaphysical overreach and skeptical despair.
It draws distinctions between "immediate loop" and "extended loop", "primary meaning" and "received meaning", and responds to objections concerning sacrificial acts, posthumous legacy, and religious frameworks.
The result is a boundary‑marking tool that clarifies what can be meaningfully said and what must remain silent.

**Keywords**: meaning · verificationism · boundary · empirical interval · closed‑loop operation · Wittgenstein

---

## 1 Introduction
What does it mean for a life, a word, or an action to be "meaningful"?
Traditional accounts often appeal to eternal values, cosmic justice, or posthumous recognition.
This paper argues that such appeals are structurally defective: they place the "verification button" outside the finite interval within which meaning is generated and received.

Drawing on the verificationist insight—that unverifiable propositions are cognitively empty—I propose the Limit‑of‑Meaning Thesis:

`Meaning = ∮_interval closed‑loop operation | outside‑boundary = undefined`

Put plainly: meaning arises within a system’s operational interval (typically its lifespan) through completed feedback cycles (input → processing → output → feedback).
Any claim extending meaning beyond this interval is not negated, but merely undefined—much like asking for the value of a function outside its domain of definition.

This paper is structured as follows.
Section 2 situates the theory within historical debates over meaning and verifiability.
Section 3 defines core notions, introducing key distinctions: immediate‑loop / extended‑loop and primary‑meaning / received‑meaning.
Section 4 derives the theorem and its corollaries.
Section 5 addresses possible objections, including the case of sacrifice and Mishima Yukio.
Section 6 draws practical implications for ethics, religion and personal life.
Section 7 discusses theoretical limitations and open questions.
Section 8 concludes with "Silence beyond the Boundary".

## 2 Historical Background: The Verificationist Tradition
The association of meaning with verifiability has a rich history.
In his *Tractatus Logico‑Philosophicus* (1922), Wittgenstein famously declared: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent."
For him, a proposition is meaningful only if it depicts possible facts; ethical, aesthetic and religious statements lack factual content and thus fall outside the bounds of meaning.
Logical positivists (Carnap 1928; Ayer 1936) radicalized this into the **verifiability principle**: a statement possesses cognitive meaning if and only if it is empirically verifiable (or at least confirmable).

This principle faces well‑known difficulties: it is not itself empirically verifiable, and it appears to disqualify scientific laws and historical claims.
Later philosophers (Quine 1951; Putnam 1981) abandoned the strict criterion, yet the core intuition—that meaning must connect to possible experience—persists in various forms, for example Dummett’s verification‑based theory of meaning (1976).

My Limit‑of‑Meaning Thesis does not revive original logical positivism.
Instead I adopt a **system‑relative verificationism**: meaning is defined relative to a particular system (a conscious subject) and its finite operational interval.
This evades self‑refutation: the criterion is a **definitional convention for the operational use of the word "meaning"**, not a universal factual claim about the world.

## 3 Core Concepts
### 3.1 Primary Meaning and Received Meaning
#### Primary Meaning
A signal generated by the system’s internal closed‑loop when performing an action.
It is defined only within the interval in which the action occurs.

#### Received Meaning
A new closed‑loop signal generated within its own interval by a subsequent system (e.g. later readers, witnesses) when processing causal traces left by an earlier system.

This distinction is crucial.
It allows us to acknowledge the intuition that sacrificial acts are meaningful (the mother completes an immediate loop in acting; the child generates received meaning within their own interval), while preserving the core theorem that meaning does not cross boundaries.
Traces may cross intervals, but meaning is always regenerated within a new interval.

### 3.2 Empirical Interval ($`I_e`$)
The time span in which a system (e.g. a human subject) actively receives feedback signals and generates a sense of orientation.
It begins when the system starts its operations.
It ends when signal‑processing ceases (usually death or loss of consciousness).

### 3.3 Causal Traces ($`T_r`$)
Observable external changes brought about by a system’s operations, denoted $`T_r`$.
Traces may persist after the interval ends (e.g. written books, a rescued child).
Nevertheless they are only physical or informational continuations.
They are not meaning‑bearing signals for the original subject.
This is because no feedback loop is still running.

### 3.4 Meaning ($`M(t)`$)
In this paper, a signal counts as "meaningful" if and only if two conditions hold:
(a) it orients the system’s operations;
(b) it is produced by a closed‑loop operation: input received → processing → output action → feedback signal received, which then guides subsequent operations.

This stipulation is not dogmatic a priori; it draws on a cybernetic insight (Bateson 1972): the meaning of information lies in its differential effect upon subsequent system states.
A signal unable to feed back and alter what the system does next is operationally neutral and uninformative.
Completion of the closed‑loop is therefore treated as a necessary operational condition for generating meaning.
Physical traces outside this loop (books, legacies) exist only as causal traces $`T_r`$; their meaning must be regenerated in later systems.

Two kinds of closed‑loop are distinguished:
- **Immediate Loop**: feedback arrives internally right upon completion of the act.
Example: I follow my conscience and make a choice; immediately I perceive "this is right" or "this is what I ought to do".
This is the primary case treated in the paper.
- **Extended Loop**: meaning depends on external outcomes occurring some time after the act, even after the system ceases to function.
Example: "I save this child and hope she survives"—the verifying event (child’s survival) may happen after the system has ended.

Extended‑loop constructions are widespread in human meaning‑making.
Still they do not satisfy "complete feedback within the interval".
Within the present framework their status is therefore **undecidable** (neither defined nor negated).
This is not to deny their importance; it marks them as objects outside the scope of this framework.
Regarding such extended loops this paper remains silent—they may well be valid, but they belong to another domain of definition.

### 3.5 Undefined versus Zero
At the boundary of the interval, meaning ceases to be defined.
This is not negation (as if meaning becomes "zero") but termination of the function’s domain—analogous to evaluating a mathematical function outside its prescribed domain.
We call this state **undefined**, not "zeroed‑out".

## 4 Derivation of the Limit‑of‑Meaning Theorem
**Theorem**: For any finite system S with empirical interval $`[0, T]`$, the meaning function $`M(t)`$ is defined only for $`t\in[0,T]`$.
For $`t>T`$, $`M(t)`$ is undefined.

**Premises**:
1. **Finite‑system premise**: meaning depends on a signal‑processing system. When the system stops, the carrier of meaning disappears.
2. **Condition‑dependence premise**: meaning is not an inherent constant; it varies with system states, external conditions and time.
3. **Verifiability‑exclusion premise**: any assertion extending $`M(t)`$ beyond $`T`$ relies on unverifiable presuppositions (after‑life, cosmic accounting etc.), and therefore cannot be part of an operable framework.

**Proof sketch**:
If $`M(t)`$ were defined for $`t>T`$, some mechanism would have to record, store or update meaning, without the original system operating.
Such a mechanism requires extra presuppositions (cosmic ledger, divine judgement, historical progress) which are unobservable within $`[0, T]`$.
By premise 3 these presuppositions are excluded from our definition.
Hence $`M(t)`$ is undefined beyond the interval.

The theorem does not deny persistence of causal traces $`T_r`$, nor deny that later systems may assign new meaning to those traces (received meaning).
What it denies is that original‑subject meaning‑signals remain defined as operational signals of the original subject after the subject’s interval closes.

**Corollary**:
Any claim that "the meaning of an action persists after the agent’s death" is logically marked undecidable within this framework.
It might be true, but its truth‑value lies outside the framework’s demonstrative power.
This is not refutation, but honest demarcation of the theory’s scope.

## 5 Objections and Replies
### 5.1 Objection One: Sacrificial Acts
A mother sacrifices her life to save her child.
Does not the act derive its very point from consequences (the child surviving) that lie beyond her own lifespan?
Does the Limit‑of‑Meaning Thesis render such acts meaningless?

**Reply**: The framework operates on two levels.
(a) **Primary meaning**: at the moment of sacrifice the mother completes an **immediate closed‑loop**.
She perceives danger, values the child’s life, and carries out the act.
Meaning is assigned *at that moment* as she receives internal feedback: "I have done what I ought to do".
This counts as a meaningful operation within the framework.
(b) **Received meaning**: after surviving, the child, together with witnesses, reads this causal trace $`T_r`$ and generates received meaning belonging to their own interval.

Sacrificial acts are therefore not "meaningless".
Primary meaning is already closed in life, and received meaning will be continuously regenerated by successors.
The theory rejects only one claim: that the mother’s primary meaning itself continues to exist across the boundary of death.
Traces can be transmitted, but meaning can only be generated on‑site.

### 5.2 Objection Two: The Case of Yukio Mishima
Mishima Yukio’s suicide points toward an aesthetic ideal, and he looks forward to understanding and echo from posterity.
If the verification he desires lies after his death, how does this theory view the situation?

**Reply**: Distinguish two readings.
If he pursues an immediate closed‑loop confirmed inwardly in the moment of action, then this primary meaning holds within the framework.

If his appeal depends on evaluation by later generations, then verification falls outside his interval and counts as an extended‑loop, marked undecidable.
The framework does not pronounce this pursuit illusory.
It only states: such expectation cannot be verified through his own closed‑loop.

Many tragedies possess this structure: the subject entrusts the verification of meaning to a future he will never see.

### 5.3 Objection Three: Religious and Metaphysical Frameworks
Religion, karma, or narratives of historical final destiny all supply meaning crossing individual life‑and‑death boundaries.
Does this theory negate them?

**Reply**: The Limit‑of‑Meaning Thesis does not deny the subjective utility of such frameworks within the interval.
They can supply direction, consolation and motives for action.
Nevertheless, when such frameworks claim that meaning remains effective across empirical‑interval boundaries, the relevant propositions become undecidable under this operational definition.
They are not "false", nor "hollow"; they lie outside the judging capacity of this tool.

This theory does not assert "religious meaning is illusory".
It only states: if we adopt this closed‑loop test for meaning, cross‑boundary statements about meaning cannot be incorporated.
These are two separate language‑games; neither can serve as the tribunal for the other.
One may hold religious narratives, so long as one does not demand cognitive endorsement from this framework.
Correspondingly, the framework does not deny real closed‑loop religious experience within the present‑life interval (e.g. inner peace in prayer, release upon repentance).

## 6 Practical Implications
This theory offers several stable guidelines for individual existence.
- **Ethics**: Moral value need not be deferred to an after‑life or remote historical judgement.
An action possesses primary meaning as long as intent, performance and internal validation complete a closed‑loop within the agent’s interval.
People may still voluntarily choose actions oriented toward extended‑loops—provided they clearly recognise that ultimate verification remains undecidable.
- **Death**: Death terminates the domain of definition for meaning; it does not erase what has already been accomplished in life.
Already‑closed primary closed‑loops are not revoked, and require no post‑humous ratification.
This is not equivalent to everything turning to nothing.
Causal traces $`T_r`$ persist, left for successors to continuously generate new received meaning.
- **Religion and belief**: Faith can be a stable source of orientation within the present‑life interval.
Yet its cross‑boundary assertions cannot be verified under this closed‑loop standard.
The framework neither supports nor opposes religious practice.
It only draws boundaries: the ultimate warrant of faith lies beyond the justifying competence of this framework.

This is not a doctrine of despair.
On the contrary, it lifts the heavy burden that meaning must be eternal.
Meaning can occur fully, solidly and completely within finite life.
A meal, a conversation, a choice can complete a perfect closed‑loop without hoping for eternal preservation.
At the same time, the mechanism of received‑meaning explains why classics continue to move later generations.
It is not that ancient meaning survives across time; rather every new reader collides with textual traces $`T_r`$ and regenerates meaning belonging to themselves.

## 7 Theoretical Limitations and Open Questions
The whole framework is built upon one foundational presupposition: the bearer of meaning is a finite subject equipped with feedback closed‑loops.
This is a working hypothesis, not an ontological conclusion already proven.
It can explain a large set of human phenomena of meaning, yet it may not cover everything.
Many human beings naturally anchor meaning in grand processes transcending individuals.
Such psychological reality will be marked undecidable by the framework, instead of being simply dismissed as wrong.
This marks the boundary of the theory, not a flaw.

Two open directions worthy of further investigation:
1. Can meaning be generated by non‑individual closed‑loops?
If we expand the bearer of meaning from individual consciousness to collectives or civilizations as distributed systems, can we construct a definition of closed‑loop applicable to collectives?
This will reshape the boundary of the empirical interval.
2. Does "undecidable" equal valueless?
This framework strictly distinguishes cognitive verifiability and value weight.
Something which cannot be verified by closed‑loop does not mean it is not worthy of human commitment.
Logical boundary and value weight are two separate matters.

This paper holds: honestly marking boundaries is more prudent than forcing a grand theory that claims to explain everything.

---

## 8 Conclusion: Silence beyond the Boundary
The Limit‑of‑Meaning Thesis is a boundary‑marking instrument, not a new metaphysics.
It does not claim to uncover ultimate truth of the world.
It merely proposes a self‑consistent, operable rule for judging meaning compatible with finite observation.
It inherits Wittgenstein’s distinction between what can be said and what cannot be said.
Meanwhile it incorporates cybernetic closed‑loop conditions, placing boundaries at both ends of every subject’s empirical interval.

Within the interval, meaning can be real, stable and sufficient.
Beyond the interval, the domain of primary‑meaning closes.
What lies beyond the boundary? This framework cannot answer.
Any answer itself would constitute boundary‑transgression.

Silence is not negation.
It does not advocate nihilism.
It merely acknowledges: discourse of meaning built upon subject‑closed‑loops possesses inherent boundaries.
Finite life can still generate complete meaning.
We only ought not to demand that this meaning must live forever.
