# On the Limits of Meaning: A Verificationist Framework

> **Core Thesis**: Meaning is a signal generated by a finite system within a finite experiential interval through closed-loop operations. Outside that interval, meaning is undefined — not zero, but no longer defined. Any claim that extends meaning beyond the interval relies on unverifiable premises and must be excluded from operational discourse. This paper proposes a "Limit Theory of Meaning" as a boundary-marking tool that clarifies what can be meaningfully said and what must be consigned to silence.

## Abstract

This paper proposes a *Limit Theory of Meaning*, according to which meaning is a signal generated by a finite system within a finite experiential interval through closed-loop operations. Outside that interval, meaning is undefined — not zero, but no longer defined. The theory is built upon a verificationist criterion: any claim that extends meaning beyond the interval relies on unverifiable premises and must be excluded from operational discourse. I trace the intellectual lineage from Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* and logical positivism, and introduce crucial distinctions between *immediate loops* and *extended loops*, and between *primary meaning* and *received meaning*. These distinctions allow the theory to address objections concerning sacrifice, posthumous legacy, and religious frameworks without overreaching into normative judgment. The result is a boundary-marking tool that clarifies what can be meaningfully said and what must be consigned to silence.

**Keywords**: meaning · verificationism · limit · experiential interval · closed-loop operation · Wittgenstein

## Literature Positioning and Scholarly Dialogue

Before proceeding to the formal analysis, it is necessary to clarify this paper's position within the existing scholarly landscape. On the relation between meaning and verifiability, the existing literature mainly contains three competing explanatory paths:

1. **Logical positivist tradition** (Carnap, 1928; Ayer, 1936): adopts the verifiability principle as the demarcation criterion for cognitive meaning — a statement is cognitively meaningful only if it is empirically verifiable (or at least confirmable). However, the principle faces notorious difficulties: it is not itself empirically verifiable, and it seems to disqualify scientific laws and historical claims.

2. **Late Wittgensteinian tradition** (Wittgenstein, 1953): abandons the search for a unified criterion for meaning, turning instead to the actual use of language games. Meaning is use — different language games have different rules; there is no "essence of meaning" beyond concrete practices. However, this path tends toward description rather than prescription, making it difficult to provide a clear demarcation tool.

3. **Pragmatist tradition** (Putnam, 1981; Rorty, 1979): treats meaning and truth as relative to particular communities and contexts, rejecting any transcendental standard of meaning. However, this path may lead to relativism, making it difficult to address the question "whether some meaning-claims are better than others."

This paper's contribution lies in integrating insights from all three traditions — the positivist demand for verifiability, Wittgenstein's sensitivity to language games, and the pragmatist emphasis on context — into a **system-relative verificationist framework**. It avoids the self-refutation problem of logical positivism (because the criterion is set as a definitional convention rather than a universal factual claim), avoids the descriptivism of late Wittgenstein (because it provides an operational demarcation tool), and avoids the relativism of pragmatism (because its validity criterion is internal consistency).

## Theoretical Framework and Methodological Statement

### Definition of Core Concepts

Before proceeding, it is necessary to strictly define the core concepts used in this paper.

**Experiential Interval (Iₑ)**: The time span during which a system (e.g., a human subject) actively receives feedback signals and generates a sense of orientation. It begins when the system initiates operation and ends when it ceases signal processing (typically at death, or at the loss of consciousness).

**Causal Trace**: Observable changes in the external world caused by the system's operations. Traces can continue after the interval ends (e.g., a book written, a child saved), but they are mere physical or informational continuations — not meaning-bearing signals for the original subject, because no feedback loop operates for that subject.

**Meaning (M)**: A signal generated within the interval that orients the system's operations — encompassing value judgments, emotional assignments, and cognitive directions. Meaning is generated through **closed-loop operations**: the system receives input, processes it, executes an output, and receives feedback in response.

**Immediate Loop**: The feedback signal is received by the system at the very moment the action is completed. Example: I make a choice according to my conscience; upon completion, I immediately perceive "this was right" or "this is what I should have done." This is the primary object of the theory's analysis.

**Extended Loop**: The meaning of an action depends on outcomes that occur after the action is completed — possibly after the system itself has ceased to operate. Example: "I save this child, hoping she will live" — the verification signal (the child's survival) may come after the system's interval has ended. Extended loops do not satisfy the condition of "complete in-interval feedback," and therefore their meaning-status is treated as **undecidable** (neither defined nor negated) within this framework.

**Primary Meaning**: The signal generated within the system's internal closed loop at the moment of action. It is defined only within the interval in which the action occurs.

**Received Meaning**: The new closed-loop signal generated by a subsequent system (e.g., a later reader, a witness) when it processes the causal traces left by a prior system.

**Zeroing vs. Undefined**: At the boundary of the interval, meaning ceases to be defined. This is not a negation (as if meaning became "nothing"), but a termination of the function's domain — like the value of a mathematical function outside its specified range. We call this state **undefined**, not "zeroed."

### Methodological Statement: System-Relative Verificationism

The methodological path adopted in this paper is **system-relative verificationism**. Unlike logical positivism, this paper does not treat the verificationist criterion as a universal truth about the world, but rather as a **definitional convention for the operational use of the term "meaning."** Its validity does not consist in its reflecting some cosmic truth, but in its providing an internally consistent, usable boundary-marking tool.

In other words, this paper does not claim that "all meaning must conform to this framework." It claims: "If you adopt this framework for delimiting 'meaningful operations,' you will obtain a self-consistent theory that is immune to unverifiable premises." It is a tool, not a doctrine. Whether one adopts the tool depends on whether one accepts verificationist criteria as the priority standard for demarcating meaning.

The types of evidence used in this paper include:

1. **Conceptual definitions**: strict definitions of core concepts such as experiential interval, meaning, and closed-loop operation.
2. **Logical derivation**: deriving the Limit Theorem and its corollaries from the definitions.
3. **Case testing**: testing the theory's explanatory power through cases such as sacrificial acts and the Mishima case.
4. **Objections and replies**: responding to possible objections and clarifying the theory's boundaries.

## I. Introduction

What does it mean for a life, a word, or an action to be *meaningful*? Traditional answers often appeal to eternal values, cosmic justice, or posthumous recognition. This paper argues that such appeals are structurally flawed: they place the "verification button" outside the finite interval in which meaning can be generated and received. Drawing on the verificationist insight that unverifiable propositions are cognitively empty, I formulate a *Limit Theory of Meaning*:

> **Meaning = ∮_Interval Closed-loop Op | Beyond = Undefined**

In plain terms: meaning arises only within a system's operational interval (typically its lifetime), through actions that complete a feedback loop (input → processing → output → feedback). Any purported meaning that extends beyond the interval is not *negated*, but simply *undefined* — like asking for the value of a function outside its domain.

Section III defines the core concepts, introducing the key distinctions between immediate/extended loops and primary/received meaning. Section IV derives the theorem and its consequences. Section V answers likely objections, including sacrificial acts and the case of Yukio Mishima. Section VI outlines the practical import of the theory. ## II. Historical Background: The Verificationist Tradition

The idea that meaning is tied to verifiability has a rich history. In the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*, Wittgenstein (1922) famously declared: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." He argued that propositions only make sense if they picture possible facts; ethical, aesthetic, and religious statements, lacking factual content, fall outside the bounds of sense. The logical positivists (Carnap, 1928; Ayer, 1936) radicalised this into the *verification principle*: a statement is cognitively meaningful only if it is empirically verifiable (or at least confirmable).

However, the principle faced notorious difficulties: it was not itself empirically verifiable, and it seemed to disqualify scientific laws and historical claims. Later philosophers (Quine, 1951; Putnam, 1981) abandoned the strict criterion, but the core intuition — that meaning requires some connection to possible experience — survives in various forms (e.g., Dummett's verificationist theory of meaning, 1976).

My Limit Theory does not revive logical positivism in its original form. Instead, I adopt a *system-relative* verificationism: meaning is defined relative to a particular system (a conscious agent) and its finite operational interval. This avoids the self-refutation problem — because the criterion itself is a **definitional convention** for how the term "meaning" is operationally used, not a universal factual claim about the world. Its validity does not consist in its reflecting some cosmic truth, but in its providing an internally consistent, usable boundary-marking tool.

In other words, this paper does not claim that "all meaning must conform to this framework." It claims: "If you adopt this framework for delimiting 'meaningful operations,' you will obtain a self-consistent theory that is immune to unverifiable premises." It is a tool, not a doctrine. Whether one adopts the tool depends on whether one accepts verificationist criteria as the priority standard for demarcating meaning.

## III. Core Concepts

### 3.1 Experiential Interval (Iₑ)

The time span during which a system (e.g., a human subject) actively receives feedback signals and generates a sense of orientation. It begins when the system initiates operation and ends when it ceases signal processing (typically at death, or at the loss of consciousness).

### 3.2 Causal Trace

Observable changes in the external world caused by the system's operations. Traces can continue after the interval ends (e.g., a book written, a child saved), but they are mere physical or informational continuations — not meaning-bearing signals for the original subject, because no feedback loop operates for that subject. However, traces can serve as inputs for *new* systems, which may generate *received meaning* in their own intervals.

### 3.3 Meaning (M) and Closed-Loop Types

Meaning is a signal generated within the interval that orients the system's operations — encompassing value judgments, emotional assignments, and cognitive directions. Meaning is *defined* only when the system completes a closed loop: it receives input, processes it, executes an output, and receives feedback in response.

This paper distinguishes two types of closed loops:

- **Immediate Loop**: The feedback signal is received by the system at the very moment the action is completed. Example: I make a choice according to my conscience; upon completion, I immediately perceive "this was right" or "this is what I should have done." This is the primary object of the theory's analysis.

- **Extended Loop**: The meaning of an action depends on outcomes that occur after the action is completed — possibly after the system itself has ceased to operate. Example: "I save this child, hoping she will live" — the verification signal (the child's survival) may come after the system's interval has ended.

The theory acknowledges that extended loops are widespread in human meaning-construction. However, because they do not satisfy the condition of "complete in-interval feedback," their meaning-status is treated as **undecidable** within this framework (neither defined nor negated). This is not to deny their importance, but to mark them as "outside the scope of this framework." On extended loops, the theory remains silent — they may be perfectly valid, but they belong to a different domain of discourse.

### 3.4 Primary Meaning and Received Meaning

The theory further distinguishes two layers:

- **Primary Meaning**: The signal generated within the system's internal closed loop at the moment of action. It is defined only within the interval in which the action occurs.
- **Received Meaning**: The new closed-loop signal generated by a *subsequent* system (e.g., a later reader, a witness) when it processes the causal traces left by a prior system.

This distinction is crucial. It allows us to acknowledge the intuition that "sacrificial acts are meaningful" (the mother completed an immediate loop at the moment of action; the child generates received meaning in her own interval), while preserving the core theorem that meaning does not cross boundaries. Traces can cross; meaning is regenerated within each new interval.

### 3.5 Zeroing vs. Undefined

At the boundary of the interval, meaning ceases to be defined. This is not a negation (as if meaning became "nothing"), but a termination of the function's domain — like the value of a mathematical function outside its specified range. We call this state **undefined**, not "zeroed."

## IV. The Limit Theorem and Its Derivation

**Theorem**: For any finite system S with experiential interval [0, T], the meaning function M(t) is defined only for t ∈ [0, T]. For t > T, M(t) is undefined.

**Premises**:

1. **System finitude**: Meaning depends on a system capable of receiving and processing signals. When the system ceases, meaning loses its carrier.
2. **Conditional dependence**: Meaning is not an inherent constant; it varies with the system's state, external conditions, and time.
3. **Verifiability exclusion**: Any claim that extends M(t) beyond T rests on an unverifiable premise (e.g., an afterlife, a cosmic ledger, historical progress), and thus cannot be part of an operational framework.

**Proof sketch**:

If M(t) were defined for t > T, there would have to be some mechanism that continues to register, store, or update meaning without the original system. Such a mechanism would require an additional premise — e.g., a cosmic ledger, divine judgment, or historical progress — that is not observable within [0, T]. By premise 3, such premises are excluded from the definition. Therefore, M(t) is undefined outside the interval.

This theorem does not deny that causal traces persist; nor does it deny that subsequent systems can assign *received meaning* to those traces. It only denies that the original subject's meaning-signal continues to be defined as the original subject's operational signal after its interval has ended.

**Corollary**: Any claim that "the meaning of an action continues after the agent's death" must be logically marked as *undecidable* — it may be true, but its truth-value lies outside the framework's capacity for proof. This is not a negation, but an honest marking of the theory's limits of application.

## V. Possible Objections and Replies

Any theoretical argument must be open to questioning of its core claims. This chapter outlines the main objections this paper may face and responds to each.

### Objection 1: Sacrificial Acts

A mother sacrifices her life to save her child. Her action seems meaningful precisely because it aims at an outcome (the child's survival) beyond her own lifespan. Does the Limit Theory render such an act meaningless?

**Reply**: This objection requires the distinction between two layers.

**(a) Primary Meaning**: At the moment of sacrifice, the mother completed an *immediate loop*: she perceived a threat, processed the value of her child's life, and executed the action. Meaning was assigned *at that moment* — she received the internal feedback of having acted according to her values. This is a meaningful operation as defined by the framework.

**(b) Received Meaning**: After the child survives, the child generates meaning-signals within *her own interval* in response to the mother's action ("She died for me"). This is a new closed-loop operation, triggered by the causal trace left by the mother. This meaning is fully defined within the child's interval.

Therefore, the sacrificial act is not "meaningless" — it is complete at the level of primary meaning, and it can generate received meaning in subsequent systems. The theory only states that meaning does not *persist* across boundaries — but *traces can cross*, and traces can trigger *new* meaning loops in new intervals. This is precisely the point: meaning does not endure, but meaning can be regenerated.

### Objection 2: The Mishima Case

Yukio Mishima's suicide was intended to prove the value of a certain aesthetic ideal, with an expectation of posthumous recognition. If his meaning depended on that recognition, how does the Limit Theory assess his operation?

**Reply**: This case requires a more nuanced analysis.

If Mishima's primary target was an *immediate loop* — i.e., the instantaneous verification of his aesthetic ideal in the very act of its execution — then his operation is defined within this framework. He perceived the alignment of his will with his ideal, executed the act, and may have received internal feedback at that moment.

However, if his meaning depended on the *extended loop* of posthumous recognition, then the verification signal lay outside his interval. In that case, the operation's meaning-status is **undecidable** under this framework. The theory does not call it "failure" — it calls it "a case this framework does not assess."

The tragedy of Mishima, from a structural perspective, may lie in anchoring his meaning to a signal he could not verify within his interval. This is a structural observation, not a moral judgment. A rational agent may choose to anchor meaning in extended loops — provided they acknowledge that the verification button lies beyond their interval.

### Objection 3: Religious and Metaphysical Frameworks

Religion, karma, or historical progress offer meaning that transcends individual intervals. Are they all to be dismissed?

**Reply**: The Limit Theory does not deny the *subjective utility* of such frameworks within the interval — they can provide orientation, comfort, and motivational force. However, when these frameworks claim that their meaning extends beyond the experiential boundary, the theory marks such claims as **undecidable**: not "false," not "empty," but "the framework's toolbox cannot process their justification."

The theory does not claim "religious meaning is false." It claims: "If you adopt this framework for delimiting meaning in operational terms, then cross-boundary extensions of meaning cannot be subsumed under that definition." This is a logical distinction, not a value judgment. One may live by a religious narrative — as long as one does not demand that this theory certify its cognitive validity. Conversely, the theory need not deny that religious experiences can constitute genuine immediate loops within an individual's interval (e.g., the peace felt in prayer, the release felt in confession).

In other words, religious frameworks and this framework are **different language games**. They can coexist, but neither should serve as the other's verification tribunal.

## VI. Falsifiability Conditions

If the core thesis of this paper is to hold, it must be open to challenge — that is, there must be discoverable facts or arguments that, if they turned out to be true, would seriously weaken or require revision of the paper's central position.

1. **Falsifiability Condition 1:** If it could be shown that there exists a mechanism by which the original subject's meaning-signal continues to be defined as the original subject's operational signal after its experiential interval has ended — for example, through some observable technological means that enable consciousness to continue receiving and processing feedback signals after death — then the paper's core thesis that "meaning is undefined outside the interval" would be weakened.

2. **Falsifiability Condition 2:** If it could be shown that certain cross-boundary meaning-claims (such as religious promises, historical judgment) can be empirically verified within the interval, without relying on unobservable premises, then the paper's claim that "cross-boundary meaning-claims rely on unverifiable premises" would be shaken.

3. **Falsifiability Condition 3:** If it could be shown that closed-loop operation is not a necessary condition for the generation of meaning — for example, that there exists a meaning-generating mechanism that does not depend on feedback loops — then the paper's definitional claim that "meaning is generated through closed-loop operations" would need to be revised.

To date, none of the above falsifiability conditions have been met. The framework remains valid within the current range of observation and logic.

## VII. Practical Applications

The theory offers clear guidelines for individual life:

- **Ethics**: Moral value is not deferred to an afterlife or historical judgment; it is enacted within the interval. Actions are meaningful if they complete a loop of intention, execution, and internal validation. This does not preclude an individual from anchoring their ethics in extended loops — provided they recognise the undecidability of that verification.

- **Death**: Death is the termination of the domain, not a negation of what was done. The integral generated within the interval is already complete; it does not require external confirmation. Nor does this render all things void: traces persist, and can be reinvested with received meaning by subsequent systems.

- **Religion**: Faith can be a source of orientation within the interval, but its cross-boundary promises are cognitively undecidable. The theory neither endorses nor opposes religious practice; it only clarifies its logical status. It allows an individual to live by a religious narrative while acknowledging that its ultimate verification lies outside the framework's justification.

The theory is not a doctrine of despair. On the contrary, by removing the demand for eternity, it frees meaning to be fully realised in finite acts. A meal, a conversation, or a piece of music can be perfectly meaningful without needing to last forever. At the same time, through the concept of received meaning, it preserves the possibility that traces, when read by later systems, can generate new meaning — not the *persistence* of meaning, but its *regeneration*.

## VIII. Conclusion: The Silence Beyond the Boundary

The Limit Theory of Meaning is a boundary-marking tool, not a metaphysical system. It does not claim to discover a hidden truth about the universe; it merely proposes a definition of meaning that is internally consistent, empirically grounded in the system's operational interval, and immune to unverifiable premises. It stands in the lineage of Wittgenstein's silence and Russell's logical clarity, but advances beyond them by formalising the boundary and offering a practical criterion for distinguishing meaningful discourse from mere speculation.

What can be said, has been said here. But what lies beyond the boundary? This framework cannot answer that question — because *any answer would be a crossing of the boundary*. Yet we are not compelled to answer. As Wittgenstein wrote at the end of the *Tractatus*: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent."

But silence is not negation. It is not the despair of nihilism, but an honest acknowledgment of the finitude of operational meaning. Beyond the silence, perhaps, stands something — an existence that does not belong to the operational logic of meaning, yet may be more real than any operation. But that lies beyond the scope of this paper.

Here, we stop.
