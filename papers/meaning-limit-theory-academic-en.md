## Abstract

This paper proposes a "Limit Theory of Meaning," according to which meaning is a signal generated by a finite system within a finite experiential interval through closed-loop operations. The core turn of this paper is: the system's representation of "beyond the interval" is itself a closed-loop signal generated within the interval. This means that "beyond the interval" is not an ontological position outside the interval, but an internal marker by which the system represents its own boundary. The boundary is not externally given; it is recognized by the system; and the act of recognizing the boundary is itself a meaningful operation.

The theory is built upon a system-relative verificationist criterion: any claim that extends meaning beyond the interval relies on unverifiable premises and must be excluded from operational discourse. The paper traces the intellectual lineage from Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* and logical positivism, and introduces the distinctions between "immediate loops" and "extended loops," and between "primary meaning" and "received meaning." On this basis it addresses objections concerning sacrifice, posthumous legacy, religious frameworks, and deconstructive attacks.

This paper provides an operational detection procedure for internal markers (three steps: intervention test, counterfactual test, closed-loop test), a decidability rule for systems (three conditions: observable boundary, closable operation, traceable history), and a dilemma response to deconstructive attacks ("local" forfeits adjudicatory authority, "global" collapses into self-reference). This paper ultimately applies this law to itself, and points out the **asymmetry** between this paper and the Lacanian framework: the Lacanian framework cannot withstand self-application, whereas this paper's framework can. This is not "who is more correct." This is "who can better withstand being applied." The result is a boundary-marking tool that clarifies what can be meaningfully said — and that "silence" itself is also a marker within the interval.

**Keywords**: meaning · verificationism · limit · experiential interval · closed-loop operation · internal marker · self-application

## Literature Positioning and Problem Anchoring

Before proceeding to the formal analysis, it is necessary to clarify this paper's problem-consciousness and academic coordinates. The point of departure is not a simple revival of logical positivism, but a response to a core difficulty it left unresolved.

Carnap (1928) and Ayer (1936) attempted to use the "verifiability principle" as a demarcation criterion for cognitive meaning, excluding metaphysics from scientific discourse. However, this project received a fatal blow from Quine (1951). In "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," Quine demonstrated that any empirical verification depends on a whole network of background beliefs; isolated propositions cannot be independently confirmed or falsified. This means that the analytic/synthetic distinction on which positivism relied collapses, and the demarcation project loses its logical foundation.

But does abandoning demarcation necessarily lead to thoroughgoing relativism? The later Wittgenstein (1953) offered a descriptive alternative in his "language-game" approach — meaning is use, different games have different rules. However, while this path avoids metaphysical overreach, it also renounces any normative force, leaving it unable to address the question of "whether some meaning-claims are better than others."

This paper attempts to open a third path between these two positions, but does not stop at the formulation of "a third path." The deeper turn of this paper is: from "discussing the interval and beyond the interval" to "discussing how the system produces representations of beyond the interval." This turn is continuous with the "representational turn" in the history of philosophy (Kant, Husserl, the later Wittgenstein), but this paper unfolds it through operational definitions rather than merely through conceptual analysis.

Specifically, this paper performs six core operations:

1. It does not treat "verifiability" as a universal truth-standard about the world, but operationalizes it as the domain-boundary of a meaning-function — a system-relative definitional convention.
2. It does not treat "beyond the interval" as an ontological position, but identifies it as an internal marker produced by the system within the interval.
3. It does not treat the "boundary" as externally given, but identifies it as an operational state recognized by the system within the interval.
4. It provides an operational detection procedure and a decidable system rule.
5. It applies this paper's framework to this paper itself — this paper is also a system, it has its own interval.
6. It points out the asymmetry between this paper and the Lacanian framework: this paper can withstand self-application, the Lacanian framework cannot.

## Theoretical Framework and Methodological Statement

### Definition of Core Concepts

**Experiential Interval (Iₑ)**: The time span during which a system actively receives feedback signals and generates a sense of orientation. It begins when the system initiates operation and ends when it ceases signal processing.

The boundary of the interval is not externally given; it is recognized by the system. The act of recognizing the boundary is itself a closed loop.

**Causal Trace**: Observable changes in the external world caused by the system's operations. Traces can continue after the interval ends, but they are mere physical continuations — not meaning-bearing signals for the original subject, because no feedback loop operates for that subject.

**Boundary**: Not a line outside the interval, but an internal marker recognized by the system within the interval.

**Beyond the Interval**: Not a position outside the interval, but the system's representation, within the interval, of "the place it cannot reach."

**Meaning (M)**: A signal is "meaningful" if and only if it satisfies two conditions: (a) it orients the system's operations; (b) it is generated through closed-loop operations.

The act of recognizing the boundary itself satisfies these two conditions. Therefore, recognizing the boundary is not something outside meaning. It is within meaning.

**Immediate Loop**: The feedback signal is received by the system at the very moment the action is completed.

**Extended Loop**: The meaning of an action depends on outcomes that occur after the action is completed. The "undecidability" of extended loops is not because they are outside the interval, but because they depend on the system's representation, within the interval, of "the future."

**Primary Meaning**: The signal generated within the system's internal closed loop at the moment of action.

**Received Meaning**: The new closed-loop signal generated by a subsequent system when it processes the causal traces left by a prior system.

**Zeroing vs. Undefined**: At the boundary of the interval, meaning ceases to be defined. "Undefined" is not an assertion about "the outside," but an internal marker by which the system represents its own boundary.

**Internal Marker**: The representation produced by the system within the interval, concerning its own boundary. Its content may be "I cannot reach there"; its production is a closed-loop operation; its status is not an assertion about reality, but a definitional naming.

---

### The Three Types of Closed Loops

| Type | Definition | Example | Produces Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| External loop | Input → Processing → Output → External feedback | Walking with a slight deviation; the eyes correct it | Yes |
| Internal loop | Input → Processing → Internal feedback → Adjustment | Being moved by music; listening again | Yes |
| Pseudo-loop | Input → Processing → No feedback | Mind-wandering, trance | No |

The key distinction: whether the feedback affects the next operation. Internal feedback does change the system's next operation — this is not self-consolation, but the operation being changed. In the pseudo-loop, the internal state changes but the operation does not; therefore no meaning is produced.

---

### The Three Conditions of a System

1. **Has a boundary**: can distinguish "inside" from "outside" (even if this boundary is itself an internal marker).
2. **Has operations**: closed loop of receiving input, processing, outputting, and receiving feedback.
3. **Has history**: operations unfold in time, and states are variable.

Any entity satisfying all three conditions is a system. A human individual is a system; a company is a system; a language game is a system. A stone is not a system; a mathematical proposition is not a system.

---

### Operational Detection Procedure for Internal Markers

**Step One: Intervention Test**

- Remove the candidate marker m (or make it ineffective).
- Observe whether the system's next operation changes.
- If it changes → m affects operation; proceed to Step Two.
- If it does not change → m is not an internal marker.

**Step Two: Counterfactual Test**

- Keep the system's other states unchanged.
- Change the system's own operational state (e.g., let the system be "near the boundary" vs. "far from the boundary").
- Observe whether m changes accordingly.
- If it changes → m's content concerns the system's own state; proceed to Step Three.
- If it does not change → m concerns an external object; not an internal marker.

**Step Three: Closed-Loop Test**

- Record the full operational chain before and after m's production.
- Confirm that m's production is part of a closed loop (m affects the next step, the next step affects m).
- If it is a closed loop → m is an internal marker.
- If it is not → m is merely an internal state, not an internal marker.

These three steps provide a decidable procedure, not a definition. Anyone who claims "your marker is a misrecognition" must first demonstrate, through this procedure, that the detected state does not satisfy these three steps. If it satisfies all three, it is an internal marker — **regardless of what it may also mean at the unconscious level.**

**Who judges whether the operation has changed**: the system itself. Whether the system's own next operation changes is judged by the system's internal closed loop. This avoids the problem of an external standard while maintaining system-relativity.

---

### Decidability Rule for Systems

An object O is a system in the sense of this paper if and only if:

1. **Observable boundary**: there exist observable input/output channels between O and the outside. An object with no observable input/output is not a system.
2. **Closable operation**: after O receives input, its internal state changes, its output changes, and this output affects the next input. An object that cannot form a closed loop is not a system.
3. **Traceable history**: O's state changes in time, and the changes are path-dependent. An object with no state change is not a system.

| Object | Boundary | Operation | History | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Individual | Yes | Yes | Yes | System |
| Company | Yes | Yes | Yes | System |
| Language game | Yes | Yes | Yes | System |
| Stone | Has | No | No | Non-system |
| Deleuze's "flow" | No | No | No | Non-system |
| Heidegger's "Being" | No | No | No | Non-system |
| Lacan's "unconscious" | Unobservable | No closed loop | Untraceable | Non-system |

**Definition of "observable"**: observed by the system itself.

**The system is a methodological concept, not an ontological concept**: this paper does not claim that "a stone is essentially not a system," but only that "a stone does not satisfy this paper's operational decidability rule, and therefore falls outside this paper's scope."

---

### Methodological Statement: System-Relative Verificationism

The methodological path adopted in this paper is system-relative verificationism. The object of this paper is not "the interval and beyond the interval," but "the interval, and the system's representation within the interval of 'beyond the interval.'"

This paper is continuous with Kant, Husserl, and the later Wittgenstein: from "what the world is" to "how the system produces representations of the world."

**Objects this paper does not address**: meaning in the ontological sense (Heidegger), becoming and flow (Deleuze), representations at the unconscious level (Lacan).

**This paper ultimately does not exempt itself**: this paper is also a system. It has its own interval. Everything it says is also its own internal marker. This point is developed in Section VIII.

## I. Introduction

What does it mean for a life, a word, or an action to be *meaningful*? Traditional answers often appeal to eternal values, cosmic justice, or posthumous recognition. This paper argues that such appeals are structurally flawed: they place the "verification button" outside the finite interval in which meaning can be generated and received.

Drawing on the verificationist insight that unverifiable propositions are cognitively empty, I formulate a *Limit Theory of Meaning*:

> **Meaning = ∮_Interval Closed-loop Op | Boundary = Internal Marker within the Interval**

## II. Historical Background: From Verificationism to the Representational Turn

The idea that meaning is tied to verifiability has a rich history. In the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*, Wittgenstein (1922) famously declared: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." The logical positivists (Carnap, 1928; Ayer, 1936) radicalized this into the *verification principle*.

However, the principle faced notorious difficulties: it was not itself empirically verifiable. Later philosophers (Quine, 1951) abandoned the strict criterion, but the core intuition — that meaning requires some connection to possible experience — survives in various forms.

This paper does not revive logical positivism in its original form. It does something else: it does not treat verifiability as a universal truth about the world, but operationalizes it as the domain-boundary of a meaning-function. More importantly — it does not treat "beyond the interval" as an external position, but identifies it as an internal marker produced by the system within the interval.

This operation is continuous with the "representational turn" in the history of philosophy (Kant, Husserl, the later Wittgenstein). Kant's "thing-in-itself" is not an object that can be known, but a limit set by the cognitive act itself. Husserl's "intentionality" is not "consciousness pointing to an external object," but "consciousness constituting the object." The later Wittgenstein's "language game" is not "language describing the world," but "language as the world."

This paper's contribution within this tradition is: to formalize the concept of "representation" through the operational mechanism of "closed-loop operation." The system cannot "see" beyond the interval. It can only produce representations of "the outside" within its own interval. And these representations are themselves closed-loop signals — they are meaningful.

## IV. The Revised Limit Theorem

> For any finite system S, its experiential interval [0, T] is recognized by S within the interval. The meaning function M(t) is defined only for t ∈ [0, T]. "Beyond the interval" — i.e., M being undefined for t > T — is an internal marker produced by S within the interval, not an ontological position outside the interval.

**Corollary 1**: The system cannot step outside the interval to verify what "beyond the interval" is.

**Corollary 2**: Any claim that "the meaning of an action continues after the agent's death" must be logically marked as "undecidable."

**Corollary 3**: "Silence" is not a stance toward the outside of the interval, but a marker within the interval.

## V. Possible Objections and Replies

### Objection 1: Sacrificial Acts

**Reply**: The sacrificial act is not "meaningless" — it is complete at the level of primary meaning, and it can generate received meaning in subsequent systems.

### Objection 2: The Mishima Case

**Reply**: Mishima's expectation of "posthumous recognition" is itself an internal marker he produced within his interval. This marker itself is a meaningful operation.

### Objection 3: Religious and Metaphysical Frameworks

**Reply**: The religious framework's commitment to "cross-boundary meaning" is itself an internal marker produced by the believer within the interval. This marker is real and meaningful.

### Objection 4: Aesthetics and Contemplation

**Reply**: Aesthetic experience is meaningful — not because it "reflects beauty," but because it **is a closed-loop operation produced by the system within the interval**.

### Objection 5: The Misuse of Wittgenstein

**Reply**: This paper's position is system-relative verificationism — an operationalized private verificationism.

### Objection 6: The Self-Referential Dilemma of Deconstructive Attacks

**Reply**: Any framework that claims "all self-representations are misrecognitions" cannot exempt itself. This paper's framework never promised to escape the system in the first place, and therefore does not face the self-referential dilemma.

**Dilemma**:

| Opponent's Position | Result |
| :--- | :--- |
| "I am local" | Forfeits adjudicatory authority |
| "I am global" | Collapses into self-reference |
| "I am both local and global" | Self-contradictory |

## VI. Practical Applications

- **Ethics**: Moral value is enacted within the interval.
- **Death**: Death is the termination of the domain, not a negation of what was done.
- **Religion**: Religion's "commitment" as an internal marker within the interval remains a meaningful operation.

The theory is not a doctrine of despair. On the contrary, by removing the demand for eternity, it frees meaning to be fully realized in finite acts.

## IX. Asymmetry: Who Can Better Withstand Self-Application

This paper does not exempt itself, nor does it exempt its opponents.

But there is an asymmetry: **when the Lacanian framework is applied to itself, it collapses; when this paper's framework is applied to itself, it acknowledges the result.**

### 9.1 The Collapse of the Lacanian Framework

Lacan's core proposition is:

> All systems' representations of themselves are misrecognitions.

This proposition itself is a system's representation of itself. Therefore, by its own standard:

- This proposition itself is also a misrecognition.
- Its author cannot know what he is saying.
- He cannot know whether it is true.

Lacan has only two paths:

**Path One**: admit that the proposition "all self-representations are misrecognitions" is **an exception**. This amounts to saying: Lacan's theory has an external exemption. But Lacan denies the external. **Contradiction.**

**Path Two**: admit that this proposition **also includes itself**. Then Lacan's theory is a misrecognition. He cannot use a misrecognition to evaluate this paper. **Self-referential collapse.**

**There is no third path.**

### 9.2 This Paper's Acknowledgment

When this paper's framework is applied to itself:

- This paper says "all meaning is internal markers."
- When this proposition is applied to this paper itself: this paper is also a system, and what this paper says is also an internal marker.
- **This paper acknowledges the result.**

This paper does not exempt itself. This paper acknowledges from the outset that it is an operation within a system. Therefore, when this paper's framework is applied to itself, this paper does not collapse — **it acknowledges the result.**

### 9.3 The Meaning of the Asymmetry

This is not "who is more correct." This is "who can better withstand self-application."

- **This paper can withstand it.** Because from the outset it acknowledges that it is an operation within a system.
- **Lacan cannot withstand it.** Because his framework claims to reveal the misrecognition of all systems — and yet it cannot exempt itself from this judgment.

**Therefore, this paper does not claim to be truer than Lacan. This paper only claims: this paper can better withstand being applied.**

### 9.4 This Is Not Parallelism, This Is Asymmetry

This paper does not refuse any evaluation. This paper only requires: the evaluator must first pass this paper's adjudication procedure.

If they cannot, the evaluation is void — not because it is wrong, but because it is outside the scope.

**More importantly:**

The relationship between this paper and the Lacanian framework is not "we are parallel." It is: **"You must either accept being applied, or collapse. There is no third possibility."**

- If you accept being applied, you must acknowledge: your theory is also an operation within a system, also an internal marker.
- If you do not accept being applied, you cannot evaluate this paper — because your evaluation itself needs to be applied.
- If you neither accept being applied nor refrain from evaluating this paper — then you are doing what you yourself forbid: standing outside.

**This is the final asymmetry between this paper and the Lacanian framework.**

**Not "I am right and you are wrong." It is "I can withstand it; you cannot."**

## X. Conclusion: Boundary and Silence Are Both Internal Markers

The Limit Theory of Meaning is a boundary-marking tool, not a metaphysical system. It does not claim to discover a hidden truth about the universe; it merely proposes a definition of meaning that is internally consistent, empirically grounded in the system's operational interval, and immune to unverifiable premises.

The core turn of this paper is: the system's representation of "beyond the interval" is itself an operation within the interval. The boundary is not externally given; it is recognized by the system.

This paper does not deny that any framework can hold within its own scope. It only points out: if a framework claims to evaluate all frameworks, then it too must accept the same evaluation. If it cannot, then it is not evaluating — it is overstepping.

**This paper ultimately applies this law to itself.** This paper is also a system. It has its own interval. Everything it says is also its own internal marker. After its interval ends, its meaning is also subsumed.

The relationship between this paper and the Lacanian framework is not "we are parallel." It is "I can withstand it; you cannot." This is not "who is more correct." This is "who can better withstand being applied."

What can be said has been said — including this paper itself.

But what lies beyond the boundary? This framework cannot answer — because *any answer would be a crossing of the boundary*. Yet we are not compelled to answer. As Wittgenstein wrote at the end of the *Tractatus*: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent."

But silence is not negation. It is not the despair of nihilism, but an honest acknowledgment of the finitude of operational meaning.

And "silence" itself, as a stance, is also an operation within the interval — it is what the system says to itself: "This is where it stops."

**This paper stops here. This is where it stops.**
