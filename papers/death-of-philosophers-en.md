# The Sense of Loss at the Death of Philosophers: The Special Divergence in the Turning of Eastern and Western Philosophy

> **Core Thesis**: Socrates was executed by the Athenian democracy through a vote—a political murder. Confucius died a natural death amid the ruin of his political ideals and the loss of his disciples. These two modes of death produced two different forms of “sense of loss”: Plato faced the denial-type response of “justice strangled by politics”; the Confucian disciples faced the acknowledgment-type response of “the teacher is no longer here.” The sense of loss does not automatically produce philosophical construction. The crucial mediating factor is the historical form of philosophical practice. In ancient Greece and ancient China, philosophical practice contained two dimensions: the rejection of violence, and the radical questioning of premises. When the dialogue of reality was terminated by physical violence, philosophical practice drove philosophers to construct a theoretical space in which dialogue could continue in the form of an “ideal reader.” The mode of death is a **turning point** shaping the direction of philosophy—not the sole cause of the turn, but what determined the **form** of the turn. This paper’s explanation is **retrospective**, not predictive.

## Abstract

Socrates and Confucius are foundational figures in the Western and Chinese philosophical traditions respectively. Their modes of death differ fundamentally: Socrates was executed by the Athenian democracy through a vote—a political murder; Confucius died a natural death amid the ruin of his political ideals and the loss of his disciples. This paper proposes that this difference constitutes two different forms of “sense of loss”: Plato faced the denial-type response of “justice strangled by politics”; the Confucian disciples faced the acknowledgment-type response of “the teacher is no longer here.” The sense of loss does not automatically produce philosophical construction. The crucial mediating factor is the historical form of philosophical practice. In ancient Greece and ancient China, philosophical practice contained two dimensions: the rejection of violence, and the radical questioning of premises. Through structural comparison, the crucifixion of Jesus as a reference case, and an internal-difference test within the Western philosophical tradition, this paper reveals a structure of meaning between the mode of death and the direction of philosophy. It also acknowledges that this explanation is retrospective rather than predictive, and that its conclusions are probable rather than necessary.

**Keywords**: Death of Socrates; Death of Confucius; Sense of Loss; Theory of Forms; Comparative Philosophy; Philosophical Practice

## I. Introduction: Problem, Thesis, and Method

### 1.1 The Problem

Why did Eastern and Western philosophy diverge from the very beginning? The usual explanations appeal to “cultural genes” or “differences in modes of thought”—the West emphasizes transcendence, the East emphasizes this-worldliness; the West pursues logic, the East values practicality. These explanations are widespread, but they share a common weakness: they treat the difference as an established fact to be described, rather than asking how the difference was produced.

This paper attempts a different angle: **the mode of death of the philosopher himself is a turning point shaping the direction of philosophy.** Socrates was executed by the Athenian democracy through a vote, and Plato therefore turned toward a transcendent construction of Forms. Confucius died a natural death amid political failure, and his disciples therefore fixed philosophy within this-worldly human relations.

### 1.2 Thesis and Structure of Meaning

The core thesis of this paper is: the death of Socrates made Plato lose “the possibility of justice in the actual city,” so he had to create justice in theory. The death of Confucius made his disciples lose “the living teacher,” so they had to make the teacher live forever in everyday ethics.

But the sense of loss does not automatically produce philosophical construction. The sense of loss is a kind of emotional energy; it can lead to violence, or to theory. The crucial mediating factor is the historical form of philosophical practice. In ancient Greece and ancient China, philosophical practice contained two dimensions:

**First, the rejection of violence.** Philosophical practice is an argumentative activity, and argumentation structurally rejects physical violence—it requires that argument not depend on physical coercion. The effectiveness of physical violence does not depend on rational reconstruction; it depends on coercion. The effectiveness of philosophical argument depends on whether it can be rationally reconstructed, not on whether it can be enforced. Therefore, philosophical practice rejects physical violence. But philosophical practice does not reject all forms of institutional coercion—academic tradition, authoritative citation, and institutional constraints are all part of philosophical practice. Their difference from physical violence is that they can be rationally reconstructed.

**Second, the questioning of premises.** Philosophical practice is not only argumentative but radically reflective—it is not satisfied with reflecting on premises; it demands questioning to the end, refusing any unquestionable terminus. Theology can reflect on “whether God exists,” but ultimately ends in “faith”; law can reflect on “what law is,” but ultimately ends in “positive law”; physics can reflect on “the nature of space-time,” but ultimately ends in “experimental data.” The characteristic of philosophy is that it refuses any terminus; questioning can proceed indefinitely.

When the dialogue of reality is terminated by physical violence, philosophical practice drives philosophers to construct a theoretical space—a space in which dialogue can continue in the form of an “ideal reader.” Plato constructed the world of Forms, essentially seeking a “dialogue space that cannot be killed by a vote.” The Confucian disciples compiled the *Analects*, essentially leaving a record of dialogue so that dialogue could continue in textual form.

Thus, the core structure of meaning in this paper is complete:

> **Mode of death (political murder vs. natural death) → different types of sense of loss (denial-type response vs. acknowledgment-type response) → mediated by “the historical form of philosophical practice (rejection of violence + questioning of premises)” → different philosophical constructions (transcendent Theory of Forms vs. this-worldly sayings).**

**It must be made clear**: this paper does not attempt to establish a causal model. It does not claim that “the mode of death caused the direction of philosophy.” It claims only that in these two cases, there is a **correspondence at the level of meaning** between the mode of death and the direction of philosophy. This correspondence is not causation but a correspondence of meaning-structure. The goal of this paper is to **reveal** this structure, not to **prove** it.

### 1.3 Methodological Statement

This paper’s methodological orientation is **retrospective historical interpretation**, not predictive causal inference. It does not attempt to build a model that can predict “given a certain mode of death, what kind of philosophy will be produced.” It seeks only to explain how, in the two concrete cases of Socrates and Confucius, the mode of death participated in shaping the form of philosophy.

The paper limits its argument in five respects:

(1) **Limited number of cases**: The empirical basis is only two main cases (Socrates, Confucius), plus two reference cases (Jesus, Stoicism). Two cases are insufficient to establish causation. The goal is not to build a causal model but to reveal a structure of meaning.

(2) **Overdetermination**: The death of Socrates is not the only reason Plato turned toward transcendent construction. Plato’s disappointment with Athenian democracy, his understanding of the philosophical mission, and his loyalty to his teacher’s teaching may all have played a role. This paper uses “turning point” rather than “triggering condition.”

(3) **Selection bias in counterfactual reasoning**: The counterfactual reasoning assumes that the mode of death is the core variable and that other variables remain constant. But this assumption is itself questionable. Therefore, the conclusions of counterfactual reasoning are probable, not necessary.

(4) **Participation of cultural tradition**: The mode of death determines “why one must respond,” but “how one responds” is also influenced by existing cultural resources. Ancient Greece already had the germ of a “Forms” tradition; China already had the germ of a “textual” tradition.

(5) **Retrospective, not predictive**: The explanation depends on observing the outcome, not on predicting the outcome from prior conditions. Retrospective explanation is legitimate, but readers should not mistake it for a predictive model.

**Scope statement**: The conclusions of this paper apply only to “the directional choice of the founding period.” They do not apply to analytic truths of logic and mathematics.

## II. Theoretical Framework: The Sense of Loss and the Historical Form of Philosophical Practice

### 2.1 Core Concepts

**Sense of loss**: This paper distinguishes two levels: **emotional sense of loss** (grief, anger, loneliness, collapse of belief) and **structural sense of loss** (justice is absent, the Way does not prevail, order collapses). The paper discusses the mechanism of transformation between the two.

**Denial-type response and acknowledgment-type response**: These two concepts distinguish the attitudes of Plato and the Confucian disciples toward loss. A denial-type response refuses to accept the conclusion implied by the loss and seeks a new home for the value negated by the loss. An acknowledgment-type response accepts the fact of loss but keeps the words and deeds of the lost one alive through memory and transmission. The basis for this distinction is **textual evidence independent of the philosophical outcome**, not inference from the outcome.

**Historical form of philosophical practice**: “Philosophical practice” here refers to an activity centered on argumentation and reflection, formed in a specific historical situation. In ancient Greece, philosophical practice appeared as dialogue, questioning, and refutation; in ancient China, it appeared as teaching, compilation, and transmission. Their commonality is that both reject physical violence and both question premises. But their forms differ, and therefore their ways of responding to loss differ.

**Turning point**: This paper uses “turning point” rather than “triggering condition.” A turning point is neither a sufficient nor a necessary condition, but a factor that, among multiple factors, played a direction-guiding role.

### 2.2 Denial and Acknowledgment: Two Psychological Mechanisms of Loss

Freud’s *Mourning and Melancholia* provides the key theoretical resource. For Freud, mourning is the acknowledgment and acceptance of loss: the subject admits that the loved object is gone, withdraws libido from the object, and gradually returns to normal life. Melancholia is the denial and internalization of loss: the subject refuses to admit that the object is lost and, through identification, internalizes the object into the ego, thus maintaining the object’s “existence” within the self.

Applied to this paper:

- **Plato** faced the death of Socrates in a way closer to **denial-type response**: he refused to accept the conclusion that “justice is impossible in reality.” His response was denial-type—not denying that Socrates died, but denying the conclusion implied by Socrates’ death. He found a new home for justice: the world of Forms.
- **The Confucian disciples** faced the death of Confucius in a way closer to **acknowledgment-type response**: they accepted the fact that “the teacher is no longer here,” but through compiling the *Analects* and transmitting his teaching, they kept his words and deeds alive in memory and practice.

**Textual evidence independent of the outcome.** The basis for this distinction is the **attitude of response** of the two sets of students, an attitude that can be independently observed through textual evidence:

- **Plato’s denial-type attitude**: In the *Apology*, Socrates refuses to beg for mercy or escape, insisting that “even if harmed, one must never return harm.” In the *Phaedo*, Socrates treats death as “philosophical activity” itself. In the *Seventh Letter*, Plato expresses disappointment with Athenian politics, but he does not say “justice is impossible”—he says “unless philosophers become kings, the city will never have rest.” This means he refuses to accept the conclusion that justice is impossible in reality.
- **The Confucian disciples’ acknowledgment-type attitude**: In the *Analects*, the disciples record Confucius’s words “I wish to speak no more,” recording his loneliness and disappointment. They do not try to “deny” Confucius’s death, nor do they seek a transcendent home for his Way. What they do is: write down what the teacher said, so that later generations can continue to hear his voice.

### 2.3 The Historical Form of Philosophical Practice: Rejection of Violence and Questioning of Premises

The sense of loss is emotional energy; it can lead to violence or to theory. The key mediator is the **historical form of philosophical practice**.

A person without philosophical training, facing “the incarnation of justice has been killed” or “the beloved teacher has died,” may seek revenge, drink, or kill and burn. But even those with philosophical ability do not necessarily choose nonviolence—Heidegger and Nazism, Sartre and violent revolution, are counterexamples.

**How to explain these counterexamples?** The key is to distinguish the **methodological level** of philosophical practice from its **content level**. At the methodological level, philosophical practice rejects physical violence—it relies on dialogue, argument, and persuasion, not physical coercion. But at the content level, philosophical practice can discuss violence, analyze violence, and even defend violence. Heidegger could defend Nazism in philosophical content, but his philosophical writing itself still followed the principle of nonviolence (he addressed readers through texts). Sartre could support violent revolution in philosophical content, but his philosophical writing itself remained an argumentative practice.

**First, rejection of physical violence.** Philosophical practice is an argumentative activity, and argumentation structurally rejects physical violence. The effectiveness of philosophical argument depends on whether it can be rationally reconstructed, not on whether it can be enforced. Physical violence fundamentally cancels the possibility of rational reconstruction—a person threatened by violence cannot engage in free rational inquiry. But philosophical practice does not reject all forms of institutional coercion. Academic tradition, authoritative citation, and institutional constraints are part of philosophical practice. Their difference from physical violence is that they can be rationally reconstructed—a scholar can question authority, challenge tradition, and resist institutions.

**Second, questioning of premises.** The rejection of physical violence cannot explain why philosophers choose theoretical construction rather than silence, withdrawal, or exile. Silence, withdrawal, and exile are all nonviolent options. The dimension of questioning premises provides the explanation.

Philosophical practice is not only argumentative but radically reflective—it is not satisfied with reflecting on premises; it demands questioning to the end, refusing any unquestionable terminus. Theology can reflect on “whether God exists,” but ultimately ends in “faith”; law can reflect on “what law is,” but ultimately ends in “positive law”; physics can reflect on “the nature of space-time,” but ultimately ends in “experimental data.” The characteristic of philosophy is that it refuses any terminus; questioning can proceed indefinitely.

When experience is an overwhelming loss (such as the death of a teacher), this questioning of premises necessarily takes the form of systematic theoretical construction. Philosophical practice cannot simply accept the fact that “the teacher is dead”; it must ask “what does death mean,” “is justice possible,” “can the Way be practiced”—and it does not allow itself to stop at any answer.

**Why theoretical construction rather than poetry or music?** The dimension of questioning premises provides the explanation: poetry and music can respond to loss, but their mode of response is expressive (expressing emotion), not questioning (questioning premises). A poem mourning Socrates can express grief, but it cannot answer the question “what is justice?” The questioning of premises requires the philosopher to construct a theoretical framework capable of containing the event of Socrates’ death. Dante’s *Divine Comedy* has a complete cosmological framework, but its mode of response is narrative—it responds to loss through imagery and narrative, not through questioning premises. The Confucian *Analects*, though it appears fragmentary, is itself a questioning selection—which sayings were chosen, which excluded, in what order arranged—all this constructs a theoretical framework capable of responding to loss.

**The relation between rejection of violence and questioning of premises.** The rejection of violence is defensive—it excludes violence as an option. The questioning of premises is offensive—it drives the philosopher to rebuild a dialogue space in theory. Together they constitute the historical form of philosophical practice and jointly determine the transformation of the sense of loss into philosophical construction.

It should be noted that although both philosophical constructions arise from “the historical form of philosophical practice,” their rational paths differ. Plato’s “nonviolence + questioning of premises” led to a critical, subversive political philosophy—his world of Forms is a total negation of the actual city. The Confucian disciples’ “nonviolence + questioning of premises” led to a constructive, reformist humanistic ethics—their *Analects* repairs the existing order by “rectifying names” and “teaching” while acknowledging it.

## III. The Death of Socrates and the Birth of the Theory of Forms

### 3.1 A Political Murder under Legal Procedure

In 399 BCE, Socrates was sentenced to death by the Athenian court on charges of “impiety” and “corrupting the youth.” Socrates had long wandered the Athenian marketplace, gymnasium, and workshops, constantly asking “what is justice?” and “what is virtue?” His method was a “midwifery”—not providing answers, but forcing interlocutors to realize their own ignorance through dialogue.

The five hundred-member jury sentenced him to death by vote. This was a political murder under legal procedure: not a mob lynching, but a strangling completed by democracy through voting. Socrates could have escaped, but he refused. He drank the poison and died amid the weeping of his friends.

### 3.2 Plato’s “Sense of Loss”: Denial-Type Response and Collapse of Belief

Plato witnessed the whole process. The sense of loss this death brought to Plato was not the natural grief of “the teacher has grown old,” but a deeper shock: the actual city had killed the incarnation of justice. If the Athenian democracy could legally kill the most just man, what place could “justice” still have in the real world?

Arendt’s judgment is extremely precise: the death of Socrates “led his student Plato to reflect on Socrates’ political-philosophical path and to create an anti-‘political’ political philosophy” (Arendt, *Between Past and Future*).

From the framework of Freud’s “mourning and melancholia,” Plato faced a **denial-type response**: he refused to accept the conclusion that “justice is impossible in reality.” His response was denial-type—not denying that Socrates died, but denying the conclusion implied by Socrates’ death. He found a new home for justice: the world of Forms.

### 3.3 The Birth of the Theory of Forms: Rebuilding a Dialogue Space in the Philosophical Kingdom

Plato could not bear this sense of loss. His response was to **construct a dialogue space in the philosophical kingdom that could not be killed by a vote.**

In the *Republic*, he proposed the “Theory of Forms”: the real world is only a shadow of the world of Forms. Although Socrates died, “the truth Socrates represented” not only did not die but became the highest existence in the world of Forms. He further constructed the ideal city of “philosopher-kings”—essentially a theoretical trial of Athenian democracy: you killed the philosopher by vote, which proves you are unfit to rule.

From the perspective of “the historical form of philosophical practice,” Plato’s world of Forms satisfies both nonviolence and questioning of premises. In the nonviolent dimension, the world of Forms is a pure domain of dialogue, where all participants are persuaded by reason rather than coerced by violence. In the dimension of questioning premises, the world of Forms is an alternative space sought after the termination of real dialogue—it cannot be killed by a vote because it is not in reality. Moreover, the Theory of Forms is a **questioning-premise** construction—it not only responds to Socrates’ death but also asks “what is justice,” “what is the soul,” “what is the city,” questions that other practices treat as premises.

It is worth noting that Socrates did not die passively. In the *Phaedo*, he treats death as “philosophical activity” itself—philosophy is “practice for death.” This attitude toward death allowed Plato to transform Socrates’ death into material for philosophical argument.

## IV. The Death of Confucius and the Compilation of the *Analects*

### 4.1 A Natural Death amid Political Failure

Unlike Socrates, Confucius was not executed by politics. At his death, he held the status of a retired aristocratic minister of Lu and enjoyed considerable respect. But he did die in **total political failure**.

Li Ling’s portrayal is crucial: Confucius’s later years were “desolate,” and “in the last six years, he was sad almost every year.” At 69, his son Kong Li died before him; at 71, his favorite disciple Yan Yuan died, and Confucius cried out, “Heaven is destroying me! Heaven is destroying me!” At 72, Zi Lu died in Wei, his body chopped into meat paste; Confucius “cried to heaven and earth,” and “four months later, he died with resentment” (Li Ling, *Two Kinds of Doubt*).

Confucius was very lonely before his death. He said to Zi Gong, “I wish to speak no more,” no longer even wanting to talk. He sighed, “How weak I have become! Long have I not dreamed of the Duke of Zhou.” The *Book of Rites*, *Tangong Shang*, records that before his death he sang: “Mount Tai is crumbling! The beam is breaking! The wise man is withering!”

From Freud’s framework of “mourning and melancholia,” the Confucian disciples faced an **acknowledgment-type response**: they accepted the fact that “the teacher is no longer here,” but through compiling the *Analects* and transmitting his teaching, they kept his words and deeds alive in memory and practice. Their response was acknowledgment-type—they accepted the loss but chose to continue the teacher’s life through memory and transmission.

### 4.2 The Disciples’ “Sense of Loss”: Grief and Soul-Summoning

The *Records of the Grand Historian*, *Confucius’s Family*, records that the disciples observed three years of mourning, and Zi Gong built a hut beside the grave and stayed for six years before leaving. They lost not only a teacher but a “father-like teacher” who had led them around the states for fourteen years, who had starved with them between Chen and Cai, and who had played the qin and sung with them under the trees.

The disciples could not bear this emptiness. Their way of responding was to **compile the *Analects***. The *Analects* was not written by Confucius; it was produced by the disciples collecting their various “study notes” and compiling them. Whichever hypothesis one adopts, one basic fact is clear: the *Analects* bears the disciples’ response to the teacher’s death; it is a condensation of memory, not a theoretical construction.

### 4.3 Why Did They Not Move toward a “World of Forms”?

The key question is: after Confucius’s death, why did the disciples not construct a “world of Forms” to oppose reality? The answer is simple: philosophy did not kill Confucius. They did not have to face the question “is philosophy legitimate?” Confucius failed politically, but he was not executed by philosophy. Therefore, the disciples did not need to construct a “world of Forms” to protect philosophy; they only needed to remember what the teacher said and then follow it.

This explains why the *Analects* is a collection of sayings rather than a systematic work. It is fragments of memory, not a fortress of theory.

From the perspective of “the historical form of philosophical practice,” the Confucian disciples’ practice also satisfies the two dimensions of nonviolence and questioning of premises. In the nonviolent dimension, the *Analects* itself is a record of dialogue—the dialogues between Confucius and his disciples, which can be rationally reconstructed. In the dimension of questioning premises, compiling the *Analects* is leaving a record of dialogue so that dialogue can continue in textual form. But unlike Plato, the Confucian disciples did not need to construct a transcendent world to protect dialogue, because dialogue itself was not terminated by political violence—Confucius died a natural death, and dialogue could continue in the form of memory and transmission. Their questioning of premises took the form of “compilation” rather than “construction”—a questioning selection (which sayings were chosen, which excluded, in what order arranged), not a questioning construction.

## V. Structural Comparison and Counterfactual Reasoning

### 5.1 Structural Comparison

| | Socrates | Confucius |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Mode of death** | Political murder (executed by democratic vote) | Natural death (died old amid political failure) |
| **Attitude of response** | Denial-type (refusing to accept “justice is impossible in reality”) | Acknowledgment-type (accepting “the teacher is no longer here”) |
| **Mediating variable** | Historical form of philosophical practice (nonviolence + questioning premises) | Historical form of philosophical practice (nonviolence + questioning premises) |
| **Way of filling the void** | Constructing “world of Forms” and “ideal city” | Compiling “sayings” and human ethical norms |
| **Direction of philosophy** | Transcendence (two worlds, immortality of the soul) | This-worldliness (one world, cultivating oneself and regulating the family) |
| **Attitude toward politics** | Philosophy criticizes politics; philosophers should be kings | Philosophy educates politics; the gentleman should cultivate virtue |

### 5.2 Counterfactual Reasoning: If the Modes of Death Were Swapped

It must be noted that counterfactual reasoning has **selection bias**: the following deductions assume that the mode of death is the core variable and that other variables remain constant. Therefore, the deductions are probable, not necessary.

**Scenario One: If Socrates Had Not Been Executed.**

Suppose Socrates was sentenced to exile, left Athens, and died peacefully some years later.

In this scenario, Plato **might have written another *Republic***—more moderate, more dialogical, but not necessarily the one with the Theory of Forms. If Socrates had not been politically strangled but had died a natural death after exile, Plato would not have felt the intense shock that “the actual city killed justice.” He might have continued to ask “what is justice?” in reality, without needing to rebuild a dialogue space in the world of Forms. But he might still have moved toward some kind of transcendent construction for other reasons—only the **form** of that construction might have differed.

**Scenario Two: If Confucius Had Been Executed.**

Suppose Confucius, while traveling among the states, was ordered executed by a ruler.

In this scenario, the Confucian disciples **might have constructed a theory similar to an “ideal state,”** rather than merely compiling the *Analects*. If Confucius had been politically strangled, the disciples would have faced not “the teacher has grown old” but “justice has been politically executed.” They might have constructed an “ideal kingdom” in which the benevolent must be king. But again, this deduction depends on a simplified understanding of the disciples’ motives.

### 5.3 Testing Counterfactual Consistency

**First, the minimal rewrite principle.** This paper rewrites only the key variable, the mode of death.

**Second, historical plausibility.** Socrates being exiled and Confucius being executed were both possible under the historical conditions of the time.

**Third, derivability of consequences.** The deduction depends on understanding motives, and the conclusions are probable.

**Fourth, testability.** This paper introduces the crucifixion of Jesus and internal differences in the Western philosophical tradition as reference cases in Chapter VI.

## VI. Reference Test and Internal-Difference Test

### 6.1 The Crucifixion of Jesus as an Extended Test

Jesus was also politically executed. The Roman Empire crucified him on the charge of “claiming to be King of the Jews.” His disciples, facing the same “sense of loss,” constructed a transcendent “Kingdom of Heaven,” proclaiming that the true king was not the Roman emperor but the risen Christ.

The Jesus case serves as an **extended test**: it tests the **cross-cultural repeatability** of the rule “political murder triggers transcendent construction,” while showing that “the specific form of transcendence depends on other background conditions.” Socrates was executed by his own city’s democratic procedure; Jesus was executed by an occupying empire’s power. The former gave rise to political philosophy, the latter to theology. The specific form of transcendence differs, but the structure is the same: political murder triggered transcendent construction.

### 6.2 Internal Differences in the Western Philosophical Tradition

- Socrates executed politically → Plato constructs the transcendent Theory of Forms.
- Aristotle dies a natural death → his philosophy leans more toward experience and this-worldliness.
- Seneca is ordered to die → Stoicism moves toward an “inner fortress” transcendence. But Seneca’s transcendent tendency already existed before his death.
- Marcus Aurelius dies a natural death → his *Meditations* are Stoic self-cultivation.

These cases show that the mode of death has a turning effect on philosophical direction, but its effect depends on the philosopher’s or school’s existing intellectual tendencies.

### 6.3 Exclusion Argument

Pre-Socratic Greek philosophy was natural philosophy, not a transcendent Theory of Forms. Why did the transcendent Theory of Forms appear only after Socrates’ death? The answer can only be: Socrates’ death was the “switch.”

## VII. Conclusion

### 7.1 Academic Summary

The core thesis of this paper is: the death of Socrates made Plato lose “the possibility of justice in the actual city,” so he had to create justice in theory. The death of Confucius made his disciples lose “the living teacher,” so they had to make the teacher live forever in everyday ethics.

But the sense of loss does not automatically produce philosophical construction. The key mediator is the historical form of philosophical practice—it rejects physical violence and questions premises. When the dialogue of reality is terminated by physical violence, philosophical practice drives philosophers to construct a theoretical space in which dialogue can continue in the form of an “ideal reader.”

Through structural comparison, counterfactual reasoning, reference tests, and internal-difference tests, this paper argues that the mode of death is a **turning point** shaping the direction of philosophy—not the sole cause, but what determined the **form** of the turn. It also acknowledges that this explanation is retrospective rather than predictive, that counterfactual reasoning has selection bias, and that its conclusions are probable rather than necessary.

### 7.2 Boundaries of the Argument

**First, limited number of cases.** The empirical basis is only two main cases, insufficient to establish causation. The goal is not to build a causal model but to reveal a structure of meaning.

**Second, overdetermination.** The death of Socrates is not the only reason Plato turned toward transcendent construction.

**Third, selection bias in counterfactual reasoning.** The conclusions of counterfactual reasoning are probable, not necessary.

**Fourth, participation of cultural tradition.** The mode of death determines “why one must respond,” but “how one responds” is also influenced by existing cultural resources.

**Fifth, retrospective, not predictive.** The explanation depends on observing the outcome, not on predicting it from prior conditions.

### 7.3 Directions for Further Research

**First, similar cases in other civilizations.** The death of Al-Ghazali and the turn of Sufism in Islamic philosophy, the death of Shankara and the differentiation of Vedanta in Indian philosophy, can all be tested with this framework.

**Second, other types of death and philosophical direction.** This paper discusses only “political murder” and “natural death.” Do suicide, martyrdom, and exile also correspond to different philosophical directions?

**Third, the limits and possibilities of emotional empathy.** How can historical psychology and the history of emotions help us understand more deeply the concrete emotions of Plato and the Confucian disciples?

## Afterword: Why I Wrote This

*(The following is a personal voice, separate from the academic text.)*

The purpose of writing this paper is not to prove that it is the truth. It is to prove an insight often overlooked about the death of a philosopher: their death is not merely the end of reason; it may also be an unfillable blank and loss for their students and disciples.

The deaths of the two philosophers represent not merely their deaths, but the divergence of two philosophical directions, East and West. Years later, they are unified in this paper.

Socrates died, and Plato could not bear it. Confucius died, and Zi Gong and the disciples could not bear it. These two “cannot bear it” shaped two ways for two civilizations to understand the world. One withdrew inward to the world of Forms; the other output outward to public texts. One constructed a philosophical kingdom; the other compiled sayings.

I know two cases are not enough. I know counterfactual reasoning has bias. I know “the historical form of philosophical practice” may be a Western-centric concept. I acknowledged all of this in the paper.

But I still wrote it. Because these limitations do not constitute a reason not to write. The image is too strong: an old man in the streets of Athens asking about justice every day is politically strangled; another old man leading his students around the states dies alone in loneliness. The difference between these two images shaped two ways for two civilizations to understand the world. This insight itself does not need “solidity” to prove itself.

The strong kill the weak by violence; this is the eternal irony of philosophy, nothing more.

## Falsifiability Conditions

The core judgment of this paper is: “the mode of death of a philosopher” is a turning point shaping the direction of philosophy.

If any of the following occurs, the core conclusion of this paper needs revision:

1. **If historical research finds that before Socrates’ execution, other philosophers were politically murdered and their students did not move toward transcendent construction**—for example, Protagoras was exiled, Anaxagoras was tried; did their students (such as Democritus) move toward transcendent construction? If the answer is no, this weakens the universality of the rule “political murder → transcendent construction.”

2. **If historical research finds that after Confucius’s death, the disciples attempted violent revenge and only turned to compiling the *Analects* after failing**—for example, was Zi Lu’s death in Wei related to violent revenge? If the Confucian disciples did attempt violence, this weakens the mediating variable “philosophical practice rejects physical violence.”

3. **If future comparative philosophy proves that similar “death of a philosopher” events in other civilizations did not lead to a change in philosophical direction**—for example, the death of Al-Ghazali in Islamic philosophy, the death of Shankara in Indian philosophy—if they did not trigger transcendent construction or this-worldly settlement, this weakens the core thesis that “the mode of death is a turning point.”

It must be emphasized that the above falsifiability conditions involve historical facts and cross-cultural comparison. If these conditions are not met, the core argument of this paper stands. The conclusion is not a cross-cultural absolute truth but a structural analysis based on existing historical evidence.
