## I. Abstract

This paper is the third in the author's "Fiscal-Institutional Trilogy." The first paper, *Strong State, Tired People*, established a five-dimensional fiscal transmission framework (security, geography, external rules, demography, livelihood), diagnosing the structural causes of China's "tension." The second paper, *Resilience in Tension*, examined the resilience performance of internally absorbed growth under external shocks, and reported a falsifying finding: there is no statistically significant correlation between institutional elasticity and relaxation decline (R²=0.003, t=-0.19).

Building on this, the present paper uses the five-dimensional framework to conduct a structural comparison between China (internally absorbed) and the United States (mixed). The Soviet Union and North Korea serve as historical references for the "non-adjustable system," providing boundary markers for the typological distinction.

**The core judgment of this paper is**: both China and the United States are located in the high-range of the five-dimensional structure, but face structural constraints of different natures. China's structural constraint comes from the five-dimensional rigidity of the internally absorbed path; America's structural constraint comes from the political polarization of the mixed path. Both systems have reached the high point of their respective paths under current structural constraints, and external pressure is unlikely to force structural transformation on either side.

This paper further proposes a judgment worthy of future research: **China has already come very close to the United States in surface-level economic indicators, but its underlying operating mechanism still follows the structural logic of "internally absorbed growth."** This "data-proximate, skeleton-different" structural position may be the analytical starting point for understanding America's strategic misjudgment of China.

## II. Introduction: The Endgame of the Trilogy

This is the third paper in the author's "Fiscal-Institutional Trilogy."

The first paper, *Strong State, Tired People* (Zhong, 2026a), established a three-stage transmission path—"upstream rigid expenditures → midstream livelihood squeeze → downstream tension"—and proposed the five-dimensional fiscal transmission framework. Its core contribution lies in revealing the transmission dilemma of "internally absorbed growth."

The second paper, *Resilience in Tension* (Zhong, 2026b), examined the resilience performance of internally absorbed growth under external shocks based on the five-dimensional framework. Its core finding is that the internal absorption index can explain approximately 80% of the variation in relaxation decline (R²=0.805, t=7.34). **However, the paper also reported a falsifying finding**: there is no statistically significant correlation between institutional elasticity and relaxation decline (R²=0.003, t=-0.19).

The first two papers completed the "internal diagnosis of China" and the "shock resilience test." Building on this, the present paper raises a more fundamental question: **Can the five-dimensional framework provide a useful analytical tool for understanding China-US structural differences?**

**The positioning of this paper**: This is a **structural tendency study**, not an empirical paper. Its purpose is to propose a conceptual framework and identify directions for future research. It does not conduct causal inference, nor does it attempt to prove any hypothesis. The causal statements herein should be understood as "structural tendencies," not cross-temporal deterministic laws.

**This paper does not answer the following questions**: What is the ultimate outcome of China-US structural competition? Can the causes of the Soviet collapse be reduced to structural factors? These questions are left for future research.

## III. Literature Positioning: From Five-Dimensional Framework to Structural Comparison

### 3.1 The Explanatory Boundaries of Existing Theories

On the relationship between "state capacity" and "people's welfare," existing research is mainly distributed across the following strands:

**The development economics tradition** (Kuznets, 1955; Acemoglu & Robinson, 2012) argues that economic growth will ultimately benefit people's welfare through various mechanisms. Its blind spot is that it ignores the political process of fiscal allocation.

**Welfare state theory** (Esping-Andersen, 1990) emphasizes the mediating role of distribution systems, but its analysis focuses on mature Western welfare states.

**New institutionalism** (North, 1990; Rodrik, 1999) focuses on the impact of institutional quality on economic development, but its analysis centers on the typological distinction of institutions themselves.

**Great power competition theory** (Mearsheimer, 2014; Allison, 2017) focuses on the structural logic of great power conflict, but rarely asks: does the **form** of conflict depend on the fiscal-structural position of both sides?

### 3.2 Contributions and Limitations of the Five-Dimensional Framework

The five-dimensional fiscal transmission framework previously proposed by the author (Zhong, 2026a) decomposes "rigid costs" into five measurable dimensions—net security cost, net geographical connection burden, net external rule gap, demographic dependency pressure, and livelihood-oriented fiscal expenditure share—explaining why China's transmission dilemma has the structural characteristics of a "five-fold lock-in."

**In the second paper, the author attempted to introduce "institutional elasticity" as a sixth dimension**, expecting it might be the "boundary condition for the conversion of internal absorption into resilience." However, regression results from 15-country data show that there is no statistically significant correlation between institutional elasticity and relaxation decline (R²=0.003, t=-0.19). **This falsifying finding indicates that the introduction of the sixth dimension lacks empirical support. Therefore, this paper returns to the five-dimensional framework.**

### 3.3 Dialogue Space with American Strategic Literature

- **Dialogue space with Mearsheimer (2014)**: Offensive realism argues that great power conflict is structural. This paper's five-dimensional framework may offer a complementary perspective: the **form** of conflict may depend on the structural position of both sides. Does the structural difference between China and the Soviet Union mean that the United States cannot use the same strategy against two different opponents?
- **Dialogue space with Allison (2017)**: The Thucydides Trap argues that conflict between a ruling power and a rising power is difficult to avoid. This paper's framework may offer a complementary perspective: whether conflict is "difficult to avoid" may depend on whether both sides misjudge each other's structural position.
- **Dialogue space with Fukuyama (1992, 2018)**: Fukuyama's "End of History" thesis was proposed in 1992, but he made a substantive revision in his 2018 book *Identity*. This paper's framework may resonate with Fukuyama's later revision: convergence in institutional form (surface) may not equal convergence in institutional substance (underlying).

## IV. Research Methods

### 4.1 Case Selection

This paper adopts an **extreme case comparison strategy** (Flyvbjerg, 2006; Gerring, 2007), identifying common structural mechanisms in the maximum variation of the four countries' five-dimensional structures.

The selection of the four cases is based on the following logic:

- **China**: A typical representative of the internally absorbed type. The first five dimensions are extremely rigid (full security self-reliance, geographical peak period, external rule cliff, accelerating dependency ratio).
- **United States**: A typical representative of the mixed type. It possesses both internally absorbed capabilities (energy self-sufficiency, food self-sufficiency, monetary autonomy) and externally shared channels (dollar system, security alliances).
- **Soviet Union (historical)**: A historical case of the non-adjustable system. It disintegrated under external pressure.
- **North Korea (contemporary)**: A contemporary case of the non-adjustable system. It continues to shrink rather than demonstrate resilience under external pressure.

**It should be noted**: Data from the Soviet Union and North Korea are unreliable and should not be used to support any empirical proposition. This paper uses them only as "historical references" and "boundary markers," not as core pillars of argumentation.

### 4.2 Analytical Framework

This paper's five-dimensional fiscal-institutional framework consists of five dimensions:

1. **Net security cost**: military spending as % of GDP, net of external sharing
2. **Net geographical connection burden**: per capita annual infrastructure investment net of weighted depreciation
3. **Net external rule gap**: the net difference between globalization dividend period and barrier period
4. **Demographic dependency pressure**: total dependency ratio (2024 cross-section + 2035 projection)
5. **Livelihood-oriented fiscal expenditure share**: education, healthcare, social security, housing security as % of fiscal expenditure

### 4.3 Operational Definitions of Core Concepts

**(1) "Hardened State"**

The "hardened state" referred to in this paper means that a state still retains full behavioral capacity (industrial output, technological R&D, military projection, global supply chain control have not significantly declined), but selective reception and systematic shielding of external signals may cause its behavior to no longer follow externally identifiable decision-making logic.

**(2) "Adjustable System" and "Non-Adjustable System"**

This paper uses "adjustable system" to refer to countries whose scores in all five dimensions are above the threshold (China, United States), and "non-adjustable system" to refer to countries whose scores in the two core dimensions of net security cost and external rule gap are below the threshold (Soviet Union, North Korea). **It should be noted**: Since institutional elasticity has been falsified by the empirical test in the second paper, this paper no longer uses institutional elasticity as a classification criterion. The classification criteria are changed to the two dimensions of net security cost and external rule gap in the five-dimensional structure.

### 4.4 Time Window

The analytical window of this paper is **2019-2024**, covering three external shocks. Historical data for the Soviet Union uses the **1980s** as the reference cross-section.

### 4.6 Methodological Limitations

This paper has the following clear limitations:

(1) **Sample boundary**: The conclusions of this paper are based on a comparison of four cases, and the validity of extrapolation to other countries is limited;

(2) **Data boundary**: Historical data for North Korea and the Soviet Union are constrained by statistical opacity, with uncertainty intervals;

(3) **Time boundary**: The analytical window of this paper is 2019-2024, and the conclusions do not claim validity beyond that period;

(4) **Shock type boundary**: The analysis of this paper applies to external-origin shocks, not internal-origin shocks;

(5) **Causal identification boundary**: The data in this paper show only correlational relationships and cannot identify causal direction.

This means that China's distance from the United States in the five-dimensional data structure may be much smaller than China's distance from the Soviet Union. This is an observation worthy of in-depth future research; this paper does not treat it as a proven conclusion.

## VI. Structural Spectrum: Typological Differences Among North Korea, the Soviet Union, and China

### 6.1 Historical Sources of American Strategic Logic

The underlying logic of America's China strategy is deeply influenced by its Cold War experience. During the four-decade Cold War, the United States exhausted the Soviet economy through an arms race, undermined Soviet politics through ideological penetration, and severed the Soviet Union's ties to global markets through economic blockade—ultimately, the Soviet Union disintegrated (Gaddis, 2005; Zubok, 2007).

This historical experience has formed a deep-seated belief in American strategic circles: sustained pressure will ultimately lead to the internal collapse of an opponent (Mearsheimer, 2014). This belief found its most systematic expression in Fukuyama's (1992) "End of History" thesis.

This strategic logic is explicitly stated in the 2017 and 2022 National Security Strategy reports (The White House, 2017, 2022), which define China as a "strategic competitor" and "the greatest challenge to the international order," continuing the basic framework of "long-term competition + institutional pressure" from the Cold War era.

However, the applicability of this belief depends on three key premises—and China may not satisfy any of them. These three premises are: (1) the opponent's economy is closed, so pressure will not backfire on the pressurer; (2) the opponent's military spending is extremely high, so an arms race can exhaust the opponent's economy; (3) the opponent's structural elasticity is zero, making structural adjustment impossible.

**It should be noted**: This paper does not take a single-cause explanatory stance on the multiple causes of the Soviet collapse. Economic stagnation, ideological fatigue, the Afghan War, ethnic tensions, and Gorbachev's reform missteps (Zubok, 2007; Kotkin, 2017) are all important factors. This paper's analysis focuses on the structural role of the five-dimensional structure in the fiscal-institutional framework.

### 6.2 Structural Positioning of the Three Reference Cases

North Korea, the Soviet Union, and China may constitute a complete structural spectrum:

| Dimension | North Korea | USSR (1980s) | China (Current) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Global embeddedness | Nearly zero | Extremely low (trade 8.8-15%) | **Extremely high (trade 37%)** |
| State behavioral capacity | Low | Medium (military strong, economy weak) | **Extremely high (full-spectrum power)** |
| Recognition of US-led order | Complete non-recognition | Complete non-recognition | **Partial recognition (bargaining within the system)** |
| Nuclear weapons | Yes, limited delivery | Yes, massive | **Yes, modernized and reliable** |
| Predictability | Extremely low | Medium (derivable from ideological logic) | **High (derivable from national interest logic)** |

### 6.3 An Opponent Type Worth Studying

North Korea is a "nuisance" to the United States—small, economically weak, militarily limited.

The Soviet Union was a "danger" to the United States—economically closed, institutionally rigid, exhaustible. The United States ultimately won, but it took 40 years (Gaddis, 2005).

If China enters a "hardened state"—it may be an opponent type worth studying: not the North Korean type of "weak but unpredictable," not the Soviet type of "strong but exhaustible"—but a "strong, large, behaviorally capable system that may no longer receive external signal input." Whether this judgment holds requires future research to test.

## VII. Research Agenda

This paper proposes the following research agenda for future research:

### 7.1 Application of the Five-Dimensional Framework to Other Countries

**Question**: Is the five-dimensional framework applicable to other countries? For example, India, Brazil, Turkey, Saudi Arabia?

**Method**: Collect data on these countries across the five dimensions, conduct standardization and cluster analysis. Test whether they belong to the "adjustable system" or "non-adjustable system," and their structural distance from China and the United States.

**Expected contribution**: Test the universality of the five-dimensional framework.

### 7.2 Link-by-Link Testing of the Transmission Chain

**Question**: How does the five-dimensional structure produce specific external shock response patterns through the fiscal-institutional transmission mechanism?

**Method**: Conduct empirical testing of each link in the five-dimensional framework:

- Net security cost → fiscal squeeze
- Geographic net burden → fiscal squeeze
- External rule gap → fiscal squeeze
- Demographic dependency → fiscal squeeze
- Livelihood expenditure share → micro-level relaxation

**Expected contribution**: Provide link-by-link evidence for the transmission mechanism of the five-dimensional framework.

### 7.3 Counterfactual Analysis

**Question**: If the United States adopts different China strategies (full cooperation, mixed strategy, maximum pressure), how would China's structural position change?

**Method**: Construct a dynamic model containing US strategy variables and China's five-dimensional structural variables, and conduct scenario simulations.

**Expected contribution**: Provide quantitative assessment of the "causal effects of US strategy."

### 7.4 Further Testing of the Institutional Elasticity Hypothesis

**Question**: The second paper falsified the hypothesis that "institutional elasticity is the boundary condition for the conversion of internal absorption into resilience." But could this falsifying finding be due to measurement problems?

**Method**: Try using other institutional quality indicators (such as V-Dem, Polity V, World Bank Ease of Doing Business Index) to replace WGI, and re-test the relationship between institutional elasticity and resilience.

**Expected contribution**: Provide a more comprehensive test of the institutional elasticity hypothesis.

### 7.5 Empirical Testing of "Has China Already Become the United States?"

**Question**: This paper proposes a judgment worthy of future research—China has already come very close to the United States in surface-level economic indicators, but its underlying operating mechanism still follows the structural logic of "internally absorbed growth." Can this judgment be empirically tested?

**Method**: Construct a two-tier indicator system containing "surface indicators" (GDP per capita, urbanization rate, trade dependence) and "underlying indicators" (internal absorption index, security self-reliance, external rule dependence), and test China's relative position on these two tiers.

**Expected contribution**: Provide an empirical basis for the "data-proximate, skeleton-different" judgment.

### Priority Ranking of the Research Agenda

Among the five directions above, this paper recommends prioritizing **Direction 4 (Further Testing of the Institutional Elasticity Hypothesis)** and **Direction 5 (Empirical Testing of "Has China Already Become the United States?")** . The reason is that these two directions directly respond to the core questions of this paper and the two preceding papers.

## VIII. Conclusion

This paper is the third in the "Fiscal-Institutional Trilogy," a structural tendency study, not a completed empirical paper.

**The core question this paper raises is**: Can the five-dimensional framework provide a useful analytical tool for understanding China-US structural differences?

This paper's answer is: The five-dimensional framework may provide a useful analytical tool. China's distance from the United States in the five-dimensional structure may be much smaller than China's distance from the Soviet Union. This suggests a judgment worthy of future research: America's use of the "Soviet framework" to understand China may be the root of its strategic misjudgment.

**The core judgments of this paper are**:

1. **The typological distinction between internally absorbed and mixed provides an analytical framework for understanding China-US structural differences.** China is internally absorbed, the United States is mixed. Their structural positions differ, but both have reached the high point of their respective paths.

2. **China has already come very close to the United States in surface-level economic indicators, but its underlying operating mechanism still follows the structural logic of "internally absorbed growth."** This "data-proximate, skeleton-different" structural position may be the analytical starting point for understanding America's strategic misjudgment of China.

3. **External maximum pressure is unlikely to transform China from internally absorbed to mixed.** The possible direction of transformation is a "hardened state"—reducing responsiveness to external signals and accelerating the construction of a parallel system.

**This paper does not claim that these judgments are causal; it claims only that they are structural, tendential, and exploratory.**

This paper's framework does not presuppose "Chinese exceptionalism." The classification of internally absorbed/externally shared is based on cross-national comparisons of fiscal structure, not on attributes specific to any particular country.

**This paper does not answer the following questions**:

1. What is the ultimate outcome of China-US structural competition?
2. Can the causes of the Soviet collapse be reduced to structural factors?
3. Is "Has China already become the United States?" a testable proposition?
4. Can the "hardened state" be predicted and verified?
5. Is the five-dimensional framework applicable to other countries?

These questions are left for future research.

## IX. Research Boundaries and Falsification Conditions

This paper is a structural tendency study; therefore, its "falsification conditions" are not traditional statistical tests, but **the applicable boundaries of the narrative**.

**This paper's analytical framework applies to the following conditions**:
- External-origin shocks (not internal-origin shocks);
- Countries with a certain degree of structural stability;
- The specific historical window of 2019-2024.

**This paper's analytical framework does not apply to the following conditions**:
- Internal-origin shocks (such as real estate debt crises, local fiscal crises);
- Extremely structurally fragile countries;
- Technological conditions after 2024.

**If any of the following conditions occur, this paper's analytical framework needs revision**:

1. **If China experiences an external shock between 2025 and 2030, and its relaxation decline exceeds that of the United States in the same shock, then this paper's judgment that "the internally absorbed structure is more stable" needs revision.**

2. **If, after controlling for competitive variables such as GDP per capita, foreign trade dependence, capital account openness, and country size, China's five-dimensional structural distance from the United States is no longer significantly smaller than its distance from the Soviet Union, then this paper's "data-proximate, skeleton-different" judgment needs revision.**

3. **If future research finds a better analytical tool than the five-dimensional framework that more accurately explains China-US structural differences, then this paper's framework needs to be replaced.**

4. **Scope of application statement**: This paper's framework applies to "external-origin shocks" and does not apply to "internal-origin shocks." This is a boundary of the framework, not a flaw.
