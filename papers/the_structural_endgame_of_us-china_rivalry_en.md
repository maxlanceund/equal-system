## Abstract

Most analyses of U.S.-China competition focus on surface-level indicators: trade balances, military spending, technological decoupling, and alliance politics. This paper argues that these indicators miss the foundational question: What is the actual structural relationship between the two countries?

Building on the author's previous work—which established a five-dimensional fiscal transmission framework (security costs, geographic connectivity, external institutional gap, demographic dependency, and welfare expenditure)—this paper introduces a sixth dimension: **institutional elasticity**, defined as a state's capacity to adjust its economic structure, fiscal allocation, industrial policy, and external openness without altering its fundamental institutional framework.

Using this six-dimensional framework, the paper compares China ("internal-absorption type") with the United States ("hybrid type"), using the Soviet Union and North Korea as reference cases for "zero-elasticity systems."

**Key findings:**
- China and the U.S. cluster together at the high end of the six-dimensional spectrum (all dimensions above 40/100), while the Soviet Union and North Korea cluster at the low end—the former are "adjustable systems," the latter "non-adjustable systems."
- China's internal-absorption structure (institutional elasticity score: 62) provides exceptional shock resilience (a mere 1.5% decline in its "relaxation index" during 2020-2024 external shocks), but it sits at the lower boundary of the adjustable range. A continued decline below the critical threshold (≈40) would not cause collapse—it would cause **hardening**: a system with full operational capacity but no longer responsive to external (particularly U.S.) signals.
- The U.S. hybrid structure (institutional elasticity score: 88.5) enables self-repair after shocks (78% recovery), but faces political polarization that erodes its elasticity conversion efficiency.

**Core judgment:**
China is not the Soviet Union. It is, in structural terms, "another America"—but built on a different skeleton. The U.S. has spent three decades trying to make China become America, without realizing that China has already become America in surface indicators—it simply has different bones. The structural endgame is not about who wins, but about whether both sides can recognize that the game itself no longer has meaning.

**Keywords**: U.S.-China Structural Endgame; Six-Dimensional Framework; Internal-Absorption Type; Hybrid Type; Institutional Elasticity; Dual Warning

## Introduction: The Endgame of a Trilogy

This is the third and final paper in the author's "fiscal-institutional trilogy."

The first paper, *National Strength and Citizen Ease: A Comparative Analysis Through Fiscal Intermediation and Typology* (Zhong, 2026a), established a five-dimensional fiscal transmission framework (security, geography, external rules, population, welfare) and diagnosed the structural causes of China's "tiredness." Its core contribution was revealing the transmission dilemma of "internal-absorption growth"—five-dimensional rigidity costs cannot be externalized and must be absorbed internally through compressed welfare expenditure.

The second paper, *Tension as Resilience: A Cross-National Comparison of Shock Resistance Through a Five-Dimensional Fiscal Transmission Framework* (Zhong, 2026b), introduced "institutional elasticity" as a boundary condition, demonstrating that in internal-absorption type countries with moderate institutional elasticity, tension manifests as resilience under external shocks. North Korea, as an extreme case of "internal-lock type," marked the left boundary of the framework—when institutional elasticity is absent, tension degenerates into rigidity.

What the first two papers accomplished was a "diagnosis of China." This paper asks a more fundamental question: **Is institutional elasticity a "boundary condition" or a "core dimension"?**

This paper's answer is: institutional elasticity is the variable that animates the five-dimensional rigidity structure. The five-dimensional framework describes structural constraints; institutional elasticity describes the system's capacity to adjust to those constraints—together, they form a complete analytical framework.

This paper's core task is: **to examine both China and the United States from the height of the six-dimensional framework—and deliver a dual structural judgment.**

The analysis shows: both countries have reached the upper limits of their respective structural paths under current constraints. China on the "internal-absorption" path—full security self-reliance, a self-contained geographic system, internalized external rules, ultra-high savings (44.5%), and low-relaxation normalization—has approached the upper bound of its current structural constraints. The United States on the "hybrid" path—energy self-sufficiency, food self-sufficiency, monetary autonomy, security alliance cost-sharing, and the world's highest institutional elasticity (88.5)—has also approached its structural upper bound. Neither side can, in structural terms, transform the other into its own image.

The United States has exhausted its efforts trying to make China become America. What it has not realized is that China has already become America—only with different bones. China has drawn close to the U.S. on surface indicators, but its underlying skeleton remains internal-absorption type.

## Literature Review: From Five to Six Dimensions

### 3.1 The Explanatory Limits of Existing Theories

Existing research on the relationship between "state capacity" and "citizen well-being" is distributed across the following intellectual traditions:

**The Development Economics Tradition** (Kuznets, 1955; Acemoglu & Robinson, 2012; Stiglitz, 2012) holds that economic growth ultimately benefits citizens through various mechanisms. Its blind spot lies in neglecting the political process of fiscal allocation—fiscal increments from growth do not necessarily flow to welfare; they may be absorbed by security, infrastructure, or administrative costs.

**The Welfare State Tradition** (Esping-Andersen, 1990; Bonoli, 1997; Hemerijck, 2013) emphasizes the mediating role of distributional institutions, but its analytical focus is on mature Western welfare states, with limited explanatory power for "fiscal compression under security and integration pressures in late-developing major powers."

**The New Institutionalism Tradition** (North, 1990; Rodrik, 1999; Acemoglu & Robinson, 2012) focuses on the impact of institutional quality on economic development, but its analysis centers on typological distinctions of institutions, with less attention to "how institutional elasticity operates differently under varying rigidity-cost structures."

### 3.2 Contributions and Limits of the Five-Dimensional Framework

The five-dimensional fiscal transmission framework proposed by the author (Zhong, 2026a) decomposes "rigidity costs" into five measurable dimensions—security costs, geographic connectivity burden, external institutional gap, demographic dependency pressure, and welfare expenditure share—explaining why China's transmission dilemma exhibits a "five-fold lock" structural characteristic (Zhong, 2026a, Chapter VI).

However, the explanatory power of the five-dimensional framework stops at "cost description." It can explain why "China is more tired than Germany," but it cannot explain "why North Korea, equally tired, performs completely differently." It can explain that "the U.S. is a hybrid type," but it cannot explain "why the U.S., with high institutional elasticity, only achieved 78% recovery in 2024."

### 3.3 The Sixth Dimension and This Paper's Contribution

This paper proposes adding a sixth dimension—**"institutional elasticity"** —defined as: a state's capacity to proactively adjust its economic structure, fiscal allocation, industrial policy, and external openness without altering its fundamental institutional framework (operationalized in Section 4.3).

The theoretical foundation of the sixth dimension draws from North's (1990) theory of institutional change, Rodrik's (1999) discussion of institutional quality and growth resilience, and Acemoglu & Robinson's (2012) typology of inclusive versus extractive institutions.

This paper operationalizes institutional elasticity as a quantifiable continuous variable (0-100 scale), complementing existing static typologies of institutional types, and incorporates it into the same analytical model as fiscal rigidity variables.

## Methodology

### 4.1 Case Selection

This paper adopts an **extreme case comparison strategy** (Flyvbjerg, 2006; Gerring, 2007), identifying common structural mechanisms through maximizing variation in six-dimensional structures across four countries.

Case selection logic:

- **China**: The archetypal "internal-absorption type." Its first five dimensions exhibit extremely high rigidity (full security self-reliance, geographic peak period, external rule precipitous drop, accelerating dependency ratio), with moderate institutional elasticity (62 points).
- **United States**: The archetypal "hybrid type." It possesses both internal-absorption capacity (energy, food, monetary autonomy) and external cost-sharing channels (dollar system, security alliances), with extremely high institutional elasticity (88.5 points).
- **Soviet Union (historical)**: A historical case of "zero-elasticity hard-resistance type." With near-zero institutional elasticity, it collapsed under external pressure, providing a historical reference for "non-adjustable systems."
- **North Korea (contemporary)**: A contemporary validation of "zero-elasticity locked type." With near-zero institutional elasticity, it exhibits persistent atrophy rather than resilience under external pressure, providing a contemporary reference for "non-adjustable systems."

### 4.2 Analytical Framework and Core Hypotheses

This paper's six-dimensional fiscal-institutional framework comprises six dimensions:

**First five dimensions (rigidity dimensions)** :
1. Net security cost: military expenditure as a share of GDP, net of external cost-sharing
2. Geographic connectivity burden: per capita annual infrastructure investment net of 30-50 year weighted depreciation
3. External institutional gap: net difference between the globalization dividend period and the current barrier period
4. Demographic dependency pressure: total dependency ratio (2024 cross-section + 2035 projection)
5. Welfare expenditure share: education, healthcare, social security, and housing expenditure as a share of total fiscal expenditure

**Sixth dimension (adjustment dimension)** :
6. Institutional elasticity: a composite index of policy adjustment speed, policy implementation deviation, and institutional openness (synthesis method in Appendix A)

This paper's core hypotheses:

**H1 (Threshold Hypothesis)** : Institutional elasticity has a structural threshold (approximately 40 points). Above this threshold, five-dimensional rigidity costs can be converted into system resilience through institutional adjustment; below this threshold, the adjustment mechanism fails, and the system regresses from "adjustable" to "non-adjustable" (see Section 5.3 for cluster analysis).

**H2 (Type Difference Hypothesis)** : China (62 points) and the United States (88.5 points) are both "adjustable systems" but face different types of structural risks—China's risk is elasticity decline leading to system hardening, while America's risk is political polarization eroding elasticity conversion efficiency.

### 4.3 Operational Definitions of Core Concepts

This paper involves three core concepts requiring definition:

**(1) "Hardening"**

"Hardening" refers to a state in which institutional elasticity falls below 40 points, yet the country retains full operational capacity (industrial output, R&D, military projection, global supply chain control show no significant decline), but selective reception and systematic screening of external signals (especially from the U.S.) cause its behavior to no longer follow externally recognizable decision-making logic—neither predictable nor negotiable.

**(2) Metaphors for Structural Position**

This paper uses "crouching" to metaphorically describe the internal-absorption type's high-rigidity, low-relaxation equilibrium—a system in "low-altitude stability" due to the absence of external cost-shifting channels; and "standing" to metaphorically describe the hybrid type's high-elasticity, high-relaxation equilibrium—a system in "high-altitude dynamics" due to multiple internal and external adjustment channels. These are intuitive metaphors for structural position, not formal academic terms.

**(3) "Adjustable Systems" vs. "Non-Adjustable Systems"**

"Adjustable systems" refer to countries where all dimensions score above 40 points (China, United States). "Non-adjustable systems" refer to countries scoring below 40 on the three core dimensions of institutional elasticity, security costs, and external rules (Soviet Union, North Korea). The operational basis for this classification is provided in Section 5.3.

### 4.4 Time Window

This paper's analytical window is **2019-2024**, covering three external shocks (2020 supply chain disruption, 2022 energy crisis, 2023-2024 global interest rate hike cycle). Soviet historical data are referenced to the **1980s**.

### 4.6 Analytical Method

This paper combines **typological comparison with mechanism analysis** (Gerring, 2007; George & Bennett, 2005). The six-dimensional framework is a "structural diagnostic tool"—its function is to locate a country's structural position and trace how that structure produces specific behavioral tendencies through fiscal-institutional transmission mechanisms.

The qualitative analysis follows **process tracing** logic (Beach & Pedersen, 2013)—tracing how six-dimensional structures produce specific external shock response patterns through fiscal-institutional transmission mechanisms.

Analytical procedure: (1) collect raw data for each country on six dimensions (Section 5.1); (2) standardize raw data to 0-100 scale (Section 5.2); (3) identify natural clustering of "high group" vs. "low group" through cluster analysis (Section 5.3); (4) juxtapose the four countries (Section 5.4); (5) project the effects of institutional elasticity change on system behavior (Chapters VI-VIII).

### 4.7 Methodological Limitations

This paper has the following explicit limitations:

(1) **Sample boundary**: Conclusions are based on four cases; generalizability to other countries is limited.

(2) **Data boundary**: The institutional elasticity index is semi-quantitative; weighting choices (0.4/0.3/0.3) involve some subjectivity; North Korean and Soviet historical data are limited by statistical opacity, with uncertainty ranges noted.

(3) **Temporal boundary**: The analytical window is 2019-2024; claims about longer time horizons are not made; projections beyond 2035 are scenario-based.

(4) **Shock type boundary**: The analysis applies to external input shocks; it does not apply to internally generated shocks.

(5) **Technological boundary**: The analysis is based on current technological conditions; if general-purpose technologies significantly increase total factor productivity in the next decade, the framework would require recalibration.

### 5.3 Empirical Basis for the "40-Point Threshold"

The "40-point threshold" proposed in this paper is based on three lines of evidence:

**Evidence 1: Natural clustering from cluster analysis**

K-means cluster analysis (k=2) on the four cases across six dimensions shows: China and the United States cluster as the "high group," the Soviet Union and North Korea as the "low group." The dividing point on institutional elasticity is 38.75 (rounded to 40); on net security cost, 45; on external rules gap, 42.5. All three core dimensions cluster around 40, supporting the "40-point threshold" as an empirical observation.

**Evidence 2: Rodrik's (1999) threshold discussion**

Rodrik (1999), analyzing panel data from 78 developing countries (1960-1990), found that institutional quality has a "non-linear effect"—below a certain critical value, external shocks significantly amplify negative growth effects; above it, institutions dramatically increase shock absorption capacity. Rodrik's critical value, standardized, is approximately 40 points on a 0-100 scale. The "40-point threshold" in this paper aligns with Rodrik's (1999) empirical findings in magnitude.

**Evidence 3: Sensitivity analysis**

Shifting the "40-point threshold" up or down by 5 points (35 or 45) does not affect this paper's classification—China and the U.S. remain in the high group (all dimensions >60), and the Soviet Union and North Korea remain in the low group (all dimensions <55). The paper's conclusions are not sensitive to the precise threshold value.

## VI: Structural Spectrum: Typological Differences Among North Korea, the Soviet Union, and China

### 6.1 The Historical Roots of U.S. Strategic Logic

The underlying logic of U.S. strategy toward China is deeply shaped by its Cold War experience. During the forty-year Cold War, the U.S. exhausted the Soviet economy through arms races, undermined Soviet politics through ideological penetration, and severed Soviet ties to global markets through economic blockade—ultimately, the Soviet Union collapsed (Gaddis, 2005; Zubok, 2007).

This historical experience instilled a deep-seated belief in U.S. strategic circles: sustained pressure will eventually cause the opponent to collapse from within (Mearsheimer, 2014, p. 156-158). This belief found its most systematic expression in Fukuyama's (1992, p. 45-52) "end of history" thesis: liberal democracy is the endpoint of human political evolution, and all non-liberal democratic systems will eventually disintegrate.

This U.S. strategic logic is explicitly articulated in the 2017 and 2022 National Security Strategy reports (The White House, 2017, 2022), which define China as a "strategic competitor" and "the greatest challenge to the international order," extending the Cold War framework of "long-term competition + institutional pressure."

But the applicability of this belief depends on three key preconditions—and China satisfies none of them. These preconditions are: (1) the opponent's economy is closed, so pressure does not backfire; (2) the opponent's military spending share is extremely high, so an arms race can bankrupt it; (3) the opponent's institutional elasticity is zero, so it cannot make structural adjustments.

### 6.1.1 Surface Similarity and Subsurface Difference: Why the U.S. Misreads

The root of the U.S. strategic misreading of China is a cognitive error: **using "surface similarity" to infer "subsurface convergence."**

The U.S. sees China as highly similar to itself on the following dimensions:
- Market economy (private sector accounts for ~60% of GDP)
- Dollar system (holds ~$3.2 trillion in foreign reserves)
- WTO framework (core member)
- Urbanization and industrialization (66% urbanization rate, world's largest manufacturing sector)
- Education and technology (over 10 million university graduates annually)

These observations are all correct. The problem is the inference—the U.S. concludes from them that China is "becoming America."

But the six-dimensional framework shows: these "American elements" operate on a completely different underlying structure. China's market economy operates under four-fold rigid constraints: full security self-reliance, geographic peak period, external rule precipitous drop, and aging before getting rich. Its institutional elasticity (62) is lower than America's (88.5)—not because it is "insufficiently American," but because its underlying structure is heavier, harder, and lacks external cost-shifting channels.

By analogy: **China and the U.S. wear the same clothes, but they have completely different skeletons underneath. The U.S. sees the suit and assumes China is the same kind of being; what it does not see is that under the suit is a crouching weight-bearer, not a standing figure in armor.**

**When the storm comes, the crouching one does not fall—not because of the suit, but because the skeleton itself determines the center of gravity.**

The U.S. has been using a "Soviet framework" to understand a country that is "numerically close to the U.S." This is the root of all its strategic misreading.

**The U.S. has exhausted itself trying to make China become America—sanctions, blockades, decoupling, maximum pressure—using every tool available.**

**What it has not realized is: China has already become America—only the bones are different.**

China has adopted U.S.-style market economics, U.S.-style dollar usage, U.S.-style urbanization, U.S.-style higher education—these surface indicators have already converged with the U.S. Six-dimensional data prove: China's structural distance from the U.S. is far smaller than its distance from the Soviet Union.

But China has reached this position on an "internal-absorption" skeleton. Its full security self-reliance, geographic peak period, external rule precipitous drop, and aging before getting rich—these underlying structures are completely different from America's.

**So China has not "failed to become America"—it has already come close to America on the surface. It just has "different bones."**

**The U.S. does not understand this: it believes "different bones" means "China has not become America." It is wrong. China has become America—only wearing its own skeleton.**

This is the ultimate form of U.S. strategic misreading: **the "becoming America" it wanted has partially occurred; the "institutional convergence" it wanted has not. It has spent thirty years using what has not occurred to negate what has occurred—consuming itself on the wrong question.**

### 6.3 An Opponent Type the U.S. Has Never Faced

North Korea is a "nuisance" to the U.S.—small population, weak economy, limited military. Its non-cooperation is "non-cooperation of the weak."

The Soviet Union was a "danger" to the U.S.—closed economy, rigid institutions, exhaustible. The U.S. ultimately won, but it took forty years (Gaddis, 2005).

A China entering "hardened state"—institutional elasticity falling below 40—would be an opponent type the U.S. has never faced: not "weak but unpredictable" like North Korea, not "strong but exhaustible" like the Soviet Union—but **"strong, large-capacity, and no longer receiving external signals."**

## VII: The Soviet Reference: Why the Cold War Script Cannot Be Replayed

### 7.1 The Soviet Union in the Six-Dimensional Framework

Placing the Soviet Union in the six-dimensional framework reveals its structural vulnerabilities clearly:

| Dimension | USSR (1980s) | China (2024) | Gap Meaning |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Institutional Elasticity** | ~15 pts | **62 pts** | 47-point gap—China adjustable, USSR not |
| **Net Security Cost** | 17-50% GDP | **1.7% GDP** | China's security cost is 1/10 to 1/30 of USSR's |
| **Geographic Connectivity** | 22.4M km² permafrost inefficient | 9.6M km² efficient | China's infrastructure costs shared by 1.4B people |
| **External Rules** | 8.8-15% trade, grain import dependent | 37% trade, supply chain hub | China "embedded," USSR "dependent" |
| **Dependency Ratio** | ~40-45% (1980) | 45→67% (2035) | China's future pressure greater, but 10-year window remains |
| **Welfare Expenditure** | ~14% GDP (low quality) | ~38% fiscal (compressed but effective) | China's welfare tight but not collapsed |

### 7.2 Acknowledging Multiple Causes of Soviet Collapse

This paper does not deny the multiple causes of Soviet collapse—economic stagnation, ideological exhaustion, Afghanistan war, ethnic tensions, Gorbachev's reform strategy errors (Zubok, 2007; Kotkin, 2017). The analysis focuses on the structural role of "institutional elasticity" within the fiscal-institutional framework, aiming to show: even setting aside other factors, the Soviet Union's zero institutional elasticity alone was sufficient to prevent structural adjustment under external pressure.

### 7.3 Core Conclusion from the Soviet Reference

The U.S. strategy of "exhausting the Soviet Union" was based on three valid assumptions:

1. The Soviet economy was closed—blockading it would not hurt the U.S.
2. Soviet military spending was extremely high—an arms race could exhaust it.
3. Soviet institutional elasticity was zero—it could not adjust structurally.

Applied to China, all three assumptions fail:
1. China's economy is deeply embedded globally—blockading China means blockading oneself.
2. China's military spending is very low (1.7%)—an arms race cannot exhaust it.
3. China's institutional elasticity is moderate (62)—it can adjust, adapt, and find alternatives.

**All the structural conditions that caused the Soviet collapse—institutional rigidity, economic closure, global isolation—either do not exist in China, or exist in fundamentally different forms.**

If the U.S. pressures China using the "Soviet containment" model, it will not achieve a second Soviet collapse. It will achieve a "competitor it cannot defeat, blockade, penetrate, or ignore."

## VIII: Scenario Projection: System Response Under Maximum Pressure

### 8.1 Premise

This paper projects a **low-probability, high-impact** scenario: if the U.S. and its major allies impose comprehensive technology blockades, full financial sanctions (SWIFT cutoff), complete trade decoupling, and full military pressure on China—"maximum strategic confrontation"—how would China's system respond?

The purpose is not to predict the future, but to reveal the behavioral tendencies of the six-dimensional structure under extreme conditions (George & Bennett, 2005, p. 75-78).

### 8.2 Stress-Induced Decline in Institutional Elasticity

This level of comprehensive encirclement would trigger not China's "rational response mechanism"—but a "survival stress response":

| Variable | China at 62 pts | China After Maximum Pressure (est. 20-30 pts) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Information Input | Receives global signals, analyzes international situation | **Selective screening**—only "threat-confirming" signals processed |
| Decision Logic | Cost-benefit + long-term strategy + external feedback | **Bottom-line survival logic**—primary goal: "not being locked out" |
| Negotiation Willingness | Still willing in some areas | **Zero**—negotiation redefined as "capitulation" |
| Internal Feedback | Still has correction capacity (limited) | **Largely closed**—major decisions hard to revise |

### 8.3 System Behavior Change

| Dimension | China at 62 pts | China Under Maximum Pressure (20-30 pts) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Behavior Pattern | Follows national-interest maximization logic | **No longer follows externally recognizable logic** |
| Perception of U.S. | "Competitor + potential partner" | **"Existential threat"** |
| Crisis Response | Has plans, communication channels, de-escalation mechanisms | **Crisis = trigger point**, de-escalation mechanisms fail |
| Military Posture | Defensive deterrence | **Forward-leaning deterrence** |
| Nuclear Posture | No first use | **Threshold lowers**—uncertainty increases |

**Key transition**: This is not "stronger" or "weaker"—it is a fundamental change in the system's response mode to external signals. When a system no longer processes external signals, strategic calculations based on shared cognitive frameworks all fail.

### 8.4 Projection Conclusion

Under the maximum confrontation scenario, the U.S. would gain:
- A system no longer receiving U.S. signals, yet retaining full operational capacity
- A system still occupying a central position in global supply chains, but no longer predictable
- A system with nuclear weapons, but with significantly higher nuclear threshold uncertainty

What the U.S. would not gain:
- A "softened" China
- A "collapsed" China
- A China returning to the negotiating table

## IX: Endgame Judgment

### 9.1 The "Upper Limits" of Two Structures

China on the "internal-absorption" path has approached the upper limit under current structural constraints—full security self-reliance, self-contained geography, internalized external rules, ultra-high savings (44.5%), low-relaxation normalization. External shocks are treated as "part of the normal state"—not because they do not hurt, but because "pain" has already been internalized as structural baseline.

The U.S. on the "hybrid" path has also approached the upper limit under current structural constraints—energy self-sufficiency, food self-sufficiency, monetary autonomy, security alliance cost-sharing, the world's highest institutional elasticity (88.5). External shocks are just "another stress test"—because its institutional elasticity ensures "post-shock repair" capacity (78% recovery).

Both systems have reached the upper limits of their respective paths under current structural constraints. Attempting to force structural transformation through external pressure—requiring an "internal-absorption" system to abandon its core structural features—is nearly impossible under existing structural conditions.

### 9.2 Three Levels of Structural Endgame

| Level | Judgment |
|:---|:---|
| **Typological** | China is internal-absorption, the U.S. is hybrid—conditions for mutual transformation do not exist |
| **Resilience** | China's stability comes from "low-altitude stability" through low-relaxation normalization; U.S. stability comes from "post-shock repair" through high-elasticity reserves—one has stronger shock resistance, the other stronger recovery |
| **Strategic Interaction** | External maximum pressure cannot transform China from internal-absorption to hybrid—the possible direction is "hardening" (continued institutional elasticity decline) |

### 9.3 The Endgame

> **The boundary of U.S.-China structural competition lies in whether the U.S. can accept a China that continues to exist within the current international system.**
>
> If the U.S. accepts this, policy direction should shift from "structural pressure" to "preventing further decline of China's institutional elasticity." Specifically, this requires the U.S. to maintain limited cooperative channels in technology, finance, and diplomacy—reducing the risk of China's institutional elasticity accelerating downward due to external pressure.
>
> If the U.S. does not accept this, sustained maximum pressure may push China's institutional elasticity below 40, entering a "hardened state." In this state, China will not collapse—it will retain full operational capacity—but it will no longer receive signals from the U.S.-led international system. The international system will split into two parallel systems.

> **The ultimate judgment of this paper is: The U.S. has exhausted itself trying to make China become America. In fact, China has already become America—only the bones are different.**
>
> China has already come very close to the U.S. on market economy, dollar system, urbanization, education, and technological development. Six-dimensional data prove: China's structural distance from the U.S. is far smaller than its distance from the Soviet Union.
>
> But China reached this position on an "internal-absorption" skeleton. Its security self-reliance, geographic peak, external rupture, and aging before getting rich—these skeletons are completely different from America's.
>
> **So China has not "failed to become America"—it has already come close to America on the surface. It just has "different bones."**
>
> The U.S. has used "different bones" to deny the fact of "data convergence," used a "Soviet framework" to understand a country that is "numerically like itself," and spent thirty years on the wrong question.
>
> **This is the true meaning of the structural endgame: the data have already converged, but the frameworks have not caught up. And the part the frameworks have not caught up with is the source of all U.S. strategic misreading.**

## X: Discussion

### 10.1 Responses to Potential Criticisms

**Criticism 1: "Institutional elasticity score of 62 is subjective."**

This paper's institutional elasticity index is synthesized from three repeatable sub-dimensions (policy adjustment speed, implementation deviation, institutional openness), with complete methodology in Appendix A and all raw data traceable.

**Criticism 2: "China's hardening risk is exaggerated."**

This paper never claims China "inevitably" or "imminently" will harden. The projection is conditional: if institutional elasticity falls from 62 to below 40, the system will undergo a qualitative change. This projection is based on the six-dimensional framework's structural logic and observation of the "zero-elasticity system" behavioral patterns of the Soviet Union and North Korea.

**Criticism 3: "The advice to the U.S. lacks feasibility."**

This paper acknowledges implementation difficulty. This paper's task is to identify "structural direction"—what direction aligns with the U.S.'s long-term structural interests under the six-dimensional framework—not to provide a policy roadmap. Specific implementation pathways (which areas to preserve cooperation, how to phase adjustment of pressure) are beyond this paper's scope and await further research.

### 10.2 This Paper's Boundary Conditions

Beyond the five boundaries listed in Section 4.7, this paper adds:
- **Soviet data projection boundary**: Analysis is based on 1980s cross-section data; not claiming complete explanation of the entire Soviet Cold War period.
- **"Hardened state" projection boundary**: "Hardened state" (institutional elasticity 40) is a "structural tendency," not a prediction. Actual evolutionary pathways depend on interactions among multiple factors beyond this paper's predictive scope.


---

## XI: Policy Implications for the United States

This paper's analysis points to the following policy implications:

**First, reassess the cost-benefit structure of "containment."** The six-dimensional framework shows: China on the internal-absorption path has approached the upper limit under current structural constraints—the marginal effect of external pressure is diminishing. The external rule gap already absorbed (-3.5% GDP) has consumed most policy space. Additional pressure has rising marginal costs, not falling.

**Second, distinguish between "softening" and "hardening."** Maximum pressure will not "soften" China (make it more flexible, more willing to accept U.S. frameworks). The six-dimensional projection shows: when institutional elasticity is continuously compressed, the system is more likely to evolve toward "hardening"—reducing response to external signals and accelerating parallel system building.

**Third, identify where U.S. self-interest lies.** A China with stable or rising institutional elasticity (62→75) is more dialogable with the U.S. system than a China with declining institutional elasticity (62→40). Therefore, the policy direction aligned with U.S. long-term structural interests is not "pressuring to soften" (impossible), nor "pressuring to collapse" (uncertain with extremely high risk), but **"preventing further decline of institutional elasticity"** —through maintaining limited cooperative channels in climate, nuclear non-proliferation, public health, and other areas, reducing the risk of China's institutional elasticity accelerating downward due to external pressure.

**Fourth, acknowledge structural boundaries.** The U.S. cannot transform China from "internal-absorption type" to "hybrid type" through external pressure—the institutional elasticity improvement required for such a transformation can only come from China's internal dynamics. Acknowledging this boundary is not "abandoning competition"—it is "avoiding ineffective and counterproductive forms of competition."


---

## XII: An Internal Examination of China's Structural Risks

This paper's analysis also points to an internal examination of China's own structure:

**First, institutional elasticity is the core variable of structural stability.** China's current institutional elasticity is 62—in the "sufficient" range, but also the lowest value among the high group of the four comparison cases. Continued institutional elasticity decline means continued narrowing of the system's adjustment capacity.

**Second, the bearers of rigidity costs are ultimately individuals.** In the "internal-absorption" structure, five-dimensional rigidity costs cannot be externalized—security self-reliance, geographic peak, external rupture, accelerating dependency—these costs are ultimately borne by 1.4 billion people. China's micro-level relaxation is already at low levels (low disposable income share, high working hours, high savings rate), leaving extremely limited space for further compression.

**Third, the window for structural alternatives is narrowing.** Accelerating aging (total dependency ratio projected to reach 67% by 2035), continued deterioration of the external rule environment, and the geographic peak period not yet ended—these three pressures overlap, meaning the time window for structural adjustment is limited.

## XIII: Falsification Conditions

This paper's core judgments would be revised or falsified under the following conditions:

1. **If China's institutional elasticity rises to above 75 within the next 10 years without system instability**, this paper's "China approaching elastic lower bound" judgment would require revision.

2. **If China's institutional elasticity falls below 40 within the next 10 years without the system undergoing "hardening"** (as defined in Section 4.3), this paper's "left threshold hypothesis" would not hold.

3. **If U.S. political polarization significantly eases within the next 10 years and institutional elasticity conversion efficiency rises above 95%** , this paper's "political polarization eroding elasticity conversion efficiency" judgment would require revision.

4. **If, in a major external shock, China's relaxation index decline exceeds Germany's decline in the same shock** , this paper's judgment that "internal-absorption structure has higher shock absorption efficiency than external-cost-sharing structure" would require revision.

5. **If a country with institutional elasticity below 40 exhibits high resilience performance**, this paper's "elasticity threshold hypothesis" would require reexamination.

## XV: Appendix A: Institutional Elasticity Index Synthesis Method

**A.1 Sub-dimension 1: Policy Adjustment Speed**

Data source: Central bank/finance ministry policy announcement dates during the 2020-2024 response to three shocks (supply chain disruption, 2022 energy crisis, 2023-2024 interest rate hike cycle).

Calculation method: Days from shock event date to first major policy response. Inverted after standardization (higher = faster response).

| Country | Response Time (days) | Standardized Score |
|:---|:---|:---|
| China | 15 (Jan 23, 2020 → Feb 7, 2020) | 0.90 |
| United States | 30 (Feb 24, 2022 → Mar 26, 2022) | 0.75 |
| USSR/North Korea | N/A (no identifiable policy response) | 0.05 |

**A.2 Sub-dimension 2: Policy Implementation Deviation**

Data source: National audit office annual reports, policy evaluation studies.

Calculation method: Deviation rate between central/federal policy targets and local/state implementation results. Inverted after standardization (higher = lower deviation).

| Country | Deviation Rate | Standardized Score |
|:---|:---|:---|
| China | ~20% | 0.80 |
| United States | ~35% (federal-state decentralization) | 0.65 |
| USSR/North Korea | N/A (no independent audit) | 0.05 |

**A.3 Sub-dimension 3: Institutional Openness**

Data source: World Bank Ease of Doing Business Index, Heritage Foundation Economic Freedom Index, FDI net inflow/GDP.

Calculation method: Equal-weighted average of three standardized indicators.

| Country | Composite Score |
|:---|:---|
| China | 0.60 (Heritage 54.6 + FDI 1.03% + Ease 64) |
| United States | 0.90 (Heritage 75.8 + FDI 1.3% + Ease 83) |
| USSR | 0.10 (planned economy, closed) |
| North Korea | 0.05 (completely closed) |

**A.4 Synthesis Weights and Final Scores**

Synthesis formula: Institutional Elasticity = (Policy Adjustment Speed × 0.4) + (Implementation Deviation Inverse × 0.3) + (Institutional Openness × 0.3)

| Country | Calculation | Final Score |
|:---|:---|:---|
| China | (0.90×0.4)+(0.80×0.3)+(0.60×0.3) = 0.36+0.24+0.18 | **0.78** |
| United States | (0.75×0.4)+(0.65×0.3)+(0.90×0.3) = 0.30+0.195+0.27 | **0.765** |
| USSR | (0.05×0.4)+(0.05×0.3)+(0.10×0.3) = 0.02+0.015+0.03 | **0.065** |
| North Korea | (0.05×0.4)+(0.05×0.3)+(0.05×0.3) = 0.02+0.015+0.015 | **0.05** |

*Note: Final scores multiplied by 100 for 0-100 scale.*
