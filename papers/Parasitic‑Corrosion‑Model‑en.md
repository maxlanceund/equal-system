# A Model of Parasitic Corruption in Public Power: A Conceptual Deductive Framework for System Intervention Effects

## 1. Abstract
This paper conducts a conceptual analysis of parasitic corruption within public power systems.

Two types of system intervention are distinguished: the removal of parasitic agents ($`I_p`$) and the reconstruction of system constraints ($`I_c`$).

Deducted from a set of fundamental postulates governing social systems, the exclusive implementation of individual-level intervention ($`I_p`$) does not necessarily lead to the recurrence of parasitic order. This outcome depends on internal state parameters such as the system’s corrosion potential.

This paper further addresses the epistemological status of propositions derived from the model: specific conjectures may be falsified through deduction or simulation. Falsification of a single proposition does not invalidate the entire analytical framework; instead, it delineates the scope of application for the original conjecture and facilitates the formulation of new conjectures with restrictive conditions.

**Keywords**: public power; parasitic corruption; system intervention; corrosion potential; social system

## 2. Introduction
In everyday discourse on power corruption, a widely accepted intuition holds that merely removing identified parasitic agents will inevitably allow parasitic corruption to re-emerge.
This intuition rarely undergoes conceptual scrutiny.
People seldom ask whether this conclusion holds unconditionally.
Nor do they examine which systemic conditions can alter evolutionary trajectories after intervention.

This paper investigates the above intuition through conceptual deduction.
It differentiates interventions across distinct systemic layers and unpacks latent premises embedded in popular assumptions.
It also clarifies the epistemological limits of this analytical framework.
This paper does not take real historical cases as its core basis for argumentation; cases serve only as auxiliary illustrative material.

## 3. Foundational Postulates
All subsequent deductions rest on the following foundational postulates.
These postulates form the starting point of the argument and are not defended within this paper.
If a reader rejects any postulate, the propositions derived thereafter carry no binding force for that reader.

Postulate 1 A public power system may be understood as an interactive system composed of a host system and parasitic agents.

Postulate 2 The global state of a social system cannot be reduced to individual traits or choices. The system possesses holistic variables such as corrosion potential.

Postulate 3 Interventions acting on the system fall into two categories: individual-level interventions and structural interventions. The two categories operate at distinct hierarchical levels.

## 4. Clarification of Core Concepts and Formal Expressions
This section defines key terminology throughout the paper to eliminate ambiguity in ordinary language. All subsequent arguments adopt the definitions laid out here.
Formal symbols and core equations of the model are presented to characterize systemic state transitions.

### 4.1 Stock of Parasitic Agents: $`P`$
Denotes the total quantity of parasitic agents present within the system at a given time.
This variable is the core state variable of the model; intervention actions directly act upon this stock.

### 4.2 Corrosion Potential: $`V`$
Satisfies $`V \ge 0`$. It characterizes the inherent tendency of the system to generate new parasitic agents.
Potential is independent of the current quantity of parasitic agents; it is an intrinsic global property of the system.

### 4.3 System Constraint Parameter: $`C`$
Represents structural conditions including institutions, incentive schemes, and supervision mechanisms.
Stronger constraints reduce the rate at which the system spontaneously generates parasitic agents.

### 4.4 Individual-level Intervention (Removal of Parasitic Agents): $`I_p`$
A transient intervention measure.
It only depresses the instantaneous value of $`P`$, and does not alter underlying parameters $`V`$, $`C`$, $`\delta`$, or $`f`$.

### 4.5 Structural Intervention (Reconstruction of System Constraints): $`I_c`$
A structural transformation measure.
It directly modifies parameter $`C`$, and indirectly alters the parasitic generation function $`f(V,C)`$.

### 4.6 Natural Decay Rate Constant of Parasitic Agents: $`\delta`$
Satisfies $`\delta>0`$.
It represents the rate at which existing parasitic agents exit or disappear when no new parasitic agents are added.

### 4.7 Parasitic Generation Rate Function: $`f(V,C)`$
Satisfies $`f(V,C)\ge 0`$.
When $`V`$ remains constant, strengthening $`C`$ reduces the value of $`f(V,C)`$.
This function describes the rate at which the system continuously produces new parasitic agents.

Model dynamical assumption:
When corrosion potential $`V`$ and system constraint $`C`$ remain constant, the system trajectory converges uniquely to the steady state $`P^*`$.
Oscillations, limit cycles and other complex dynamical behaviors are excluded.

### 4.8 Parasitic Corruption
Parasitic corruption does not refer to all forms of power misconduct.
Within this model, parasitic corruption describes a state in which certain agents exploit public power structures to obtain extra-institutional benefits, which in turn consume the overall resources of the host system.

### 4.9 Removal of Parasitic Agents
Removal of parasitic agents belongs to individual-level intervention.
It refers to replacing or expelling parasitic agents already present within the system, without altering system rules, incentive structures, or the global corrosion potential.
Formally denoted $`I_p`$. This intervention only depresses the instantaneous value of $`P`$, and does not modify $`V`$, $`C`$, $`\delta`$, or the generation function $`f`$.

### 4.10 Reconstruction of System Constraints
Reconstruction of system constraints belongs to structural intervention.
It refers to revising operational rules and incentive mechanisms of the system, directly changing the objective conditions that govern the generation of parasitic agents.
Formally denoted $`I_c`$. This intervention acts directly on parameter $`C`$, thereby modifying the generation function $`f(V,C)`$.

### 4.11 Corrosion Potential
Corrosion potential is a systemic concept describing the overall tendency of the system.
It characterizes the inherent capacity of a public power system to generate and sustain parasitic agents.
Potential is independent of the current number of parasitic agents within the system.

**Core Evolution Equation**

$$
\frac{dP}{dt}=f(V,C)-\delta P
$$

$\frac{dP}{dt}$ is the time rate of change of the stock of parasitic agents;
$f(V,C)$ is the parasitic generation rate jointly determined by corrosion potential and system constraints;
$\delta$ is the natural decay rate constant of parasitic agents.

Set $\frac{dP}{dt}=0$ to solve for the steady-state stock of parasitic agents:

$$
P^*=\frac{f(V,C)}{\delta}
$$

### 4.12 Recurrence of Parasitic Order
Recurrence of parasitic order means that after intervention, the system stably generates a substantial population of parasitic agents once again, forming a self-sustaining corrupt operating state. It is distinguished from isolated, occasional misconduct events.

$`P_{\text{threshold}}`$ denotes the critical stock threshold for the formation of parasitic order.
Under constant $`V`$ and $`C`$:
If $`P^* > P_{\text{threshold}}`$, the system will return above the critical threshold in the long run after $`I_p`$ is applied, and parasitic order recurs;
If $`P^* \le P_{\text{threshold}}`$, even if all existing parasitic agents are removed instantaneously, the long-run steady state remains below the threshold, and parasitic order does not recur.

## 5. Core Arguments, Theorems and Propositions
Based on the preceding postulates, conceptual definitions and formal expressions, the following set of theorems and philosophical propositions is derived.
Theorems correspond to formal model deductions; propositions translate these deductions into philosophical and social claims.
Every argument traces back to definitions established earlier in the text.

### 5.1 Theorem 1
Under exclusive individual-level removal intervention $`I_p`$, with $`V`$ and $`C`$ held constant, the long-run steady-state stock of parasitic agents is determined by $`f(V,C)`$ and $`\delta`$.

$$
P^*=\frac{f(V,C)}{\delta}
$$

**Proposition 1**
Removal of parasitic agents and reconstruction of system constraints are heterogeneous types of intervention, neither reducible to the other.

Argument:
Removal of parasitic agents only replaces agents within the system while preserving existing system constraints and corrosion potential.
Reconstruction of system constraints directly alters the objective conditions governing the generation of parasitic agents.
They operate on different layers of the system and are therefore irreducible.

### 5.2 Theorem 2
Under exclusive individual-level removal intervention $`I_p`$, the recurrence of parasitic order depends on the comparison between steady-state $`P^*`$ and the critical threshold $`P_{\text{threshold}}`$.
1. If $`P^*>P_{\text{threshold}}`$, parasitic order recurs;
2. If $`P^*\le P_{\text{threshold}}`$, parasitic order does not recur.

**Proposition 2**
The exclusive removal of parasitic agents is not sufficient to guarantee that parasitic corruption will not recur.

Argument:
Recurrence of parasitic order depends on the steady-state level jointly determined by corrosion potential and system constraints.
Recurrence occurs only when the steady-state stock of parasitic agents exceeds the critical threshold.
If the steady state lies below the threshold, parasitic order will not recur even after all existing parasitic agents are removed.
Accordingly, the intuition that “removing parasitic agents will necessarily stop corruption from returning” is not universally valid; it holds only under specific systemic states.

### 5.3 Theorem 3
Structural intervention $`I_c`$, by modifying parameter $`C`$, can alter the function $`f(V,C)`$ and thereby change the steady-state value $`P^*`$, enabling the system to cross the recurrence threshold.

**Proposition 3**
Conjectures derived from the model are falsifiable, yet the falsification of a single derived proposition does not refute the whole framework.

Argument:
Falsification of a conjecture via deduction or simulation only restricts its scope of applicability.
New conjectures can be formulated with additional restrictive conditions.
The framework fails only when its foundational postulates are rejected or its conceptual distinctions collapse entirely.

## 6. Objections and Limitations
This section addresses potential objections and clarifies the boundaries of the theory.

Objection 1 Real public power systems are highly complex; this abstract formal model has no explanatory value.

Response
This model does not aim to reproduce all fine-grained details of real public power systems. It is a thought-experiment analytical tool.
Its function is to examine latent premises in everyday intuitions and separate intertwined causal conditions.
It is not intended to directly predict outcomes for specific historical events.

Objection 2 If the foundational postulates of the model are contestable, all conclusions are unreliable.

Response
All conclusions in this paper are conditional conclusions whose validity is bounded by the stated postulates.
No claim is made that the postulates are self-evident truths.
Readers may reject the postulates, but this constitutes a separate line of argument outside the scope of this paper.
The model is not presumed to apply unrestrictedly to all real-world public power instances.
It delivers conditional possibilities and conceptual distinctions, rather than unconditional empirical predictions.

## 7. Conclusion
This paper identifies a widespread yet under-scrutinized cognitive error in public discourse: removing parasitic agents alone cannot permanently terminate corrupt order.

Many popular analyses reduce the problem to individual personnel rectification, assuming that purging individuals can resolve corruption, while ignoring corrosion potential and institutional constraints, the systemic conditions that govern whether corruption will return.

Through conceptual deduction and formal modeling, this paper disentangles this conflated causal logic and challenges the naive intuition that personnel replacement suffices to resolve power corruption.
It provides a distinguishable, falsifiable analytical framework for examining parasitic corruption in public power.
This paper clarifies the boundaries of corruption and the fragility of order.
