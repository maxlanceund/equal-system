# The Absolute Other: An Ontological Critique from Genetic Randomness to the Objectification of Consciousness

> **Core Thesis**: This essay begins with a visceral perplexity about the anime *Mushoku Tensei* and traces four paths—genetic randomness, bodily construction, the genesis of consciousness, and the fictionality of morality—to argue a single proposition: the "self" that humans cling to is not a subject but an "Absolute Other" pieced together from externalities (genes, language, society, death) on a particular carrier. The traditional philosophical presupposition of subjectivity—from Plato to Descartes onward—regarding "soul/consciousness/cogito" is hereby thoroughly inverted: consciousness is not the master but another Other gestated by the body, itself a product of genes. This essay engages three primary adversaries: Kant (the "I think" of transcendental apperception), Sartre (consciousness as absolutely free subjectivity), and Buddhism (no-self as ontological negation). It also dialogues with Hume, Schopenhauer, William James, Dennett, Lacan, Wittgenstein, Foucault, Althusser, Marx, Freud, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, Derrida, Levinas, and Zhuangzi. The essay fights on three fronts simultaneously, ultimately arguing: when all Absolute Others are seen through, "the Other is dead, I am born"—what dies is merely the constructed consciousness-object, and the freedom obtained after losing the Absolute Other is an experienceable, operable certainty.

## Abstract

Starting from a visceral perplexity about the anime *Mushoku Tensei*, this essay traces four paths—genetic randomness, bodily construction, the genesis of consciousness, and the fictionality of morality—to argue a core proposition: the "self" that humans cling to is not a subject, but an "Absolute Other" pieced together from all externalities (genes, language, society, death) on a particular carrier. The traditional philosophical presupposition of subjectivity—from Plato to Descartes onward—regarding "soul/consciousness/cogito" is hereby thoroughly inverted: consciousness is not the master, but another Other gestated by the body, itself a product of genes. It is labeled as "I," making people defend to the death something that does not belong to them. This essay fights on three fronts simultaneously: against Kant, it escalates from "boundary marking" to "internal explosion"—arguing that "synthetic unity" itself requires an operational labeling function, and that "I think" is not the only form of synthetic unity; against Sartre, it distinguishes early Sartre from later Sartre, acknowledges Sartre's critique of the "I," then argues that even "pre-reflective consciousness" is an Absolute Other constructed by genes and body, and reduces Sartre's "nihilization" to the system's operational plasticity; against Buddhism, it distinguishes "no-self as operational description" from "no-self as ontological negation," and directly points out the operational difficulty that "enlightenment requires a subject," responding with the "operational labeling" framework. It also dialogues with Hume's "bundle theory of the self," Schopenhauer's "will," William James's "stream of consciousness" and "I/Me," Dennett's "center of narrative gravity," Lacan's "misrecognition" and "split subject," Wittgenstein's "private language," Foucault's "subjectivation," Althusser's "interpellation," Marx's "alienation," Freud's "unconscious," Heidegger's "the They" and "authenticity," Merleau-Ponty's "body-subject," Derrida's "différance," Levinas's "Absolute Other," and Zhuangzi's "I lose myself." The essay directly responds to the recursive problem that runs through the entire text: if everything is constructed, is "construction" itself also constructed? The response is: distinguish "constructed things" from "operational difference." Operational difference is not constructed, but it is also not primary—it is the operational difference between system states, requiring no "who" to execute it. Nietzsche declared "God is dead"; this essay further declares "I" is dead—but the death of "I" is precisely the birth of absolute freedom. The essay ultimately argues: the freedom obtained after losing the Absolute Other is an experienceable, operable certainty. This essay shares the same underlying structure with *The Limits of Meaning: A Verificationalist Framework* (Zhong Shanzhen, 2026c)—finite experiential interval, closed-loop operation, undefined outside the boundary—but extends this structure from the domain of "meaning" to the domain of "self."

## Bibliographic Positioning and Academic Dialogue

This essay has three primary adversaries. They are not parallel, but form a triangle from shallow to deep, from light to dark.

**First, Sartre's core thesis in *Being and Nothingness* (1943): consciousness is absolutely free subjectivity.** Sartre holds that consciousness is "being-for-itself" (pour-soi), whose essence is freedom. Consciousness is nothing; it is always "not what it is, and is what it is not." Man is condemned to be free. You cannot escape freedom. Even if you do not choose, that is also a choice. Sartre says "Hell is other people." This essay opposes this position.

However, a distinction must first be made. In *The Transcendence of the Ego* (1936), Sartre already criticized the "I" as the master of consciousness. He holds that the "I" is an object of consciousness, not its subject. Being-for-itself is not the "I," but pre-reflective consciousness. This essay acknowledges Sartre's critique of the "I," but advances further: **even Sartre's "pre-reflective consciousness" is a constructed Absolute Other.** Sartre preserved the freedom of pre-reflective consciousness; this essay points out that pre-reflective consciousness itself, as another Other derived from the body—a genetic product—is equally a product pieced together by genes, language, social feedback, and knowledge systems. Sartre made freedom the essence of consciousness; this essay points out that true freedom begins precisely with seeing through this "illusion of freedom."

**This essay must directly respond to a core objection to the Sartre front: if pre-reflective consciousness is constructed by genes and body, does it have any freedom? If not, how does it differ from Sartre's "being-in-itself" (en-soi)? If so, is this essay's negation of Sartre too absolute?**

The response is: **pre-reflective consciousness has operational freedom, but not ontological freedom.** Operational freedom refers to the system's ability to adjust subsequent behavior based on feedback signals in a closed-loop operation. This is freedom in the cybernetic sense, not Sartre's "ontological freedom." Sartre's "man is condemned to be free" means that consciousness cannot escape choice at the ontological level. This essay says: this "cannot escape choice" itself is an operational mode set by genes and body for the system. The system does choose, but the subject of choice is not the "I," but the Absolute Other pieced together by genes and body. **Sartre's freedom is the freedom of the "I"; this essay's freedom is the operation of the "system." Sartre mistook an operational mode for ontological freedom.**

**This essay further responds to Sartre's concept of "nihilization" (néantisation).** Sartre holds that consciousness has a unique ability: it can "nihilize" given existence. Consciousness is not what it is; it can negate the current given state, imagine alternatives, and thus transcend the status quo. This "nihilizing" ability is the core of Sartre's theory of freedom. This essay's response: **"Nihilization" itself is an operational capacity of the system—the system can inhibit current signals and generate alternative signals.** When a system in closed-loop operation can adjust subsequent behavior based on feedback signals, it needs the ability to inhibit the current state and generate an alternative state. This is not the ontological capacity of "consciousness nihilizing," but operational plasticity in the cybernetic sense. Sartre mistook the system's operational plasticity for the ontological capacity of "consciousness nihilizing." **There is no ontological freedom of "nihilization," only the system's operational plasticity.** (This essay targets Sartre's position in *Being and Nothingness*, not the complex position of later Sartre in *Critique of Dialectical Reason*, which attempts to combine psychoanalysis and Marxism. For contemporary Sartre studies, see Gardner, 2009; Webber, 2018.)

**This essay further dialogues with Kant's concept of "transcendental freedom" in the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788).** Kant holds that transcendental freedom is the condition of the moral law—it is the ground of "I ought, therefore I can." Without transcendental freedom, the moral law would be impossible. The difference between this essay and Kant's concept of "freedom" is: Kant's transcendental freedom is a **presupposition**, a precondition of the moral law; this essay's "unalienated freedom" is an **experienceable state**, the liberation after seeing through the label. Kant says "you must be free"; this essay says "you can be unalienated." The former is the condition of the moral law, the latter is an operable fact. (For contemporary Kant studies, see Ameriks, 2000; Kitcher, 2011; Longuenesse, 1998.)

**Second, Kant's core thesis in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781): the "I think" of transcendental apperception is the precondition for the possibility of all experience.** Kant holds that the "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations. Any experience, to be possible, must have a unified "I" behind it that synthesizes the manifold of sensation. This "I" is a priori, logically necessary, and irreducible. Without this transcendental apperception of the "I," no experience would be possible.

This essay must acknowledge: the genetic evidence—that infants have no concept of "I"—cannot directly refute Kant's transcendental apperception. Kant distinguishes **transcendental apperception** from **empirical self-consciousness**. Transcendental apperception is the **logical condition** that the "I think" must be able to accompany all representations, not a psychological fact. The infant's lack of the word "I" does not mean the infant's experience lacks the unity of transcendental apperception. Kant could fully respond: the infant's experience is still unified by apperception, only the infant has not reflected on it.

**Therefore, this essay adopts an "internal explosion" strategy against Kant: arguing that "synthetic unity" itself requires an operational labeling function.**

First layer of argument: dissolving the metaphysical burden of "I think" through linguistic constructivism. The "I think" itself is a product of linguistic structure. The subject-predicate structure—"I" as subject, "think" as predicate—is a cut imposed by language on experience. Without the subject-predicate structure of language, there would be no proposition "I think." Kant's transcendental apperception presupposes a linguistic subject capable of saying "I." And this linguistic subject itself is constructed.

**Linguistic evidence: the existence of non-subject-predicate languages.** Some Amerindian languages (such as Hopi) do not use subject-predicate structure, but rather "event-process" structure. In these languages, experience is still organized, but the mode of organization is not "I-think," but "event-occurrence." This means: the logical function of transcendental apperception (synthetic unity) is universal, but its logical form ("I think") is not universal. Kant equated "synthetic unity" with "I think," which is a linguistic-centrism error. Synthetic unity can be realized through different logical forms; "I think" is only one of them.

**But this essay must honestly admit: this strike is still limited at the purely transcendental level.** Kant could fully respond: transcendental apperception is not a linguistic act, but a logical condition. It does not need to say "I"; it only needs the logical function of "synthetic unity" to accompany all representations. Linguistic constructivism strikes at the origin of the word "I," not the logical necessity of transcendental apperception.

**Therefore, this essay proceeds to a second layer of argument: from "internal explosion"—arguing that "synthetic unity" itself requires an operational labeling function.**

Kant's transcendental apperception is a logical function: synthetic unity. But what is "synthetic unity" itself? It is the function of unifying the manifold of representations. But unification itself requires a "label"—the system needs to label certain signals as "belonging to the same experience." Without this label, how can the manifold of representations be unified? Kant's answer is: the "I think" must be able to accompany all representations. But what is "accompanying" itself? If "accompanying" is an operational process, does it require an operator?

This essay's argument is: **"Accompanying" itself is an operational process—the system, in closed-loop operation, labels certain signals as "relevant to the current operation."** This labeling does not require an "I"; it only requires an operational state. Kant's "I think" presupposes a "labeler," but this labeler itself is an operational labeling function, not a transcendental "I." **Kant mistook the operational labeling function for the "I think" of transcendental apperception.** "Synthetic unity" is universal, but its executor is not the "I," but an operational labeling function. This labeling function itself, as another Other derived from the body—a genetic product—is also constructed.

**This essay further dialogues with Wittgenstein's "private language" argument in *Philosophical Investigations* (1953).** Wittgenstein argues: private language is impossible—language is necessarily social. The so-called "I" label is necessarily public and social. This is consistent with this essay's proposition: language is the Absolute Other. But Wittgenstein remains at the linguistic level; this essay introduces genetic randomness and thermodynamic closure. **This essay's "operational labeling" is more thorough than Wittgenstein's "language game": not only is language the Absolute Other, but the body that uses language is itself a product of random genetic assembly.**

**But this essay must honestly admit: this strike is still limited at the purely transcendental level.** Kant could fully respond: transcendental apperception is not an operational process, but a logical condition. It does not require a "labeler"; it only requires the logical function of "synthetic unity" to accompany all representations. This essay's conclusion is: **even if transcendental apperception is logically irrefutable, it cannot provide any metaphysical weight for the "I."** The word "I" is a product of linguistic structure; the consciousness labeled as "I" is the Absolute Other. As for transcendental apperception, this essay does not deny its necessity as a logical function, but refuses to equate it with the "self."

**Third, the Buddhist doctrine of "no-self" (anātman).** The "no-self" discussed in this essay has surface similarities with the Buddhist "no-self," but there are fundamental differences. The Buddhist "no-self" is an **ontological assertion**: it asserts that the "I" does not exist at all, that the five aggregates are empty, that all dharmas are without self, and on this basis constructs a complete soteriology (Four Noble Truths, Eightfold Path, nirvana). This essay's "no-self" is an **operational description**: it points out that what is labeled as "I" is entirely the Absolute Other, but whether the "I" ultimately exists, this essay does not assert. This essay does not deny that the "I" may not exist, nor does it deny that the "I" may exist—it only confirms one thing: the freedom obtained after losing the Absolute Other is an experienceable, operable certainty. **Buddhism has an ultimate home (nirvana); this essay has only an experienceable state (freedom after losing the Absolute Other).**

**This essay must directly point out a core difficulty for the Buddhist front: does enlightenment require a subject?**

If Buddhism says the "I" does not exist at all, then who is practicing Buddhism? Who is attaining enlightenment? Who is entering nirvana? If there is a "who" practicing, is this "who" precisely the "I" that Buddhism denies? If there is no "who" practicing, how is practice itself possible? This is an operational difficulty: any operation requires an operator. If the "I" does not exist, how is operation possible?

This essay's response: **enlightenment is not a "who" attaining enlightenment, but a change in operational mode.** Just as a machine stopping a program is not a "who" inside the machine deciding to stop, but the program's operating conditions no longer being met. Similarly, so-called "enlightenment" is also a change in operational mode, not a "who" attaining a "truth." Buddhism says "no-self" is a truth that can be enlightened; this essay says: so-called "enlightenment" is also a change in operational mode, not a "who" enlightening a "truth." **If the "I" does not exist, then "enlightenment" is not a "who" enlightening, but a change in operational mode. The Buddhist "no-self" assertion itself also faces an operational difficulty: enlightenment requires an operator, but Buddhism denies the operator. This essay's "operational labeling" framework can respond to this difficulty: enlightenment is not a "who" enlightening, but a change in operational mode.** (For contemporary Buddhist studies, see Williams, 1989; Siderits, 2007.)

This essay does not attempt to refute Descartes's (1641) "I think, therefore I am"—because this essay agrees that "I think" is a fact, only that the "I" in "I think" is not a subject, but a constructed object. This essay also does not attempt to refute Husserl's (1913) transcendental phenomenology, which belongs to another theoretical level: it is normative (answering "how consciousness constitutes the world"), while this essay is descriptive (answering "how consciousness is constructed in language and society").

**On the extension of the concept of the "Absolute Other."** Levinas's "Absolute Other" (Autrui) in *Totality and Infinity* (1961) and *Otherwise than Being, or Beyond Essence* (1974) originally refers to **the other person**, with infinite ethical responsibility. Before the face of the other, I bear infinite responsibility. Ethics is not founded on the "I," but on the "Other." This essay extends this concept: not only is the other person the Absolute Other, but body, consciousness, language, social feedback, morality, and death are all Absolute Others. This extension is not a negation of Levinas, but an application of his framework: if the other person is the Absolute Other, then the consciousness labeled as "I," as a constructed product, also has the structure of the Absolute Other.

**The extended "Absolute Other" does not weaken ethical force, but extends it from "responsibility to the other" to "humility toward oneself."** Levinas says "the other is unpossessable"; this essay says "the consciousness you label as 'I' is equally unpossessable." If you do not even belong to yourself, what right do you have to possess others? This argument not only does not weaken Levinas's ethical force, but pushes it to a more thorough conclusion: **if the "I" is also the Absolute Other, then all possession—whether of others or of the "self"—is violence against the Absolute Other.**

**This essay dialogues with Schopenhauer's concept of "will" in *The World as Will and Representation* (1818).** Schopenhauer argues: the essence of the world is "will" (Wille)—a blind, irrational, ceaseless impulse. Will is the archetype of the Absolute Other: it is not controlled by reason, not bound by morality, and only pursues its own satisfaction. The difference between this essay and Schopenhauer is: Schopenhauer's "will" is a **metaphysical entity**, while this essay's "Absolute Other" is an **operational description**. Schopenhauer says will is the essence of the world; this essay says what is labeled as "I" is the Absolute Other. Schopenhauer preserved "will" as primary existence; this essay points out: even "will" itself is constructed—it is another Absolute Other derived from the body—a product of random genetic assembly.

**Schopenhauer would respond: will is not labeled; it is the essence of the world. This essay's response: this essay's "operational labeling" is not a negation of Schopenhauer's "will," but an application of his framework. If will is the essence of the world, then it is also the archetype of the Absolute Other. But this essay further points out: even this "essence" itself is an operational labeling effect. Will is not primary; it is a labeling effect produced by the system in operation. This labeling does not require a "who" to execute it; it only requires a change in operational state.**

**This essay dialogues with Merleau-Ponty's concept of the "body-subject" (corps sujet) in *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945).** Merleau-Ponty argues: the body is not an object, but a subject—it is our way of interacting with the world. The body is not something "I" have, but something "I" am. The difference between this essay and Merleau-Ponty is: Merleau-Ponty's "body-subject" presupposes a "subject," while this essay points out that this "subject" is also constructed. The body is not a subject; the body is the Absolute Other of random genetic assembly. Merleau-Ponty's "body-subject" still presupposes an "I" as the core of the body; this essay points out: this "I" never existed; what exists is only the operational labeling derived from the body—a genetic product.

**Merleau-Ponty would respond: the body is not something "I" have, but something "I" am. This essay's response: this essay's "Absolute Other" is not a negation of Merleau-Ponty's "body-subject," but an application of his framework. If the body is a way of being-in-the-world, then it is also the archetype of the Absolute Other. But this essay further points out: even this "way of being" itself is an operational labeling effect. The body is neither subject nor object; it is operational difference.**

**The difference between "operational labeling" and Buddhist "ignorance" (avidyā) and Daoist "fixed mind" (chengxin).** The concept of "operational labeling" used in this essay has surface similarities with Buddhist "ignorance" (avidyā) and Daoist "fixed mind," but there are fundamental differences. Buddhist "ignorance" is a truth-state that can be enlightened—it asserts that the "I" does not exist at all, and one realizes this through practice. Daoist "fixed mind" is a social construction that can be "lost"—it holds that the "I" is a social solidified product, and one returns to the "Dao" through "I lose myself." This essay's "operational labeling" is an operational event that can be seen through—it does not assert whether the "I" exists, nor whether it can be "lost"; it only describes an operational fact: the system labels a certain operational signal as "I." This labeling has no "correct" or "incorrect" distinction; it is merely an operational fact. Buddhism and Daoism both have a positive, affirmative home (nirvana, Dao); this essay has only an experienceable state (freedom after losing the Absolute Other).

**The relationship between this essay and *The Limits of Meaning: A Verificationalist Framework* (Zhong Shanzhen, 2026c).** That essay argues: meaning is a signal generated by a finite system through closed-loop operation within a finite experiential interval; outside the interval, meaning is undefined—not zeroed, but no longer defined. This essay extends this framework from the domain of "meaning" to the domain of "self": if meaning is undefined outside the interval, then the "I" is equally undefined outside the interval. If meaning is a product of closed-loop operation, then the "I" is also a product of closed-loop operation. *The Limits of Meaning* is the epistemological premise of this essay; this essay is the ontological extension of *The Limits of Meaning*. The two essays share the same underlying structure—finite experiential interval, closed-loop operation, undefined outside the boundary—but move in different directions: the former deals with the boundary of "meaning," the latter with the boundary of "self."

## Introduction: Starting from an Awkward Anime

*Mushoku Tensei*, as a male-oriented harem anime, presents a perplexing "awkwardness": although it is male-oriented, it is accepted by a large female audience; it advertises a harem, yet makes the male protagonist kneel and beg; it depicts the bodies and beauty of female characters while endowing them with trauma, will, and independent personalities.

This "awkwardness" is not a flaw of the work itself, but a symptom of a deeper contradiction—when "male fantasy" nakedly encounters the fact of "woman as Absolute Other," the logic of traditional harem narrative cannot be self-consistent. The more "flesh-and-blood" the female characters are, the less likely they are to accept a shared fate; but if they are portrayed as brainless, self-throwing tools, the work's themes of "growth" and "redemption" completely collapse.

It is precisely this fissure that leads to this essay's core question: **who is this "I" that we take for granted?**

This essay's answer is: **"I" is the Absolute Other.** Not Sartre's "free subjectivity," not Kant's "transcendental apperception," not Buddhism's "illusion that does not exist at all." It is a temporary program pieced together by genes, language, society, and knowledge systems, occupying your body, making you label it as "I."

**This is a three-front war against Sartre, Kant, and Buddhism.** This essay aims to prove: Sartre is wrong, because even "pre-reflective consciousness" is constructed, and Sartre's "nihilization" is merely the system's operational plasticity; Kant is wrong, because "synthetic unity" itself requires an operational labeling function, and "I think" is not the only form of synthetic unity; Buddhism is also wrong, because it turns an operational description into an ontological assertion, and the operational difficulty that "enlightenment requires a subject" precisely exposes the internal tension of the Buddhist "no-self" assertion. This essay's weapons are genetics, thermodynamics, linguistic constructivism, cybernetics, and social constructivism. This essay's battlefield is the definitional right over the concept of "I."

**This essay must directly respond to a recursive problem that runs through the entire text: if everything is constructed, is "construction" itself also constructed? If "construction" is constructed, it requires another construction, leading to infinite regress. If "construction" is not constructed, then it is primary, and this essay's position is self-contradictory.**

**This essay's response is: distinguish "constructed things" from "operational difference."** Constructed things—consciousness, body, morality, death—are operational labeling effects. But operational difference itself—i.e., the difference between system states—is not constructed. It is also not primary. It is merely an operational fact: the difference between the system's current state and its previous state.

**The relationship between "operational difference" and "operational state" is: the change of operational state is operational difference. The two are different descriptions of the same thing. The change of operational state does not require a "who" to execute it; it only requires a change in system state.**

**The relationship between "operational labeling" and "operational difference" is: labeling is the operational recording of difference. Labeling is not the "cognition" of difference, but the operational effect of difference itself. This recording does not require a "recorder" to execute it; it only requires a change in operational state. Just as the mercury column of a thermometer does not require a "who" to push it, it only requires a change in temperature. Similarly, operational difference does not require a "who" to execute it; it only requires a change in system state.**

## I. The Body: The Random Meat Grinder of Genes

### I.1 Mendel's Dice

Mendelian genetics reveals the essence of sexual reproduction: during meiosis, homologous chromosomes randomly separate, non-allelic genes freely assort, and homologous recombination further exchanges segments. Each gamete is a random sampling of the parental genome; each fertilization is a reshuffling of two sets of random samples.

This means: **any person's bodily features—appearance, figure, constitution, talent—are essentially products of random genetic assembly.**

So-called "beauty" and "ugliness" are merely the result of this random combination happening (or not happening) to cater to the preferences for symmetry, youth, and health in human evolutionary psychology. A beautiful face is not "hers," but a display case for a winning genetic lottery ticket; a face considered "ugly" is not "his," but merely a genetic dice that did not roll six sixes.

**Beauty and ugliness have no ontological status, only statistical distribution.**

### I.2 Gravity, Entropy, and Death's Net

The second law of thermodynamics pronounces the ultimate fate of the universe: entropy increase is irreversible, and everything will eventually return to heat death. And gravity, the most fundamental interaction in the universe, is precisely the strongest force accelerating entropy increase—it condenses nebulae, ignites stars, shapes planets, and ultimately drags all matter into the abyss of black holes or gravitational wells.

Life is a brief vortex in the river of entropy increase. The replication, mutation, recombination, and evolution of genes are essentially a doomed resistance under the urging of the second law of thermodynamics. Genes "know" they are doomed, so they frantically chaos, expand, mutate, and reproduce—the noise of humanity, countless copulations, are merely the last struggle of genes before death's net closes.

**Gravity accelerates death, genes accelerate chaos, and death will ultimately net everything.**

### I.3 Conclusion of This Section

The body is not "mine"; it is the Absolute Other of random genetic assembly. Beauty and ugliness have no ontological status, only statistical distribution. Gravity and entropy together guarantee the inevitability of death's net. This conclusion directly points to this essay's first adversary: Sartre. If the body is the Absolute Other of random genetic assembly, then Sartre's "consciousness as free subjectivity" loses its foundation—because the body to which consciousness clings is itself not "mine."

## II. The Genesis of Consciousness: From "No-Self" to the Construction of "I"

### II.1 The Infant Has No "I": Dialogue with Hume and Schopenhauer

The genetic fact is: when an infant is just born, it has no concept of "I." It has only a pure flow of sensation—hunger, pain, comfort, cold. No boundary, no subject, no opposition between "I" and "world." What we later call "I" is **installed** later.

**This fact resonates with Hume's "bundle theory of the self" in *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739).** Hume says: when I introspect, I can never capture an entity called "self"; I can only capture a series of specific perceptions—heat, cold, love, hate, pain, pleasure. The so-called "self" is merely a collection of these perceptions, mistakenly sewn together by imagination. Hume's conclusion: "We never really touch a simple, identical self."

**But this essay has a key difference from Hume: Hume holds that the "self" is a collection of perceptions, but he preserves "perception" as primary existence. This essay further points out: even "perception" itself is constructed—it is the Absolute Other derived from the body—a genetic product.** Hume's "bundle of perceptions" still presupposes a "perceiver," while this essay's "operational labeling" presupposes no "perceiver." Perception itself is an operational state that is labeled, not primary existence. Hume distinguishes "impressions" from "ideas" in *A Treatise of Human Nature*. Impressions are the raw material of sensation; ideas are copies of impressions. This essay must respond to a possible objection: impressions are the most original; they cannot be further constructed. This essay's response: **impressions themselves also need to be labeled—the system needs to label certain signals as "impressions" in order to process them as "impressions." This labeling does not require a "who" to execute it; it only requires a change in operational state.**

Within the boundaries, they are valid; outside the boundaries, this essay is silent. But silence is not negation. Silence is an honest acknowledgment of the finitude of "operable freedom."**
