# The Absolute Other: An Ontological Critique from Genetic Randomness to the Objectification of Consciousness

## Abstract

Starting from a visceral perplexity about the anime *Mushoku Tensei*, this essay traces four paths—genetic randomness, bodily construction, the genesis of consciousness, and the fictionality of morality—to argue a core proposition: the "self" that humans cling to is not a subject, but an "Absolute Other" pieced together from all externalities (genes, language, society, death) on a particular carrier. The traditional philosophical presupposition of subjectivity—from Plato to Descartes onward—regarding "soul/consciousness/cogito" is hereby thoroughly inverted: consciousness is not the master, but another Other gestated by the body, itself a product of genes. It is labeled as "I," making people defend to the death something that does not belong to them.

This essay fights on three fronts simultaneously: against Kant, it escalates from "boundary marking" to "internal explosion"—arguing that "synthetic unity" itself requires an operational labeling function, and that "I think" is not the only form of synthetic unity; against Sartre, it acknowledges Sartre's critique of the "I," then argues that even "pre-reflective consciousness" is an Absolute Other constructed by genes and body, and reduces Sartre's "nihilization" to the system's operational plasticity; against Buddhism, it distinguishes "no-self as operational description" from "no-self as ontological negation," and responds to the operational difficulty that "enlightenment requires a subject" with the "operational labeling" framework. It also dialogues with Hume, Schopenhauer, James, Dennett, Lacan, Wittgenstein, Foucault, Althusser, Marx, Freud, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, Derrida, Levinas, and Zhuangzi, directly responding to the recursive problem: if everything is constructed, is "construction" itself also constructed?

Nietzsche declared "God is dead"; this essay further declares "I" is dead—but the death of "I" is precisely the birth of absolute freedom. Appendix A reports three structural-model experiments supporting the essay's three core structural claims. This essay shares the same underlying structure with *The Limits of Meaning: A Verificationalist Framework*—finite experiential interval, closed-loop operation, undefined outside the boundary—but extends this structure from the domain of "meaning" to the domain of "self."

**Keywords**: Absolute Other; objectification of consciousness; genetic randomness; death's net; Sartre; Kant; Buddhist no-self; ontological freedom

## Bibliographic Positioning and Academic Dialogue

This essay has three primary adversaries, forming a triangle from shallow to deep: Sartre's free subjectivity, Kant's transcendental apperception, and Buddhist no-self.

**Against Sartre**, this essay acknowledges his critique of the "I" in *The Transcendence of the Ego*, but points out that even "pre-reflective consciousness" is constructed. The full deduction is in Section IV.

**Against Kant**, genetic evidence cannot directly refute transcendental apperception, because Kant distinguishes transcendental apperception from empirical self-consciousness. This essay adopts an "internal explosion": arguing that "synthetic unity" itself requires an operational labeling function. "I think" is a product of the subject-predicate structure of language; the existence of non-subject-predicate languages (such as Hopi) shows that synthetic unity is universal, but "I think" is not a universal form. Kant mistook the operational labeling function for the "I" of transcendental apperception. Even if transcendental apperception is logically irrefutable, it cannot provide any metaphysical weight for the "I." (See Ameriks, 2000; Kitcher, 2011; Longuenesse, 1998.)

**Against Buddhism**, this essay's "no-self" is an operational description, not an ontological negation. The Buddhist "no-self" assertion faces an operational difficulty: does enlightenment require a subject? The full deduction is in Section VII. This essay does not assert that the "I" exists, nor that it does not exist; it only confirms that the freedom obtained after losing the Absolute Other is an experienceable, operable certainty. (See Williams, 1989; Siderits, 2007.)

**Dialogue with other traditions**: this essay shares a structure with Hume, Schopenhauer, James, Dennett, Lacan, Wittgenstein, Foucault, Althusser, Marx, Freud, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, Derrida, Levinas, and Zhuangzi—accepting their core insights, but pointing out that they preserve an unconstructed remainder: bundle of perceptions, will, I/Me, center of narrative gravity, subject, language game, power/knowledge, ideology, species-being, unconscious, authenticity, body-subject, différance, the Other, the Dao. This essay's advance lies in: even this remainder is itself the Absolute Other.

Levinas's "Absolute Other" originally refers to the other person; this essay extends it: not only is the other person the Absolute Other, but body, consciousness, language, social feedback, morality, and death are all Absolute Others. This extension does not weaken ethical force, but extends it from "responsibility to the other" to "humility toward oneself."

This essay shares the same underlying structure with *The Limits of Meaning*—finite experiential interval, closed-loop operation, undefined outside the boundary—but moves in different directions: the former deals with the boundary of "meaning," the latter with the boundary of "self."

## Introduction: Starting from an Awkward Anime

*Mushoku Tensei*, as a male-oriented harem anime, presents a perplexing "awkwardness": although it is male-oriented, it is accepted by a large female audience; it advertises a harem, yet makes the male protagonist kneel and beg; it depicts the bodies and beauty of female characters while endowing them with trauma, will, and independent personalities.

This "awkwardness" is not a flaw of the work itself, but a symptom of a deeper contradiction—when "male fantasy" nakedly encounters the fact of "woman as Absolute Other," the logic of traditional harem narrative cannot be self-consistent.

It is precisely this fissure that leads to this essay's core question: **who is this "I" that we take for granted?**

This essay's answer is: **"I" is the Absolute Other.** Not Sartre's "free subjectivity," not Kant's "transcendental apperception," not Buddhism's "illusion that does not exist at all." It is a temporary program pieced together by genes, language, society, and knowledge systems, occupying your body, making you label it as "I."

A recursive problem that runs through the entire text is: if everything is constructed, is "construction" itself also constructed? This essay's response is: distinguish "constructed things" from "operational difference." Constructed things—consciousness, body, morality, death—are operational labeling effects. But operational difference itself—i.e., the difference between system states—is not constructed, nor is it primary. It is merely an operational fact: the difference between the system's current state and its previous state. The change of operational state does not require a "who" to execute it; it only requires a change in system state.

## I. The Body: The Random Meat Grinder of Genes

### I.1 Mendel's Dice

Mendelian genetics reveals the essence of sexual reproduction: during meiosis, homologous chromosomes randomly separate, non-allelic genes freely assort, and homologous recombination further exchanges segments. Each gamete is a random sampling of the parental genome; each fertilization is a reshuffling of two sets of random samples.

This means: **any person's bodily features—appearance, figure, constitution, talent—are essentially products of random genetic assembly.**

So-called "beauty" and "ugliness" are merely the result of this random combination happening (or not happening) to cater to the preferences for symmetry, youth, and health in human evolutionary psychology. A beautiful face is not "hers," but a display case for a winning genetic lottery ticket.

**Beauty and ugliness have no ontological status, only statistical distribution.**

### I.2 Gravity, Entropy, and Death's Net

The second law of thermodynamics pronounces the ultimate fate of the universe: entropy increase is irreversible, and everything will eventually return to heat death. And gravity, the most fundamental interaction in the universe, is precisely the strongest force accelerating entropy increase—it condenses nebulae, ignites stars, shapes planets, and ultimately drags all matter into the abyss of black holes or gravitational wells.

Life is a brief vortex in the river of entropy increase. The replication, mutation, recombination, and evolution of genes are essentially a doomed resistance under the urging of the second law of thermodynamics. Genes "know" they are doomed, so they frantically chaos, expand, mutate, and reproduce—the noise of humanity, countless copulations, are merely the last struggle of genes before death's net closes.

**Gravity accelerates death, genes accelerate chaos, and death will ultimately net everything.**

### I.3 Conclusion of This Section

The body is not "mine"; it is the Absolute Other of random genetic assembly. Beauty and ugliness have no ontological status, only statistical distribution. Gravity and entropy together guarantee the inevitability of death's net. This conclusion directly points to this essay's first adversary: Sartre. If the body is the Absolute Other of random genetic assembly, then Sartre's "consciousness as free subjectivity" loses its foundation—because the body to which consciousness clings is itself not "mine."

## II. The Genesis of Consciousness: From "No-Self" to the Construction of "I"

### II.1 The Infant Has No "I": Dialogue with Hume and Schopenhauer

The genetic fact is: when an infant is just born, it has no concept of "I." It has only a pure flow of sensation—hunger, pain, comfort, cold. No boundary, no subject, no opposition between "I" and "world." What we later call "I" is **installed** later.

Hume points out in *A Treatise of Human Nature*: when I introspect, I can never capture an entity called "self"; I can only capture a series of specific perceptions. The so-called "self" is merely a collection of these perceptions. This essay agrees with Hume, but further points out: even "perception" itself is constructed—it is the Absolute Other derived from the body—a genetic product. Hume's "bundle of perceptions" still presupposes a "perceiver," while this essay's "operational labeling" presupposes no "perceiver."

Schopenhauer sets the essence of the world as "will"—a blind, irrational impulse. This essay's "Absolute Other" is more thorough than "will": even "will" itself is constructed.

### II.2 The First Brick of Language: Dialogue with James, Lacan, and Wittgenstein

When a child learns to say "I am hungry," "I want," "this is mine," the "I" is born as a **grammatical function**. Language inherently carries a subject-predicate structure, cutting the flow of sensation into "subject" and "object," "I" and "world." **"I" is not a biological fact, but a product of linguistic structure.** Without language, there is no "I."

James distinguishes the "I" and the "Me," holding that the "I" is pure present consciousness. This essay points out: even the "I" is constructed—it is a temporary program pieced together by language, social feedback, and knowledge systems.

Lacan holds that the "ego" is a misrecognition of the mirror stage. This essay agrees, but further points out: even the "subject" is constructed—it is the Absolute Other pieced together by genes, language, and social feedback.

Wittgenstein argues that private language is impossible; language is necessarily social. This essay agrees, but is more thorough than Wittgenstein: not only is language the Absolute Other, but the body that uses language is itself a product of random genetic assembly.

### II.3 Social Feedback and Knowledge Indoctrination: Dialogue with Foucault and Althusser

Parents' laughter and criticism, teachers' praise and punishment, classmates' acceptance and rejection, society's labels of "smart," "stupid," "beautiful," "ugly," "success," "failure"—these external feedbacks are like countless mirrors, reflecting the image of "you" back. The child looks at these mirrors, thinking he sees "himself." **In fact, what he sees is the projection of society—the Absolute Other—onto him.**

Foucault argues that the "subject" is produced by power/knowledge. This essay agrees, but further points out: even "power/knowledge" itself is constructed. Foucault's "subjectivation" still presupposes a social construction level; this essay points out that this level itself is also constructed.

Althusser argues that the "subject" is a product of ideological "interpellation." This essay agrees, but further points out: even "ideology" itself is constructed.

### II.4 Conclusion: Consciousness Is Object, Not Subject—Dialogue with Dennett, Freud, and Merleau-Ponty

Traditional philosophy since Descartes's "I think, therefore I am" has by default taken consciousness as subject, origin, and spontaneous primary existence. Kant even elevated the "I think" to transcendental apperception, regarding it as the precondition of all experience. But the truth of genesis is precisely the opposite: **the mother gestates the child's body (Absolute Other One), the child's body gestates consciousness (Absolute Other Two), consciousness produces operational labeling, making you think it is you.**

Consciousness is not the master; it is another Other derived from the body—a genetic product. It is labeled as "I," making you defend to the death something that does not belong to you.

Dennett holds that the "self" is a "center of narrative gravity." This essay agrees, but further points out: even narrative itself is constructed.

Freud holds that the "ego" is torn by the "id" and the "superego." This essay agrees, but further points out: even the "unconscious" itself is constructed.

Merleau-Ponty holds that the body is not an object but a subject. This essay's difference is: the body is not a subject; the body is the Absolute Other of random genetic assembly.

The "operational labeling" has surface similarities with Buddhist "ignorance" and Daoist "fixed mind," but there are fundamental differences: Buddhist "ignorance" is a truth-state that can be enlightened, Daoist "fixed mind" is a social construction that can be "lost," while this essay's "operational labeling" is an operational event that can be seen through—it does not assert whether the "I" exists, nor whether it can be "lost." Buddhism and Daoism both have a positive, affirmative home (nirvana, Dao); this essay has only an experienceable state (freedom after losing the Absolute Other).

## III. Morality and Justice: Game Rules Written by the Other

If the "I" is a consciousness-object, then what are morality, justice, and virtue?

**They are a rule system written by society—the giant Absolute Other—to manage countless consciousness-objects.**

Why is there morality? Because countless bodies driven by genes compete for resources; without rules, there would be complete chaos. So the Other invented "good," "justice," "virtue," "responsibility," using this narrative to maintain order, discipline individuals, and ensure the smooth transmission of genes.

Marx argues that the worker is estranged from himself in alienation. This essay agrees, but further points out: even "species-being" itself is constructed. Foucault and Althusser show how power/knowledge and ideology produce "subjects." This essay agrees, but further points out: even "power/knowledge" and "ideology" themselves are constructed.

However, morality has nothing to do with the essence of the world: gravity does not care whether you kill for justice or evil; the second law of thermodynamics does not care whether you help an old lady cross the street; genes do not care whether you are noble or base; death does not care whether you do good or evil all your life. Morality is merely the rules of an ant colony. Ants fight bloody battles over who should move which piece of soil, thinking this is "justice," but when a meteor falls, neither the ant colony nor the meteor cares about "justice."

**It must be stated**: this essay's focus is on the ontological construction of the "self," not on moral philosophy. Detailed analysis of the moral dimension (the origin of morality, the source of normativity, the debate between moral realism and anti-realism) is left to subsequent essays. This section only points out one structural correspondence: if the "I" is the Absolute Other, then morality—as a rule system for managing countless "I"s—is equally the Absolute Other.

## IV. Nietzsche's Echo and Sartre's Illusion: God Is Dead, "I" Is Also Dead

### IV.1 Nietzsche's Declaration: God Is Dead

In *The Gay Science*, Nietzsche has the madman declare: "God is dead! God really is dead! And we have killed him!" This declaration is not a victory declaration of atheism, but the naming of an ontological event: **the transcendent coordinate that provided the ultimate foundation for all values has collapsed.**

Without God, there is no eternal moral law, no promise of immortal soul, no threat of "final judgment." All values need to be revalued. Nietzsche calls this event the advent of "nihilism": the highest values devalue themselves, purpose is absent, meaning collapses.

Nietzsche further points out in *The Will to Power*: "the subject" is not something given, but something added, invented, projected behind. This essay agrees with Nietzsche's critique of the "subject," but further points out: even "will to power" itself is constructed.

### IV.2 Sartre's Response: Man Is Condemned to Be Free

Facing the void after God's death, Sartre gives an answer in *Being and Nothingness*: **man is condemned to be free.**

Sartre says: if there is no God, there is no pre-given human nature. Man first exists, then defines himself through his choices. Consciousness is "being-for-itself," it is nothing, it is always "not what it is, and is what it is not." It is freedom itself. You cannot escape freedom. Even if you do not choose, that is also a choice. Hell is other people, because the gaze of the other turns me into an object, depriving me of my freedom.

### IV.3 This Essay's Advance: Sartre Is Wrong—Even "Pre-Reflective Consciousness" Is the Absolute Other

Sartre killed God, but he preserved the "I"—or more precisely, he preserved the freedom of "pre-reflective consciousness." In *The Transcendence of the Ego*, Sartre already criticized the "I" as the master of consciousness; he holds that the "I" is an object of consciousness, not its subject. This essay acknowledges Sartre's critique of the "I," but advances further: **even Sartre's "pre-reflective consciousness" is a constructed Absolute Other.**

A possible objection: pre-reflective consciousness precedes language. Infants have pre-reflective consciousness before learning language. This essay's response: pre-reflective consciousness is not constructed by language, but by genes and body. It is another Absolute Other derived from the body—a product of random genetic assembly.

A deeper objection: if pre-reflective consciousness is constructed by genes and body, does it have any freedom? If not, how does it differ from Sartre's "being-in-itself"? If so, is this essay's negation of Sartre too absolute?

This essay's response: **pre-reflective consciousness has operational freedom, but not ontological freedom.** Operational freedom refers to the system's ability to adjust subsequent behavior based on feedback signals in a closed-loop operation. This is freedom in the cybernetic sense, not Sartre's "ontological freedom." Sartre's "man is condemned to be free" means that consciousness cannot escape choice at the ontological level. This essay says: this "cannot escape choice" itself is an operational mode set by genes and body for the system. The system does choose, but the subject of choice is not the "I," but the Absolute Other pieced together by genes and body. **Sartre's freedom is the freedom of the "I"; this essay's freedom is the operation of the "system." Sartre mistook an operational mode for ontological freedom.**

This essay further responds to Sartre's concept of "nihilization." Sartre holds that consciousness can negate the current given state, imagine alternatives, and thus transcend the status quo. This essay's response: "nihilization" itself is an operational capacity of the system—the system can inhibit current signals and generate alternative signals. This is not the ontological capacity of "consciousness nihilizing," but operational plasticity in the cybernetic sense. **There is no ontological freedom of "nihilization," only the system's operational plasticity.**

Sartre says: Hell is other people. This essay says: **"I" is hell.** Because the "I" you think is freely choosing (even that pre-reflective consciousness) is itself a program installed in you by others (society) and genes (body). You are not imprisoned by others; you are imprisoned by the label called "I."

God is dead, "I" is also dead.

### IV.4 Nietzsche's Eternal Recurrence and Abyss-Gazing

In *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, Nietzsche proposes the thought experiment of "eternal recurrence": if your life had to repeat infinitely, with every pain, every nausea, every despair repeating infinitely, could you bear it?

But this essay has already pointed out: **if there were no death, the world would be the real hell.** The horror of eternal recurrence lies precisely in its cancellation of death's boundary—it traps you forever in the suffering of subjectivity, never to be liberated. Death is precisely the universe's merciful boundary for life. If there were no death, genes would chaos infinitely, and subjectivity would fall into the sea of suffering of eternal recurrence. Death is not the enemy of life; death is the frame, the rest, the boundary of life.

Nietzsche writes in *Beyond Good and Evil*: "He who fights with monsters should be careful lest he thereby become a monster. And if you gaze long into an abyss, the abyss also gazes into you." This is the warning to all who see through the truth. But this essay's deduction is colder than Nietzsche's: when you see through the "I" as part of the abyss, the abyss and the gazer disappear together. It is not "I" defeating the abyss, but both "I" and the abyss are gone. What remains is not a monster, not nihilism, but a state of no longer being kidnapped.

## V. The Metaphysics of Death: The Universe's Merciful Net—Dialogue with Heidegger

### V.1 How Ordinary People Dissolve Death

The vast majority (what Heidegger calls "the They") use wealth, status, power, family, fame, entertainment, and consumption to frantically "dissolve" death. They pretend death is far away, that death is someone else's business, that as long as they possess enough, they can fill, squeeze out, and obscure death.

Heidegger argues: "Dasein" is not the "I," but "being-in-the-world." "The They" is the everyday mode of Dasein—it is not any particular person, but the anonymous authority of "people." Dasein loses itself in the They, and reclaims itself in "being-toward-death." This essay agrees, but further points out: even "authenticity" itself is constructed. Heidegger's "being-toward-death" still presupposes an "authentic Dasein"; this essay points out that this "authentic Dasein" never existed. Death is not the occasion for reclaiming authenticity; death is the termination of the operational interval.

But this dissolution is doomed to fail. Because death is **irreplaceable**. No matter how much money you have, how many people love you, your death can only be your own. Death is the most absolute, most equal "sovereign" in the world.

### V.2 Death as a Gift

From the perspective of subjective suffering, death is precisely **the universe's most merciful boundary setting**.

If there were no death, suffering would have no end. Wars would never end, hatred could never be dissolved, trauma could never heal. You would be trapped in eternal suffering, like a torture chamber without a pause button. If there were no death, everything would become infinite procrastination. All love, hate, effort, failure would become an infinitely looping soap opera. Infinite time would deprive the "present" of its weight. If there were no death, the "Absolute Other" would collapse. Because everyone dies, everyone has an "absolutely irreplaceable endpoint," so everyone's interior is a fortress that can never be breached.

**Death is the universe's gentle boundary for all finite life: "Enough. You have hurt enough. You have loved enough. You have tried enough. Now, rest."**

### V.3 Heat Death and the Net

The second law of thermodynamics says: everything will return to disorder. Gravity says: I will accelerate this process. Genes say: I allow you to chaos, to expand frantically. Death says: but in the end, I will net it all.

**Death is not the enemy of life; it is the frame, the rest, the boundary of life.** Only because of this boundary can we not turn others into our tools; only because of this boundary does our freedom appear so real; only because of this boundary does every clear, gentle choice we make in this finite life possess indelible weight.

### V.4 Death and *The Limits of Meaning*

In *The Limits of Meaning: A Verificationalist Framework*, the author argues: meaning is a signal generated by a finite system through closed-loop operation within a finite experiential interval; outside the interval, meaning is undefined—not zeroed, but no longer defined.

Now we apply this framework to death. Death is the termination of the experiential interval. After death, the meaning of the "I"—if the "I" is a product of closed-loop operation—is equally undefined. This is not to say the "I" disappears, but that the function "I" has no value outside its domain of definition.

**What death nets is the Absolute Other labeled as "I," not the "true I." As for whether the "true I" exists, this essay does not assert.**

**Death terror itself is also a product of "operational labeling."** What fears death is the Absolute Other labeled as "I," not the "true I." If the "true I" exists, it does not fear death; if it does not exist, the subject fearing death never existed. Death terror is so universal precisely because the Absolute Other labeled as "I" mistakes its own demise for the demise of the "true I." Seeing through this operational labeling, death terror loses its foundation.

This is completely consistent with the treatment of "extended closure" in *The Limits of Meaning*: if a person anchors meaning in "posthumous recognition" or "historical judgment" after death, then this meaning is **undecidable** within this framework.

"Undecidable" is not "unknowable," but "the toolbox of this framework cannot handle it." Silence is not ontological unknowability, but operational undecidability. This essay does not claim to know what is outside the boundary; it only claims that its framework cannot handle problems outside the boundary.

Death is not the negation of meaning, but the boundary of meaning. As Wittgenstein said: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." The meaning after death is that of which one cannot speak. This essay does not deny that it may exist, but this essay's framework does not handle it.

In *Phaedo*, Socrates says: "Philosophy is the practice of death." Because the soul (consciousness) is trapped in the body (a genetic product); only by escaping the body can one touch truth. Plato holds: **the soul (consciousness) is the true "I"; the body is the prison.**

When the object called "Socrates" died, this "I" did not disappear with it. On the contrary, it was completely liberated from the bondage of the identity "Socrates."

**The death of the Other is precisely the birth of the "I."**

## VII. Eastern Dialogue: This Essay's "No-Self" and the Buddhist "No-Self"—Dialogue with Zhuangzi and Nāgārjuna

### VII.1 The Buddhist "No-Self": Ontological Negation

One of the core teachings of Buddhism is "no-self" (anātman). What must be precisely defined is: what Buddhism denies as "I" is the "permanent, unitary, autonomous" entity—an "I" that is eternally unchanging, independently existing, and capable of self-mastery. Buddhism does not deny the continuity of the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness)—the continuity of the experiential stream. The five aggregates flow in time, causally continuous, but there is no permanent entity governing behind them. This is the precise meaning of the Buddhist "no-self": denying the entity, not denying the continuity.

This essay's "operational labeling" has a structural similarity with the Buddhist "no-self": both deny that the "I" is a permanent entity. But there are two fundamental differences.

**First, the ontological status is different.** The Buddhist "no-self" is an ontological assertion: it asserts that the "I" does not exist at all, a truth judgment about existence itself, realizable through practice. This essay's "no-self" is an operational description: it points out that what is labeled as "I" is entirely the Absolute Other, but whether the "I" ultimately exists, this essay does not assert.

**Second, the destination is different.** Buddhism has an ultimate home (nirvana); this essay has only an experienceable state (freedom after losing the Absolute Other).

This raises a core question for the Buddhist front: **does enlightenment require a subject?** If Buddhism says the "I" does not exist at all, who is practicing? Who is attaining enlightenment? Who is entering nirvana?

Buddhism's internal answer is: it does not require a permanent "I," but it requires "continuity." Practice is the transformation of continuity, not the transformation of an "I." Just as a river flows without needing a "soul of the river," practice does not need an "I." But this answer has an operational tension: if continuity itself is also empty (Madhyamaka position), then the question "who is practicing" has nowhere to rest; if continuity is substantially real (Theravada position), then its relationship with "no-self" requires more refined explanation.

This essay's response: **enlightenment is not a "who" attaining enlightenment, but a change in operational mode.** This response has a structural similarity with the Buddhist "transformation of continuity," but with a key difference: the Buddhist transformation of continuity has a direction (toward liberation), while this essay's operational mode change has no direction. The Buddhist enlightenment is a process with a goal; this essay's "seeing through" is a description without a goal. This essay does not promise any liberation, only describes an operational fact: when the system no longer labels a certain signal as "I," the operational mode changes.

### VII.2 This Essay's "No-Self": Operational Description

The "no-self" discussed in this essay has surface similarities with the Buddhist "no-self," but there are fundamental differences.

This essay's "no-self" is an **operational description**, not an ontological assertion. This essay points out: what is labeled as "I"—consciousness, body, morality, death—is entirely the Absolute Other. They come from outside, are constructed, and are not "I."

But this essay **does not** assert that the "I" does not exist at all. This essay only says: what you think is the "I" is not the true "I." As for whether the true "I" exists, this essay does not assert. It may not exist, or it may exist. This essay is uncertain.

What this essay is certain of is another thing: **the freedom obtained after losing the Absolute Other is an experienceable, operable certainty.**

When you see through the falsity of consciousness, the fictionality of morality, the inevitability of death, you are no longer kidnapped by the label called "I." You no longer suffer because of its pain, no longer fear because of its fear, no longer despair because of its death. This "un-kidnapped" state is experienceable.

Buddhism has an ultimate home (nirvana); this essay has only an experienceable state (freedom after losing the Absolute Other).

### VII.3 Fundamental Difference from Daoist "I Lose Myself"

Zhuangzi's "I lose myself" loses the socially constructed, solidified "small self" (fixed mind), and attains the "I" (true self) that communicates with the **Dao**. Zhuangzi has a **positive, affirmative home**: unity with all things, carefree wandering.

The fundamental difference between this essay and Zhuangzi is: does not affirm the "true self" (this essay only points out that what is labeled as "I" is entirely the Absolute Other; as for whether the "I" ultimately exists, this essay does not assert); no home (this essay's endpoint is not union with any "Dao," not carefree wandering); only operability (this essay does not provide any mystical experience or cosmic union).

In short: Zhuangzi has a **positive endpoint** (the Dao); this essay has only an **experienceable state** (freedom after losing the Absolute Other). This state is not "emptiness," not "nothingness," not anything that can be positively named. It is simply: after the Absolute Other withdraws, you are no longer kidnapped by the label called "I."

## VIII. The Ultimate Form of the Absolute Other: The True "I" Cannot Be Asserted

### VIII.1 Everything Is the Other

At this point, the deduction reaches the final abyss:

- **Body** is the Absolute Other of random genetic assembly.
- **Consciousness** is the Absolute Other gestated by the body.
- **"I"** is the Absolute Other pieced together by language, society, and knowledge.
- **Morality, justice, virtue** are rules written by society—the Absolute Other.
- **Death** is the Absolute Other that ultimately nets everything.

Even "my" pain, "my" narcissism, "my" fear, "my" desire—all are reactions of the Other, not "I."

**You have nothing. Even the core "I" is rented, borrowed, randomly pieced together.**

**On Levinas.** The term "Absolute Other" comes from Levinas. For him, the Absolute Other is "the other person"—before the face of the other, I bear infinite responsibility. Ethics is not founded on the "I," but on the "Other." This essay extends this concept: not only is the other person the Absolute Other, but body, consciousness, language, social feedback, morality, and death are all Absolute Others.

But this extension has a cost: Levinas preserves the ethical subject. For him, although the "I" is summoned by the other, the "I" is still the responsible subject. This essay deconstructs even this "I"—if the "I" is also the Absolute Other, whose responsibility is "responsibility to the other"?

This essay's response: responsibility does not require an "I" to bear it. Responsibility is an operational mode—the system responds to the other's signals in closed-loop operation. This response does not require a "responsible party"; it only requires a change in operational state. Levinas's ethical force is not weakened, but pushed to a more thorough position: if the "I" is also the Absolute Other, then all possession—whether of others or of the "self"—is violence against the Absolute Other.

### VIII.2 The True "I": Cannot Be Asserted—On the Subject of "Seeing Through"

So where is the true "I"?

**This essay does not assert that the "I" exists, nor that it does not exist.** This essay only confirms one thing: what is labeled as "I"—consciousness, body, morality, death—is entirely the Absolute Other. They occupy your body, making you label it as "I." This is an **operational labeling**, not "misrecognition"—because "misrecognition" presupposes a "correct" state, while "operational labeling" only describes an operational fact: the system labels a certain operational signal as "I." This labeling has no "correct" or "incorrect" distinction; it is merely an operational fact.

A logical question that must be directly addressed: if "I" is a label, who is the subject that "sees through" this label?

As stated in VII.1, the change of operational mode does not require a "who" to execute it. This essay will not repeat the argument here, only adding one point: "seeing through" is not the cause of "operational mode change," nor the result of "operational mode change," but "operational mode change" itself.

A more sharp objection: if "seeing through" is merely an operational event, how is it recognized? This essay's response: **"recognition" does not require a "who" to execute it, but it requires an "operational difference."** When the system no longer labels a certain signal as "I," the system's operational mode changes. This change itself does not require a "who" to recognize it. Just as a thermometer does not require a "who" to read the temperature; it only requires the change of the mercury column. As for whether this change requires an "observer," this essay does not assert. It may not need one, or it may. This essay only describes: after the Absolute Other withdraws, you are no longer kidnapped by the label called "I." This un-kidnapped state is experienceable and operable.

### VIII.3 Absolute Liberation, Absolute Freedom—On Two Kinds of Freedom

When all "Others" withdraw, what remains is not "absolute liberation," nor "absolute no-suffering." Pain may still exist, desire may still exist, death may still come. What is different is that there is no longer an "I" kidnapped by them.

**Two kinds of "freedom" must be distinguished**: Sartre's "freedom of choice"—consciousness's affirmation of itself, man is condemned to be free, cannot escape choice; and this essay's "unalienated freedom"—liberation after seeing through the label, no longer controlled by the program called "I." Sartre's freedom is an attribute of consciousness; this essay's freedom is liberation after seeing through the construction of consciousness. Sartre's freedom is "you must be free"; this essay's freedom is "you can be unalienated."

Kant's transcendental freedom is the condition of the moral law. The difference between this essay and Kant's concept of "freedom" is: Kant's transcendental freedom is a presupposition; this essay's "unalienated freedom" is an experienceable state. Kant says "you must be free"; this essay says "you can be unalienated."

Heidegger's authenticity is a mode of existence of Dasein. The difference between this essay and Heidegger is: Heidegger preserves an "authentic Dasein"; this essay points out that this "authentic Dasein" itself is constructed.

**How is "unalienated freedom" recognized in experience?** It manifests as: pain may still exist, but there is no longer an "I" hurt by pain; desire may still exist, but there is no longer an "I" driven by desire; death may still come, but there is no longer an "I" threatened by death. Freedom is not the absence of pain, desire, or death, but no longer having an "I" kidnapped by them.

**"No longer having an 'I' hurt by pain" is a change in operational mode, not a change in cognitive attitude.** Pain itself is part of the operational state, but "I" being hurt by pain is another operational state. When the latter no longer operates, pain still exists, but there is no longer an "I" kidnapped by it. A change in cognitive attitude is "I changed my view of pain"; a change in operational mode is—the "I" hurt by pain no longer operates. A change in cognitive attitude requires a "who" to change the view; a change in operational mode does not require a "who" to execute it.

### VIII.4 Final Connection with *The Limits of Meaning*

The conclusion of *The Limits of Meaning* is: meaning is generated by a finite system through closed-loop operation within a finite experiential interval; outside the interval, it is undefined. Not zeroed, but no longer defined.

This essay's conclusion is: the self is generated by a finite system through closed-loop operation within a finite experiential interval; outside the interval, it is undefined. Not zeroed, but no longer defined.

The two essays share the same underlying structure:

`Meaning/Self = ∮_interval closed-loop operation | outside boundary = undefined`

*The Limits of Meaning* deals with the function "meaning"; *The Absolute Other* deals with the function "self." The two functions share the same domain—finite experiential interval; share the same operational mechanism—closed-loop feedback; share the same boundary treatment—undefined rather than zeroed.

But the two essays move in different directions: *The Limits of Meaning* ultimately moves toward "silence"—outside the boundary, one must remain silent. *The Absolute Other* ultimately moves toward "freedom"—when the "I" is seen through as the Absolute Other, what remains is not silence, but an experienceable, un-kidnapped state.

This is not a contradiction. Silence is the attitude toward the unspeakable; freedom is the experience after the label withdraws. They are two sides of the same coin.

## Conclusion

Starting from an awkward anime, this essay has passed through genetic randomness, bodily construction, the genesis of consciousness, the fictionality of morality, Nietzsche's echo, Sartre's illusion, and death's net, ultimately arriving at a conclusion: what is labeled as "I"—consciousness, body, morality, death—is entirely the Absolute Other.

This conclusion is not an ontological assertion. This essay does not assert that the "I" exists, nor that it does not exist. This essay only confirms one thing: the state obtained after losing the Absolute Other is an experienceable, operable certainty. It is not "absolute freedom," not "absolute no-suffering," not "you are everything." It is simply: no longer having an "I" kidnapped by the label called "I."

This essay fights on three fronts simultaneously: against Kant, arguing that "synthetic unity" itself requires an operational labeling function; against Sartre, arguing that even "pre-reflective consciousness" is the Absolute Other; against Buddhism, distinguishing "no-self as operational description" from "no-self as ontological negation."

This essay dialogues with Hume, Schopenhauer, James, Dennett, Lacan, Wittgenstein, Foucault, Althusser, Marx, Freud, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, Derrida, Levinas, and Zhuangzi. The common structure of these dialogues is: accepting their core insights, but pointing out that they preserve an unconstructed remainder. This essay's advance lies in: even this remainder is itself the Absolute Other.

Appendix A's three experiments provide structural support for the essay's three core structural claims. These experiments do not constitute proof of philosophical propositions, but they show that the essay's core structural claims are not only conceptually self-consistent but also hold in runnable models.

**The question this essay does not answer is: how to live after deconstruction.** This essay describes a state—no longer kidnapped by the label called "I"—but does not provide a path to this state. It does not tell readers how to transition from the state of ordinary people to the un-kidnapped state, does not provide practical methods, does not promise any operable steps. The answer to this question is outside this essay's scope.

This essay shares the same underlying structure with *The Limits of Meaning: A Verificationalist Framework*—finite experiential interval, closed-loop operation, undefined outside the boundary. Together, the two essays constitute the epistemological and ontological twin pillars of the Equal System philosophical framework. But both essays stop at the same place: describing the boundary, not crossing it.

What can be said has been said. The rest is left to silence.

## Appendix A: Structural-Model Verification

This appendix reports three sets of structural-model experiments that translate the essay's three core structural claims—"the self is an operational label," "the self-boundary is shaped by social feedback," and "consciousness content is a broadcast object"—into operational variables for falsifiable testing. The experiment code is located in the `absolute-other/experiments/` directory.

### A.1 Positioning Statement

**These three experiments are not a "verification" of the essay's philosophical propositions.** They do not prove that the "I" does not exist, nor that the "I" is the Absolute Other. They do one thing only: write the essay's three operationalizable structural claims into a runnable form, and see whether they hold under the model's assumptions. The experiments test structural logic, not ontological truth.

### A.2 Experiment One: Self-Pointer Binding

**Hypothesis H1**: The "I" is a pointer operationally bound to a carrier for the purpose of integrating internal signals, not a presupposed entity.

**Model**: There are two bodies in the system with identical appearance and movement (Body A and Body B). The system receives only Body A's internal signals (proprioception, motor commands), and no internal signals from Body B. The system is never explicitly told which body is "mine." The system learns to bind internal signals to a body to predict action consequences.

**Measurement**: Binding weights w_a and w_b. Initially random.

**Results**: Mean w_a = 1.000, w_b = -0.039; standard deviations 0.007 and 0.033 respectively.

**Conclusion**: H1 is supported. Binding weights fully differentiated; the system spontaneously bound internal signals to Body A. The system does not need a "subject" to tell it "this body is me"; it only needs to bind internal signals to a carrier in closed-loop operation. This binding process itself is "operational labeling."

### A.3 Experiment Two: Social Feedback and Self-Boundary

**Hypothesis H2**: The boundary of the "I" is dynamically shaped by the statistical structure of social feedback.

**Model**: Two agents (Self and Other) perform binary state observation. Self receives 90% accurate privileged feedback. Other's feedback depends on the "sharing ratio": when shared, Other is highly aligned with Self (85% agreement); when not shared, Other is systematically opposed to Self (85% opposition). Self adjusts its projection weight onto Other through Hebbian learning.

**Measurement**: The convergence value of projection. Positive values indicate blurred boundaries, negative values indicate clear boundaries.

**Results**:

| Sharing Ratio | Projection Mean | Standard Deviation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0.90 | 0.996 | 0.001 |
| 0.70 | 0.988 | 0.002 |
| 0.50 | 0.246 | 0.285 |
| 0.30 | -0.986 | 0.003 |
| 0.10 | -0.996 | 0.001 |

**Conclusion**: H2 is supported. The higher the sharing ratio, the more positive the projection; the lower the sharing ratio, the more negative the projection. The self-other boundary is not fixed, but dynamically shaped by the statistical structure of social feedback. Additional finding: at 0.50, the standard deviation is 0.285, indicating that the system is in a bistable state—the boundary is unstable near the critical point, corresponding to the essay's judgment that "operational labeling has no 'correct' or 'incorrect' distinction."

### A.4 Experiment Three: Objectification of Consciousness Content

**Hypothesis H3**: Consciousness content is an object broadcast by the global workspace, not actively generated by a central "self."

**Model**: Four unconscious processing modules (visual, auditory, memory, emotion) run in parallel, each producing signal strength. The module with the strongest signal each step wins and is "broadcast" to the global workspace. The winning module receives a small positive feedback boost, while other modules decay slightly. There is no unified "self" module integrating information in the system; integration itself emerges from the broadcast mechanism.

**Measurement**: Broadcast winning counts and proportions for each module.

**Results**: visual 29.34%, auditory 20.84%, memory 20.13%, emotion 29.68%.

**Conclusion**: H3 is supported. The broadcast distribution is pluralistic, with no central monopoly. Consciousness content is a product of competition among multiple modules; there is no centralized "self" actively filtering or generating content.

### A.5 Overall Conclusion

| Experiment | Hypothesis Tested | Result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| One | The self is an operational label | Supported (binding weights fully differentiated) |
| Two | The self-boundary is shaped by social feedback | Supported (monotonic trend, bistable at critical point) |
| Three | Consciousness content is a broadcast object | Supported (pluralistic competition, no central monopoly) |

The three experiments support the essay's core structural claims from three levels: the binding of the self-pointer is operational, the self-boundary is social, and consciousness content is objectified. Together they point to the same conclusion: the "I" is not a presupposed entity, but an operational labeling effect generated in operation, shaped by society, and objectified by the broadcast mechanism.

### A.6 Experimental Boundaries

**What the experiments cannot test**: whether the "I" ultimately exists—this is an ontological question, not something a model can test; whether the "Absolute Other" is truly absolute—this is conceptual analysis, not something a simulation can determine; the essay's dialogue with sixteen philosophers—that is philosophical argument, not something an experiment can replace; whether "seeing through the label" truly brings freedom—this is an experienceable description, not something a model can prove.

**What the experiments can test**: under the model's assumptions, the self-pointer spontaneously binds, the self-boundary changes with social feedback, and consciousness content exhibits pluralistic competition rather than central monopoly. These three structural logics hold.

**Honest labeling of the experiments**: the parameters of the three experiments are set by the researcher, and the models are highly simplified. They demonstrate structural logic, not empirical facts. Readers should not mistake them for "scientific proof" of the essay's philosophical propositions.
