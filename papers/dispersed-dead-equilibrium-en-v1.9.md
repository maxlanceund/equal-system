# Dispersed Dead Equilibrium: The Structural Form of Social Reproduction in China and a Comparison with Japan and South Korea

---

## Abstract

This paper distinguishes two forms of damage accumulation: concentrated breakthrough and dispersed breakthrough. The low fertility rates in Japan and South Korea represent concentrated breakthrough—the costs of social reproduction are concentrated onto families, and when the family's ROE collapses, the marriage and fertility valves close simultaneously. China's damage accumulation is dispersed breakthrough—costs are dispersed across multiple accounts: hukou, land, healthcare, pensions, and savings. No single account can independently explain the fertility decline.

This paper proposes the concept of "dispersed dead equilibrium" as Form C, a variant of the "scarred zombie equilibrium" (Form B) in *Dead Equilibrium*. Dispersal has two effects in opposite directions: buffering (delays collapse, but with a threshold) and accelerated accumulation (no threshold, continuously operative).

Cross-national panel regressions (8 countries, 1995–2023) show that the China interaction term is significant in the baseline model, but placebo tests indicate that this significance is insensitive to label assignment—after random label assignment, 88% of the results are more extreme than the actual China result. **Cross-national regression cannot serve as core evidence for "China's uniqueness"; it is merely descriptive reference.**

The core evidence of this paper consists of four traceable damage indicators and one buffer indicator. The four damage indicators—hukou gap, land transfer rate, rural pension replacement rate, and out-of-pocket health expenditure—show that the accounts are accumulating in a dispersed manner. The one buffer indicator—national saving rate (including corporate/government saving, an indirect proxy for household buffering)—shows that buffering still has macro-level support. **Together they indicate: "buffering is still holding, but thinning."**

**However, the framework of this paper is cautionary, not diagnostic.** Positive aspects in the data—the bottom 50% share stabilizing after 2005, a still-high saving rate, and a fertility rate above South Korea's—indicate that buffering is still functioning. **China's risk is not "currently collapsing," but "buffering is still holding, yet thinning; if buffering thins below the threshold, the form of collapse will be more abrupt than South Korea's"—this is a comparison based on mechanistic assumptions, not on historical data.**

**Keywords**: dispersed dead equilibrium; social reproduction; buffer threshold; cumulative damage; cross-national comparison

## I. Introduction

### 1.1 Research Question

In 2024, South Korea's official total fertility rate was 0.75, while China's is estimated by scholars at approximately 1.0 (range 0.90–1.09). From the perspective of current flows, China has not yet reached South Korea's low, and an optimistic reading of "slow decline" persists. This paper questions the econometric basis of this reading: fertility is a current flow, while system health is a stock. Current flows not hitting bottom does not mean stock damage is lighter.

The difference between concentrated and dispersed breakthrough lies not in the total amount of damage, but in its observability and attributability. In concentrated breakthrough, damage is concentrated in a single observable account (e.g., South Korea's marriage threshold, Japan's erosion of lifetime employment), with large indicator fluctuations, clear attribution paths, and well-defined policy targets. In dispersed breakthrough, damage is spread across multiple low-visibility accounts (hukou gap, out-of-pocket health expenditure, thinning land fallback, low pension replacement rate); fluctuations in any single account are insufficient to trigger policy responses, but the stock damage accumulated in parallel across multiple accounts may be larger. Low observability does not mean slow accumulation; blurred attribution does not mean light damage.

The research question of this paper is: How does the form of damage accumulation in China's social reproduction differ from the concentrated breakthrough in Japan and South Korea? Does dispersed accumulation imply lower or higher collapse risk?

### 1.2 Core Propositions

**Proposition 1 (Form Difference)**: The damage accumulation in Japan and South Korea is concentrated breakthrough—the state concentrated the costs of social reproduction onto families during compressed industrialization, and when the family's ROE collapsed, the marriage and fertility valves closed simultaneously. China's damage accumulation is dispersed breakthrough—costs are dispersed across multiple accounts: hukou, land, healthcare, pensions, and savings, with no single account independently explaining the fertility decline.

**Proposition 2 (Buffer Threshold)**: Dispersal has two effects. The buffering effect delays collapse but has a threshold: when buffer strength falls below a critical value, China, due to faster accumulation, collapses earlier than concentrated-breakthrough countries. The accelerated accumulation effect has no threshold and operates continuously.

**Proposition 3 (Risk Implication)**: China's core risk is not "slow decline" but "sudden collapse when buffers are exhausted." Once the two traditional buffers—family intergenerational support and rural land—are simultaneously eroded by land transfer, declining fertility, and aging, China may not decline gradually but collapse more abruptly than South Korea.

**Proposition 4 (Conditionality)**: The above judgments are conditional warnings, not current diagnoses. Positive aspects in the data (share stabilization, high saving rate, fertility rate above South Korea's) indicate that buffering is still functioning. This paper's judgment is that "if buffering thins below the threshold, the form of collapse will be more abrupt than South Korea's"—this is a mechanistic assumption, not a historical data comparison.

### 1.3 Relationship to the Literature

This paper engages with three strands of literature.

**First strand: Structural explanations of low fertility.** Existing research emphasizes familialist welfare regimes, rigid gender divisions of labor, and housing and education costs (Esping-Andersen, 1999; McDonald, 2006; Thévenon & Gauthier, 2011). This paper does not deny these mechanisms but points out that they mainly explain concentrated breakthrough—the wound is in one place, visible, attributable. China's problem is not that these mechanisms are absent, but that they are dispersed across multiple accounts, with no single concentrated visible wound.

**Second strand: Compressed modernity and familialism.** Korean studies point out that compressed modernity privatized social reproduction to families (Chang, 2010; Song, 2014). This paper continues this judgment but distinguishes between concentrated transfer (South Korea) and dispersed transfer (China), arguing that the accumulation speed and collapse form of dispersed transfer differ.

**Third strand: Cumulative damage and path dependence.** This paper continues the methodological position of *Structure and Data* (Zhong, 2026a) and *Dead Equilibrium* (Zhong, 2026b): macro data cannot infer micro structures backward, and cumulative damage is recorded in stocks rather than flows. The increment of this paper is to formalize cumulative damage into two comparable types—concentrated breakthrough and dispersed breakthrough—and to provide testable econometric implications.

**Difference from the welfare fragmentation literature**: "Dispersed breakthrough" is related to but distinct from "welfare fragmentation." Fragmentation describes the rupture of institutional supply (incomplete policy coverage, urban-rural divide, hukou restrictions) and is a cause; dispersed breakthrough describes the form of damage accumulation (parallel bleeding across multiple accounts, no single attributable source) and is a consequence. The increment of this paper is not the discovery of fragmentation, but linking fragmentation to the form of collapse.

### 1.4 Identification Strategy and Contributions

The identification strategy of this paper does not rely on causal identification, nor does it rely on cross-national regression as core evidence. The results and limitations of cross-national regression are detailed in Section 5.2. The core evidence comes from four traceable damage indicators and one buffer indicator (see Section VI).

This paper makes three contributions. First, it proposes the concept of dispersed dead equilibrium and provides a comparable definition vis-à-vis concentrated breakthrough. Second, it uses traceable institutional indicators for descriptive manifestation, providing observable evidence for the dispersed accumulation of D_accum. Third, it uses the buffer threshold mechanism to explain the abruptness of collapse forms in dispersed-breakthrough countries, and proposes the existence of a threshold as a mechanistic assumption.

Section V reports cross-national econometric results (descriptive reference). Section VI presents institutional indicator manifestation (core evidence). Section VII discusses buffer thresholds and collapse forms (including positive aspects and conditionality). Section VIII discusses boundaries. Section IX concludes.

## II. Literature and Concepts

### 2.1 Concentrated Breakthrough: Japan and South Korea Literature

The mainstream explanation for South Korea's low fertility is compressed modernity and familialism. Chang (2010) points out that South Korea completed industrialization in an extremely short period, with the state privatizing the costs of social reproduction to families, making the family an "invisible fiscal branch." Song (2014) further argues that South Korea's non-marital fertility rate is extremely low (about 3%), marriage is an absolute prerequisite for fertility, and Seoul's housing price-to-income ratio exceeds 10 times while chaebol regular jobs account for only 10%, making marriage—the "valve"—economically irrational. McDonald's (2006) gender equity theory, though based on European data, has been widely applied to East Asian research; applied to Japan, the rigidity of the family division of labor has not adjusted as female labor force participation rose, creating a double bind of "the old script not invalidated, the new script not written." Thévenon & Gauthier's (2011) cross-national comparison identifies family policy fragmentation and insufficient cash support as institutional roots of low fertility.

The common feature of this literature is: the wound is concentrated in one place—marriage valve, gender division of labor, family policy. Visible, attributable, with clear policy targets. This paper calls this concentrated breakthrough.

### 2.2 Dispersed Breakthrough: China Literature

Research on China's low fertility is dispersed across multiple subfields.

**Fragmentation of fertility support policies.** Research on China's fertility support policies generally points out that policy supply suffers from urban-rural divides and hukou restrictions, insufficient cross-departmental coordination among policy-making bodies, and significant regional differences in policy content. This fragmented pattern means that no single policy can cover the entire reproductive-age population, and no single policy can independently explain the fertility decline.

**Platform economy and flexible employment.** Zhang Yinghua points out that "the close employer-employee relationship has been replaced by the loose platform-worker relationship, with hundreds of millions of workers engaged in 'de-employerized' and 'gigized' work" (Zhang, 2025). Wu Yutong et al. provide an econometric analysis of pension insurance participation among informal workers (Wu et al., 2024). Li Gan and Dong Baohua analyze the institutional predicament of the "no-employer" status from the perspective of social insurance contribution mechanisms (Li & Dong, 2024).

**Hukou and land finance.** Ruan (2024) argues that the dual land ownership system leads to the emergence of large quantities of low-cost informal housing in urban villages, which reduces the short-term cost of labor reproduction and diminishes workers' demand for state-defined urban rights; meanwhile, the importance of land transfer revenue prompts local governments to bind the distribution of urban welfare rights to citizens' formal property rights and residential status, thereby obstructing substantive hukou reform. This mechanism simultaneously explains two phenomena: "land as an implicit buffer" and the "hukou gap."

The common feature of this literature is: the wound is not in one place. Hukou, land, healthcare, pensions, savings—each account is bleeding, but no single account can independently explain "why fertility is declining." This paper calls this dispersed breakthrough.

### 2.3 Conceptual Definition: From Scarred Zombie Equilibrium to Dispersed Dead Equilibrium

*Dead Equilibrium* proposes Form B (scarred zombie equilibrium): people are alive, but cooperation, trust, and morality have zeroed out, and the ceiling has been permanently punctured by history. This paper proposes Form C (dispersed dead equilibrium): damage is not concentrated in a single visible wound but is dispersed across multiple accounts and accumulates continuously; there is no concentrated repair mechanism; the form of collapse depends on the buffer threshold rather than on the accumulation speed itself.

**Difference from Form B**: In Form B, damage is concentrated in trust and moral ceilings; in Form C, damage is dispersed across hukou, land, healthcare, pensions, and savings accounts. Form B's collapse is "alive but relationships are dead"; Form C's collapse is "looks okay but accounts suddenly break through."

**Difference from concentrated breakthrough**: Concentrated breakthrough is rapid accumulation in a single account, attributable, with policy targets, and gradual visible collapse. Dispersed breakthrough is slow parallel accumulation across multiple accounts, difficult to attribute, with no single target, and sudden breakthrough.

**Definition of "dispersal"**: "Dispersal" in this paper specifically refers to damage accumulating in parallel across multiple accounts with no single account attributable, not to the atomization of social relations, nor to the fragmentation of institutional supply. Atomization describes the rupture of social bonds; fragmentation describes the rupture of institutional supply; dispersal describes the form of damage accumulation—three concepts at different levels. Fragmentation is a cause at the institutional supply level; dispersal is a consequence at the damage accumulation level; they are related but at different levels.

**Discrimination criterion**: If a single account contributes more than 50% of total damage, it is concentrated breakthrough (Form B); if multiple accounts bleed simultaneously with no single account exceeding 50%, it is dispersed breakthrough (Form C). The 50% is a preliminary discrimination threshold; finer discrimination requires multi-account weight estimation; the operational measurement of this criterion requires future work, and this paper temporarily substitutes qualitative description (a rough judgment is provided at the end of Section VI).

## III. Mechanism Framework

### 3.1 Three Dimensions

**Thin repair**: Social expenditure accounts for about 9% of GDP (OECD average 20–30%), and out-of-pocket health expenditure is 27.5% (Japan about 12%). Repair mechanisms exist but are thin.

**Deep transfer**: The hukou gap of 18 pp involves over 250 million people; the land transfer rate is 37.2% (in 2008, only 2.2% had no land to return to). Cost transfer penetrates rural land and hukou segmentation—deep.

**High dispersal**: Four damage indicators—hukou gap, out-of-pocket health expenditure, land transfer, and rural pensions—accumulate in parallel, with no single nameable wound. Indicators widely cited in public discussion, such as social insurance dropout rates and flexible employment scale, are not included in core evidence due to inconsistent definitions.

### 3.2 Mechanistic Assumption of the Buffer Threshold

Let buffer strength be B and accumulation speed be A. Dispersal affects collapse time T through two channels: B delays collapse, A accelerates collapse. If B ≥ B* (threshold), T_dispersed > T_concentrated; if B < B*, T_dispersed < T_concentrated. A has no threshold and operates continuously.

**Mechanistic assumption, not proven conclusion**: This paper proposes the threshold effect as a mechanistic assumption—when buffer strength is above a certain threshold, dispersed countries collapse later than concentrated-breakthrough countries; when below, the opposite. There is no smooth transition in between. This is not "China declining slowly," but "if the buffer is strong enough, it holds; if the buffer breaks, collapse is more severe." Rigorous testing of this assumption (parameter calibration, numerical simulation) requires future work; this paper does not rely on specific numerical values and proposes it only as an assumption to organize the discussion in Section VII.

### 3.3 Formal Connection to *Dead Equilibrium*

*Dead Equilibrium*'s D_social(t) = ∫E(τ)/E_crit dτ measures cumulative damage. This paper's dispersed dead equilibrium corresponds to the parallel accumulation of D_social across multiple accounts: D_total = Σ D_k, where k = hukou, land, healthcare, pensions, savings. Concentrated breakthrough is rapid accumulation in a single account; dispersed breakthrough is slow parallel accumulation across multiple accounts. Total amounts may be similar, but collapse forms differ: single account breaks through first → concentrated collapse; multiple accounts bleed simultaneously → sudden breakthrough.

This is an analogy to *Dead Equilibrium*'s D_social, not a strict formal derivation; the measurement and aggregation rules for each D_k require future work.

## IV. Data and Identification Strategy

### 4.1 Data

**Cross-national panel**: World Bank World Development Indicators, 8 countries (CHN, JPN, KOR, USA, DEU, SWE, BRA, MEX), 1995–2023, 162 observations. Dependent variable: bottom 50% income share = lowest 20% + second 20% + third 20% × 0.5. Independent variables: unemployment rate, industry value added as % of GDP, female labor force participation rate, employment-to-population ratio.

**Sample selection criteria**: World Bank income share data covering 1995–2023, GDP per capita above 5,000 USD (2015 constant prices), and including candidate cases of concentrated breakthrough (Japan, South Korea) and dispersed breakthrough (China). The 8 countries are the largest intersection satisfying these conditions. The US, Germany, and Sweden are share-contraction cases under weak redistribution; Brazil and Mexico are share-increase cases under strong redistribution, serving as contrasts; Japan and South Korea are concentrated-breakthrough cases; China is a dispersed-breakthrough case.

**China institutional indicators**: National Bureau of Statistics, Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security bulletins, Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs statistics, National Health Commission bulletins, *China Health Economics* calculations. Used for descriptive manifestation in Section VI, not entering regressions.

### 4.2 Identification Strategy

**Baseline model**:

share50_it = α_i + γ_t + β1·unemp_it + β2·ind_it + β3·lfpr_f_it + β4·emp_it + δ1·(China_i×ind_it) + δ2·(China_i×unemp_it) + ε_it

where α_i is country fixed effects and γ_t is year fixed effects. δ1 and δ2 identify differences in association patterns between China and the sample-average country when industry share and unemployment change.

**Identification assumptions and threats**:

1. **Parallel trends assumption**: Causal interpretation of δ1 and δ2 requires that China and the sample-average country would have parallel trends absent institutional differences. This paper does not make causal interpretations; δ1 and δ2 are only directional manifestations.
2. **Omitted variables**: Labor law enforcement intensity, union density, and social expenditure levels are not controlled. These variables may correlate with the China interaction term, biasing δ1 and δ2. This is noted in the limitations.
3. **Reverse causality**: The employment-to-population ratio coefficient is significantly negative, possibly reflecting reverse causality (low share → families forced to increase employment). This paper does not interpret the causal direction of this coefficient.
4. **Collinearity**: The correlation between female labor force participation and year trend is −0.988. After adding year fixed effects in Model 3, the female labor participation coefficient increases (+1.04), indicating instability due to collinearity. This is noted but not corrected.
5. **Label sensitivity**: Placebo tests (see Section 5.2 R3) show that the significance of the interaction term is insensitive to the assignment of the "China" label and cannot serve as core evidence for "China's uniqueness."

### 4.3 Robustness Design

**R1: Drop China**. If main effects remain largely unchanged after dropping China, main effects are not driven by a single country.

**R2: Two-way fixed effects**. Add year fixed effects to absorb global common trends and test whether the interaction term is driven by trends.

**R3: Placebo tests**. Two designs—complete randomization and block permutation—test whether the significance of the interaction term can be replicated by random labels. Block permutation preserves each country's time-series structure and is stricter than complete randomization.

Model 1 shows that after controlling for country fixed effects, industry share is positively correlated with the bottom share (+0.20, t=3.12), and unemployment is negatively correlated (−0.50, t=−5.55). After randomly assigning the "China" label to other countries, the significant frequencies of the interaction term are 73.0% (industry share) and 35.0% (unemployment), and the actual China t-values fall at the 12.0%/12.5% quantiles of the pseudo-China distribution. Therefore, interaction results serve only as descriptive reference and do not enter core argumentation.**

### 5.2 Robustness

**R1 (Drop China)**: Comparing Model 4 with Model 2, the main effects change as follows: industry share +0.36→+0.36, unemployment −0.58→−0.59, female labor participation +0.52→+0.52, employment-to-population ratio −1.09→−1.10. All four main effects change by no more than 0.01, indicating that main effects do not depend on China and are not driven by a single country. But the uniqueness of the interaction term itself requires R3 testing; R3 results show that the interaction term is insensitive to label assignment and cannot serve as evidence for "China's uniqueness."

**R2 (Two-way FE)**: After adding year fixed effects in Model 3, China × industry share shrinks from −0.44 to −0.25 (t=−2.54), and China × unemployment from −3.42 to −1.75 (t=−2.72). The shrinkage is about 40–50%, but both remain significant at the 5% level. This indicates that part of Model 2's significance is contributed by year trends, but the difference between China and the sample-average country still holds after controlling for global trends.

**R3 (Placebo tests)**: Two designs. Block permutation preserves each country's complete time series and only permutes country labels, making it stricter than complete randomization—complete randomization may underestimate label sensitivity.

**Complete randomization**: Randomly assign the "China" label to other countries, repeating 200 times. The significant frequency of pseudo-China × industry share is 69.5%, and pseudo-China × unemployment is 30.0%. Pseudo-China t-value ranges are [−3.33, 4.30] and [−2.72, 4.33], respectively.

**Block permutation**: Repeat 200 times. The significant frequency of pseudo-China × industry share is 73.0%, and pseudo-China × unemployment is 35.0%. Pseudo-China t-value ranges are [−5.03, 4.30] and [−6.45, 4.33], with the actual China t-values exactly at the lower bound, at quantiles of only 12.0%/12.5%—i.e., 88% of pseudo-China t-values are more extreme than the actual China values.

**Conclusion: Under block permutation, the actual China t-values are at only the 12% quantile, indicating that the significance of "China × industry share" and "China × unemployment" is insensitive to label assignment and cannot serve as evidence for "China's uniqueness." Cross-national regression results serve only as descriptive reference and do not enter core argumentation.**

**Distinguishing statistical uniqueness from form difference**: R1 and R3 together indicate that the data do not support the strong proposition that "China is statistically unique"—the interaction term is insensitive to label assignment, and main effects do not depend on China. But the core proposition of this paper is not "China is statistically unique" but "China's form of damage accumulation differs." Form difference does not require a significant interaction term for proof; it requires directional consistency of institutional indicators—the "damage accumulating in a dispersed manner, buffering still holding but thinning" shown by the five indicators in Section VI is the evidence base for the core proposition. Statistical uniqueness and form difference are two different things: the former relies on regression, the latter on institutional description. The negation by R1+R3 negates the former, not the latter.

### 5.3 Economic Significance

The China × unemployment two-way FE coefficient is −1.75. The within-sample standard deviation of unemployment is about 4 pp, so a one-standard-deviation shock corresponds to a 7 pp decline in the bottom share, about 29% of the sample mean (24.25%). The China × industry share coefficient is −0.25; the standard deviation of industry share is about 5 pp, so a one-standard-deviation shock corresponds to 1.25 pp, about 5% of the mean. **Economic significance calculations are for description only; because placebo tests are not passed, they do not enter core argumentation.**

### 5.4 Unsupported Hypotheses

The trend × industry share interaction is −0.0008 (t=−0.59), insignificant. The hypothesis that cumulative damage deepens over time is not supported by cross-national data. This hypothesis can only be descriptively manifested through Chinese institutional indicators, not through cross-national regression.

### 5.5 Econometric Limitations

**Narrow sample of countries**: 8 countries, 162 observations; the country dimension is only 8, and interaction term significance is sensitive to sample composition.

**Omitted variables**: Labor law enforcement intensity, union density, and social expenditure levels are not controlled. These variables may correlate with the China interaction term, biasing δ1 and δ2.

**Reverse causality**: The employment-to-population ratio coefficient is significantly negative in all models (−0.92 to −1.65), a counterintuitive direction that may reflect reverse causality (low share → families forced to increase employment). This paper does not interpret the causal direction.

**Collinearity**: The correlation between female labor force participation and year trend is −0.988. After adding year fixed effects in Model 3, the female labor participation coefficient increases from +0.52 to +1.04, indicating instability due to collinearity.

**Label sensitivity**: Placebo tests (Section 5.2 R3) show that the interaction term is insensitive to label assignment; cross-national regression cannot serve as core evidence.

## VII. Buffer Threshold and Collapse Forms

### 7.1 Mechanistic Assumption

Dispersal affects collapse time through two channels: buffering delays collapse (with a threshold), and accumulation accelerates collapse (without a threshold). Let buffer strength be B and accumulation speed be A. If B ≥ B*, dispersed countries collapse later than concentrated-breakthrough countries; if B < B*, dispersed countries, due to faster accumulation, collapse earlier than concentrated-breakthrough countries.

**Operational definition of "sudden"**: "Sudden" in this paper refers to a fertility decline exceeding 0.2 within a 5-year window (0.2 approximates South Korea's 2015–2020 decline), or a doubling of the social insurance dropout rate within 3 years. This is an operational definition, not a precise prediction. The threshold effect described above is a mechanistic assumption: when buffer strength is above a certain threshold, dispersed countries collapse later than concentrated-breakthrough countries; when below, the opposite. There is no smooth transition in between. Specific parameters and numerical values require future calibration; this paper does not rely on specific numerical values.

### 7.2 China's Buffers Are Thinning

**Land buffer**: Contracted land transfer rate is 37.2% (2024, Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs); in the 2008 financial crisis, only 2.2% had no land to return to (public report citation). The fallback is thinning. This comparison shows that the land buffer had a record of hard holding in 2008, but the rising transfer rate means the fallback of "returning home to farm" is narrowing.

**Household buffer (macro saving)**: The national saving rate of 55.5% provides a macro background for household buffering but cannot be directly equated with household buffering. The national saving rate of 55.5% (2024, calculated per NBS definition) indicates that overall economic saving remains high, providing a macro background for household buffering. But the gross national saving rate includes corporate/government saving and **cannot be directly equated with household buffering**—direct evidence of household buffering requires decomposition of the household saving rate. Meanwhile, the rural pension replacement rate is only 13.6% (urban-rural residents' pension insurance monthly benefit per capita of 246 yuan, MOHRSS 2024), raising doubts about the sustainability of intergenerational support—buffering is still there, but its "thickness" is being eroded by pension pressure.

**Institutional buffer**: The hukou gap of 18 pp (hukou urbanization rate ~50% vs. resident 67%, NBS 2023) involves over 250 million people excluded from the urban welfare system; out-of-pocket health expenditure is 27.5% (NHC 2024), far above Japan's ~12%. Institutional buffering does not cover the floating population, and service costs are transferred to families.

**Note**: Indicators widely cited in public discussion—social insurance dropout rate, flexible employment scale, household debt-to-income ratio, grandparent caregiving rate—are not used as core evidence because their sources are baseline surveys, institutional estimates, or brokerage calculations with inconsistent definitions (see Section VI note). The above three items—land transfer rate, rural pension replacement rate, hukou gap, out-of-pocket health expenditure, saving rate—all have clear official or academic calculation sources and constitute the evidence base for this paper's judgment that "buffers are thinning."

### 7.3 Meaning of Collapse Forms

Concentrated breakthrough: single account breaks through first → attributable → policy target → gradual collapse.
Dispersed breakthrough: multiple accounts bleed simultaneously → difficult to attribute → no single target → sudden breakthrough.

Dispersed breakthrough collapse is not a gradual process of a single account breaking through first, but a sudden failure after multiple accounts simultaneously approach critical values—the suddenness of failure comes from the lack of a single early warning indicator, not from a larger total amount of damage. Dispersal is not safety; it is accumulation without a single early warning indicator.

### 7.4 Positive Aspects and Conditionality

The framework of this paper is cautionary, but there are also positive aspects in the data that must be honestly presented. **These positive aspects do not negate the framework but limit its scope of application.**

**First, the bottom 50% share stabilized after 2005.** WID data show a decline from 27.0% in 1978 to 15.0% in 2005 (−12 pp), but only a further 1.3 pp decline from 2005 to 2023 (15.0%→13.7%). The World Bank definition is even clearer: the change from 1996 to 2022 is −0.25 pp, almost unchanged. This is not "accounts have been rotting continuously" but "rotting stopped at a certain position"—indicating that redistribution began to fill the gap (agricultural tax abolition, New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme, New Rural Pension Scheme, expansion of minimum living guarantees). Although insufficient, it held the bottom.

**Second, the national saving rate is 55.5% (2024, calculated per NBS definition).** This is indirect evidence that buffering still has macro support. Overall economic saving remains high, providing a background for household buffering—but the gross national saving rate includes corporate/government saving and cannot be directly equated with household buffering. The land transfer rate of 37.2% is "thinning," not "gone"; the saving rate of 55.5% is "still there at the macro level," not "households can hold on." The mechanistic assumption suggests that when buffer strength is above a certain threshold, collapse is delayed; China's buffer strength is now likely still above the threshold, but specific judgment requires decomposition of the household saving rate and household microdata.

**Third, the fertility rate is about 1.0–1.09 (scholarly estimate), higher than South Korea's 0.75.** The gap is not large, but the direction is "China has not yet reached South Korea's pit." The fact that current flows have not hit bottom is itself information—it indicates that buffering is still functioning and collapse has not yet arrived.

**Fourth, in the 2008 financial crisis, only 2.2% had no land to return to.** This is evidence that the land buffer "was once very thick." In the massive shock of 2008, the land buffer held, with only 2.2% having no land to farm. This shows that the buffer is not paper-thin; it has a record of hard holding.

**The precise meaning of these positive aspects for this paper's conclusion is: not refutation, but limitation.** This paper's judgment is not "China is heading toward sudden collapse" but "China is currently in an intermediate state where buffering is still functioning but thinning; if buffering thins below the threshold, the form of collapse will be more abrupt than South Korea's"—this is a mechanistic assumption, not a historical data comparison. **This is a conditional warning, not a current diagnosis.** The policy priority is not "firefighting" but "thickening buffers before they are exhausted"—but this paper does not provide policy recommendations because, as stated in Section 8.2, if the framework is taken up by policy to "calculate buffers," it may instead accelerate buffer depletion.

## VIII. Discussion and Boundaries

### 8.1 Relationship to *Dead Equilibrium* and *Structure and Data*

*Structure and Data* (Zhong, 2026a): Structure is irreversible; the third layer is the most rigid. Macro data cannot infer micro structures backward.
*Dead Equilibrium* (Zhong, 2026b): Time is irreversible; the ceiling is a historical integral. D_social entering 1 enters the irreversible zone.
This paper: Form difference; dispersed dead equilibrium is Form C.

The three papers share the same methodological foundation: macro data cannot infer micro structures backward, and cumulative damage is recorded in stocks rather than flows. *Structure and Data* addresses structural irreversibility; *Dead Equilibrium* addresses temporal irreversibility; this paper addresses form difference—the same dead equilibrium, with different institutions generating different forms.

### 8.2 Why No Policy Recommendations Are Provided (Author's Position, Not Academic Argument)

**The following is the author's position, not an academic argument, for readers' reference.** The framework of this paper has policy implications (expanding social insurance coverage, reducing out-of-pocket health expenditure, narrowing the hukou gap, protecting the land fallback function), but this paper does not present them as policy recommendations. The reason is not that policy should not act, but that once policy obtains a quantitative framework for "buffer thickness," it will most likely turn it into planning parameters of "how much buffer remains, how many years can it last," rather than the normative question of "should buffers be protected." Calculating is easy, doing is hard: calculating buffers is data work; thickening buffers is interest adjustment. The framework being calculated proves its diagnostic power; but being calculated as "how many more years can be squeezed" is policy's choice, not the framework's intent. This paper writes a diagnosis, not an operation manual.

### 8.3 Boundaries

1. **No causal identification**. Significant interaction terms only indicate differences in association patterns between China and the sample-average country on measurable proxies; placebo tests show that this difference is insensitive to label assignment and cannot serve as core evidence.

2. **Sample boundary**. 8 countries, 162 observations; the country dimension is narrow, and interaction term significance is sensitive to sample composition. World Bank income share data are incomplete (Japan from 2008, South Korea from 2006); early compressed industrialization breakthroughs cannot be manifested in cross-national regression.

3. **Proxy variable boundary**. D_accum (accumulation speed) has no annual cross-national series and cannot enter regression. Institutional indicators are single-year or short series and can only provide descriptive manifestation. The trend × industry share interaction is insignificant (t=−0.59); cumulative damage deepening over time is not supported by econometric evidence.

4. **Definitional boundary**. World Bank household survey definitions differ from WID tax + national accounts definitions. The World Bank shows China's share change from 1996 to 2022 as −0.25 pp (low-level stabilization); WID shows −13.3 pp from 1978 to 2023 (stabilization after drastic contraction). The two are not contradictory; they differ in windows—the World Bank missed the 1978–1995 contraction.

5. **Employment-to-population ratio coefficient is counterintuitive**. It is significantly negative in all models (−0.92 to −1.65), possibly reflecting reverse causality (low share → families forced to increase employment) or collinearity with female labor participation. The causal direction is not interpreted.

6. **Institutional indicator definitions**. All five indicators in Table 2 have clear official or academic calculation sources with traceable definitions. The national saving rate is the gross national saving rate (including corporate/government saving), serving as an indirect proxy for household buffering; direct evidence of household buffering requires decomposition of the household saving rate. Indicators widely cited in public discussion—social insurance dropout, flexible employment scale, household debt ratio, grandparent caregiving rate—are not included in core evidence due to inconsistent definitions and are used only as directional reference in the discussion.

7. **Conditionality boundary**. This paper is a cautionary framework, not a diagnostic conclusion. Positive aspects in the data (share stabilization, high saving rate, fertility rate above South Korea's) indicate that buffering is still functioning and collapse has not yet arrived. This paper judges that "if buffering thins below the threshold, the form of collapse will be more abrupt than South Korea's"—this is a mechanistic assumption, not a historical data comparison.

8. **Assumption-based boundary of the threshold mechanism**. The threshold effect in Sections 3.2 and 7.1 is a mechanistic assumption; specific parameter settings, numerical simulation, and rigorous testing require future calibration. This paper does not rely on specific numerical values.

9. **Empirical boundary of form comparison**. The "concentrated vs. dispersed" comparison in this paper is mainly at the conceptual level (2.1 vs. 2.2); empirical bilateral comparison (China-Japan-Korea institutional indicator comparison) requires future work. Table 2 provides indicators from the China side, used to manifest the "dispersed accumulation" form, not to prove "China is more dispersed than Japan and South Korea." The latter requires systematic comparison of corresponding Japanese and Korean indicators.

## IX. Conclusion

China is not a replica of Japan or South Korea. The low fertility in Japan and South Korea is concentrated breakthrough—the wound is in one place, visible, attributable. China's damage accumulation is dispersed breakthrough—the wound is dispersed across hukou, land, healthcare, pensions, and savings accounts, invisible, difficult to attribute.

Cross-national panel regression shows that the China interaction term is significant in the baseline model, but placebo tests indicate that this significance is insensitive to label assignment (under block permutation, pseudo-China significant frequencies are 73.0%/35.0%, and the actual China t-value quantile is only 12.0%/12.5%). Cross-national regression cannot serve as core evidence for "China's uniqueness." The core evidence of this paper is five traceable institutional indicators: four damage indicators—hukou gap 18 pp, land transfer rate 37.2%, rural pension replacement rate 13.6%, out-of-pocket health expenditure 27.5%—show that accounts are accumulating in a dispersed manner; one buffer indicator—national saving rate 55.5% (including corporate/government saving, an indirect proxy for household buffering)—shows that buffering still has macro support. **Five indicators, two directions—damage accumulating in a dispersed manner, buffering still holding but thinning.**

Dispersal has two effects in opposite directions: buffering (delays collapse, with a threshold) and accelerated accumulation (no threshold, continuously operative). **But the judgment of this paper is conditional, not diagnostic.** Positive aspects in the data—share stabilizing after 2005, saving rate 55.5%, fertility rate about 1.0–1.09 (above South Korea's 0.75)—indicate that buffering is still functioning and collapse has not yet arrived. **China's risk is not "currently collapsing," but "buffering is still holding, yet thinning; if buffering thins below the threshold, the form of collapse will be more abrupt than South Korea's"—this is a mechanistic assumption, not a historical data comparison.**

Structure has not moved, but the form differs; accounts are all rotting, but the way they rot determines the way they collapse. Buffering is still holding, but the risk of multiple accounts simultaneously approaching critical values is rising—this is not a prophecy, but a conditional warning.
