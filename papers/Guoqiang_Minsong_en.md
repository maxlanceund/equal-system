# Strong State, Relaxed People?
## — A Comparative Analysis Based on Fiscal Intermediation and Typology

**Core Argument**: There is no automatic transmission mechanism between a strong state and a relaxed people. Adopting a critical realist epistemological stance, this paper operationalizes "national relaxation" as a composite vector of "the share of actual disposable income per capita in GDP per capita" and "annual leisure time," and introduces "fiscal expenditure structure (share of livelihood-oriented expenditure)" as the core mediating variable through which macro-level state capacity transmits to micro-level individual welfare. By constructing a logical transmission path of "upstream rigid expenditures → midstream fiscal squeeze → downstream net household welfare," this paper argues that China's current "tension" is rooted in the inevitable cost of an "internally absorbed growth" model—namely, multiple rigid costs (security, geographic integration, external rules, etc.) cannot be externalized and must be internally absorbed through high public savings and low livelihood-oriented spending, which at the micro level manifests as long working hours, high savings, and low leisure. This predicament is further locked in by rapid aging and exhibits significant long-term structural features during the 2024–2035 window.

----------------------------------------------------------------

## I. Abstract

Does a "strong state" imply a "relaxed people"? The prerequisite for answering this question lies in operationalizing "relaxation." This paper defines "national relaxation" as a composite vector of "actual per capita disposable income as a share of per capita GDP" (reflecting the share of economic surplus retained by individuals) and "annual per capita leisure time" (reflecting temporal autonomy). Both are indispensable: high income without leisure (e.g., overwork) or leisure without income (e.g., structural unemployment) does not constitute "relaxation." This paper adopts a "leisure-priority" weighting logic, assuming that the marginal contribution of leisure time exceeds that of an equal proportional increase in disposable income—a judgment rooted in Sen's (1999) tradition that "temporal autonomy is the ultimate freedom."

This paper makes three core theoretical advances:
1. Clarifies the micro-transmission intermediary: it establishes the "share of livelihood-oriented fiscal expenditure" as the essential intermediary between the macro and micro levels, effectively avoiding the ecological fallacy of directly equating macro aggregates with micro experiences.
2. Revises key variable definitions: geographic connection costs are adjusted to "current net burden (estimated using a 30-50-year weighted depreciation range)"; external rule costs are adjusted to "net globalization benefit gap (from +2.0% to -1.5%)."
3. Introduces a typological distinction: proposes a preliminary typology of "internally absorbed growth" vs. "externally shared growth," taking cost-transfer capacity and demographic acceleration as key structural moderators.

Through a comparative analysis of the United States, Germany, Japan, and China, this paper finds that the "relaxation" of the U.S. and Germany partly stems from their "external sharing" paths (global seigniorage, NATO security outsourcing, EU labor market complementarity) that release fiscal space. China's "tension" is rooted in the inevitable cost of its "internal absorption" path—lacking new external cost-transfer channels during the phase of globalization retreat, and coupled with unprecedented rapid aging (total dependency ratio projected at 67% by 2035), the five-dimensional rigid costs cannot be diluted and must be internally absorbed by squeezing livelihood-oriented fiscal expenditure, which at the micro level corresponds to a high precautionary savings rate (44.5%) and a high expected discount factor (U≈0.60, used only as a contextual descriptive indicator, not as an independent causal variable).

Adopting a critical realist epistemological stance, this paper aims to reveal structural generative mechanisms rather than conduct statistical causal inference. Causal statements herein should be understood as "structural tendencies" rather than cross-temporal deterministic laws.

This paper's core academic contributions are:
- Establishing the "share of livelihood-oriented fiscal expenditure" as the necessary mediating variable for macro-to-micro transmission, opening the black box of fiscal allocation;
- Proposing the typological distinction between "internally absorbed" and "externally shared" growth, revealing the structural role of cost-transfer channels;
- Explicitly limiting the conclusions to the 2024–2035 window, acknowledging the potential nonlinear changes that technological variables (e.g., AI) may bring.

## II. Introduction: Between Macro Narratives and Micro Experiences

Opening Chinese social media, one can see grand narratives everywhere: "China wins again"—GDP second in the world, high-speed rail mileage number one, photovoltaic industry occupying 80% of the global market, new energy vehicle exports ranking first globally... However, switching back to real life, another voice is equally strong: ever-longer working hours, heavy mortgage burdens, the 35-year-old career crisis, educational involution, and persistently high savings rates. There is a clear gap between these micro experiences and the macro narratives.

This gap is the starting point of this paper.

I often wonder: is there really an inherent connection between a strong state and a relaxed people? If there is, where exactly does the problem lie? Can such a problem be explained by a framework? But this is only part of the picture. I can never see the entire landscape of this issue.

This paper's core judgment is that there is no automatic transmission from a strong state to relaxed individuals. From a "GDP giant" to "relaxed citizens," there must be a key intermediary step: the political allocation of the fiscal budget. The money earned by the state is first carved away by rigid expenditures on security, geographic integration, and external frictions; only the remainder enters livelihood-oriented allocation (social security, healthcare, education, leisure infrastructure), and finally reaches individuals' wallets and leisure time.

Therefore, this paper does not attempt to prove "whether China is strong" or "whether Chinese people should feel tired." Rather, it tries to construct an observable, comparable, and discussable analytical framework that breaks down the structural sources of "tiredness" and opens the black box of "fiscal intermediation."

The core question this paper seeks to answer is: How does the rigid expenditure structure at the state level transmit, through fiscal allocation mechanisms, into micro-level individual tension? And which structural factors play key moderating roles in this transmission?

The structure of this paper is as follows: Section II reviews existing literature and positions this paper's academic dialogue (Section III); Section III presents the theoretical framework, including transmission paths, intermediary mechanisms, typological distinctions, and methodological notes (Section IV); Section IV presents comparative empirical data of four countries, breaking down the five-dimensional variables and moderators (Section V); Section V focuses on the structural lock-in of China's "internal absorption transmission dilemma" (Section VI); Section VI concludes, summarizing findings, stating limitations, and listing falsification conditions (Sections VII-VIII).

## III. Literature Review and Academic Dialogue

On the relationship between "state capacity" and "people's welfare," existing research is mainly distributed across four traditions, but all have key blind spots:

1. **Development economics tradition** (Kuznets, 1955): argues that economic growth will ultimately benefit people's welfare through "trickle-down effects." The blind spot is that it ignores the political process of fiscal allocation—the fiscal increment from growth does not necessarily flow to livelihood sectors, but may be absorbed by security, infrastructure, or administrative costs.

2. **Welfare state theory** (Esping-Andersen, 1990): emphasizes the mediating role of distribution systems. Its contribution lies in revealing that "the same GDP, different welfare regimes produce different welfare experiences," but its analysis focuses on mature Western welfare states, lacking explanatory power for "fiscal squeeze in late-developing large countries under security and integration pressures."

3. **Critical political economy** (Piketty, 2014): focuses on the imbalance between capital returns and labor returns. However, this tradition mainly examines outcome inequality in income distribution, and rarely asks "how rigid state expenditures pre-cut the fiscal cake available for distribution."

4. **Chinese livelihood fiscal studies** (Gao Peiyong, 2018; Liu Shangxi et al., 2020): domestic fiscal scholars have accumulated rich work on defining and measuring "livelihood-oriented expenditure," but their analyses mostly focus on optimizing fiscal expenditure structures, and have not yet incorporated the share of livelihood expenditure as a mediating variable in the comparative political economy framework of "state capacity – individual welfare."

These theories are internally coherent, but I wonder whether they are missing some crucial link.

This paper does not deny the explanatory power of welfare regime typologies (Esping-Andersen, 1990) for cross-national differences in welfare spending levels. However, welfare regime theory struggles to explain significant differences in relaxation within the same regime type (e.g., both Germany and Japan are conservative welfare regimes, yet Germany's comprehensive relaxation rating is "very high" while Japan's is only "medium-low"). By introducing additional variables—cost-transfer channels (security outsourcing vs. self-reliance), stage of geographic connection costs (maintenance phase vs. peak phase), and demographic acceleration—the "five-dimensional transmission framework" proposed here provides complementary explanatory dimensions for such intra-regime differences.

On the above basis, this paper proposes a "five-dimensional fiscal intermediation transmission path," shifting the analytical focus from "whether growth benefits people's welfare" to "before growth is converted into welfare, which structural rigid expenditures pre-cut it, and how this pre-cutting transmits to micro individuals through fiscal intermediation." The unique contribution lies in incorporating four variables—geographic connection costs (net burden), external rule costs (dynamic gap), demographic dependency acceleration, and fiscal expenditure structure (livelihood share)—into a unified framework, and clearly distinguishing the two growth typologies of "internal absorption" and "external sharing."

## IV. Theoretical Framework

### 1. Logical Starting Point: Macro Cannot Directly Infer Micro

This paper adheres to the ontological stance of critical realism (Bhaskar, 1975), holding that social reality is constituted by deep, unobservable "generative mechanisms." There is no direct linear deterministic relationship between the macro-level "state expenditure structure" and the micro-level "individual relaxation," but rather a transmission through a series of mediating mechanisms (especially fiscal allocation mechanisms).

Causal statements in this paper should be understood as "structural tendencies"—that is, under specific historical conditions and institutional environments, certain structural factors tend to produce certain outcomes—rather than cross-temporal "deterministic laws." From the critical realist perspective, structures may be transformed by agents, but the conditions, paths, and timing of transformation are uncertain. This paper does not pursue statistical extrapolation, nor does it engage in counterfactual inference.

The traditional simplified narrative of "strong state → relaxed people" implicitly contains three untested assumptions, each of which this paper deconstructs:

- **Assumption 1**: The money earned by the state can all be used to improve people's welfare. **Reality**: Before entering livelihood allocation, state finances must first deduct rigid expenditures on security, geographic integration, and external frictions.

- **Assumption 2**: A strong state does not need to pay additional "security costs" and "demographic support costs." **Reality**: Security self-reliance and rapid aging are two enormous rigid expenditure curves.

- **Assumption 3**: National territorial integration is already complete and no longer requires massive continuous investment. **Reality**: China is still in the "peak investment phase" of geographic connection costs, with a current net burden significantly higher than that of the U.S. and Germany, which have already entered the maintenance phase.

**Terminological clarification**: This paper uses "relaxation" as a core theoretical concept, with "tiredness," "tension," and "low relaxation" as its popular equivalents for the opposite spectrum. All four terms refer to different intensities of the same conceptual continuum and are not typologically distinguished. The "tension" discussed here refers to structural conditions, not clinical burnout—the latter falls under individual psychopathology and is outside the scope of this analysis.

### 2. Logical Transmission Path

The "transmission path" constructed in this paper refers to logical structural tendencies, not statistical causal inference based on micro-panel data. It aims to reveal structural constraints rather than make deterministic attributions of individual behavior.

The core analytical framework is a **three-stage transmission path**, not a mathematical equation. It clearly distinguishes three analytical levels: macro, meso, and micro.

**Stage 1: Upstream — National Rigid Expenditure Pool**

Each year, the GDP created by the state first faces four types of rigid expenditure demands:
- Net security costs (military spending as % of GDP, net of transfer/sharing mechanisms);
- Net geographic connection burden (annual infrastructure investment minus 30-50 year weighted depreciation amortization);
- Net external rule gap (benefits during globalization boom minus expenditures during current barrier phase);
- Demographic dependency pressure (rigid demand for fiscal transfers from the non-working population).

These four constitute the "pre-deduction items" of the total fiscal pie.

**Stage 2: Midstream — Political Allocation of Fiscal Expenditure Structure**

After the pre-deductions, the remaining fiscal space enters the political allocation stage. The core variable at this stage is the "share of livelihood-oriented fiscal expenditure" (i.e., the proportion of social security, healthcare, education, and housing security spending in total fiscal expenditure). The higher this share, the smoother the channel for fiscal resources to transfer to micro households; conversely, fiscal resources are locked in constructive, defensive, or administrative expenditures, and micro households cannot receive direct transfer income from national growth.

**Stage 3: Downstream — Micro Household Net Welfare and Relaxation**

The share of livelihood-oriented expenditure directly determines two micro indicators:
- The portion of household disposable income derived from fiscal transfers (pensions, medical subsidies, education subsidies, etc.);
- The precautionary savings rate that households are forced to raise due to expectations of future risks (old-age care, healthcare, children's education).

When transfer payments are insufficient and precautionary savings remain high, micro households rationally respond in two ways: increase labor participation and working hours (to boost income sources), and increase savings rates (reducing current consumption and leisure). Together, these manifest as the "low relaxation" defined in this paper—long hours, high savings, low leisure.

This logical chain of arrows, while providing a framework, may be overly simplified.

**Three Key Moderators**:

- **Moderator A (Demographic Dependency Acceleration)**: A rapid increase in the dependency ratio simultaneously expands the upstream "pre-deduction items" (rigid increase in pension expenditures) and compresses the midstream "maneuverable space for livelihood expenditure," producing a double amplification effect.

- **Moderator B (Expected Discount Factor U, contextual descriptive indicator)**: U reflects the anxiety discount rate of the population regarding future major expenditures (medical care, pensions, education, housing), indirectly observed through behavioral indicators such as savings rates. The higher U, even if current disposable income grows, households still choose to convert incremental income into savings rather than consumption or leisure, directly blocking the "last mile" transmission from midstream livelihood expenditure to downstream relaxation. This paper does not make causal inferences about U, only using it as a contextual descriptive indicator reflecting micro agents' responses to structural conditions. The estimation of U itself uses behavioral indicators such as savings rates, making it more of a comprehensive response to structural conditions than an independent causal variable. This paper treats it solely as a contextual descriptive indicator, without causal attribution.

- **Moderator C (Housing System, supplementary variable)**: The rent-to-buy ratio, mortgage burden, and strength of tenant protection constitute independent institutional moderators. The difference between China's high mortgage burden and Germany's robust tenant protection system may be an important explanatory variable for cross-country differences in relaxation. Due to space and data constraints, this paper only provides qualitative hints for Moderator C and does not systematically elaborate.

### 3. Typological Distinction: Internally Absorbed vs. Externally Shared Growth

Based on the above transmission path, this paper proposes a preliminary typological framework to explain systematic differences in transmission efficiency across the four countries. It should be noted that this typological distinction does not assign value judgments, but rather points to two different structural paths. Internally absorbed growth does not necessarily mean "worse," but rather that, in the absence of external transfer channels, it inevitably entails higher public savings and longer periods of individual tension. Moreover, the description is based on current technological conditions (2024–2025 labor productivity growth); if general-purpose technologies like AI significantly improve total factor productivity in the next decade, the tension-relaxation relationship of the two growth models may undergo nonlinear restructuring.

It should be particularly noted that the typology (internal/external absorption) describes a state at a specific historical stage (the 2024–2035 window), not a permanent label for any country. Late-developing large countries, during their peak phases of industrialization and sovereignty consolidation, all historically entered an "internally absorbed" stage. The United States during the construction of the transcontinental railroad in the 19th century, and Germany during the post-WWII reconstruction period, both experienced similar "internal absorption" phases. This paper does not intend to label "internal absorption" as exclusive to China, but rather treats it as a universal structural state for late-developing countries at a specific development stage.

| Typology Dimension | Externally Shared Growth (U.S., Germany as representatives) | Internally Absorbed Growth (China as representative) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Security costs | Shared or transferred through alliance systems, seigniorage, or military umbrellas | Fully self-borne, no sharing mechanism |
| Geographic integration costs | Completed during early industrialization, now only at low maintenance level | At construction peak, high current net burden |
| External rule costs | Rule-maker or intra-system beneficiary, with historical dividends accumulating | Entering barrier phase from globalization boom, cliff-like net gap |
| Demographic dependency | Smooth transition through immigration or long-term welfare systems | Getting old before getting rich, globally highest dependency acceleration |
| Cost absorption method | External transfer + high domestic welfare distribution | Domestic high savings + low livelihood expenditure share |
| Micro relaxation characteristics | High disposable income share + low hours (Germany) or high income + high consumption (U.S.) | Low transfer income + high hours + high savings |

### 4. Methodological Notes

This paper adopts a comparative case study and typology-building strategy, selecting the United States, Germany, Japan, and China as comparative cases.

**Case selection strategy**: This paper follows the logic of Mill's (1843) comparative method. However, given the systematic differences among the four countries in population size, sovereignty status, geographic area, and international standing, the comparative strategy here is a "Most Different Systems Design" (MDSD)—that is, seeking common structural mechanisms and typological patterns among highly heterogeneous cases, rather than controlling variables among similar cases. The purpose is not statistical extrapolation but to reveal the universality of deep mechanisms through extreme case comparisons.

Case selection is based on two criteria: (1) maximum variation in the core independent variables (cost-transfer channels, fiscal expenditure structure, demographic structure) across the four countries, fitting the MDSD logic; (2) data availability—all four countries have systematic, continuous, and comparable data from OECD SOCX social welfare databases, SIPRI military expenditure databases, and IMF infrastructure investment databases. Other late-developing large countries adopting "internal absorption" (e.g., India, Brazil) are not included due to the lack of comparable social expenditure data under OECD SOCX standards, constituting a direction for future research.

**Temporal window statement**: The empirical data in this paper is based on a cross-section of 2024–2025, and future projections (such as dependency ratio forecasts) are based on the UN Population Division's medium fertility variant. The conclusions apply only to the specific historical window of 2024–2035 and do not claim cross-temporal validity beyond that.

**Methodological limitation statement**: This paper has the following clear limitations, which constitute directions for future research:
1. The security cost indicator uses nominal military spending as a share of GDP as a proxy, without incorporating geopolitical risk premiums (e.g., number of neighboring countries, territorial disputes, nuclear threat distance), potentially underestimating the true weight of China's security burden.
2. Analysis of labor market institutions (union coverage, collective bargaining levels, labor law enforcement) is outside the scope of this paper.
3. Housing system (rent-to-buy ratio, mortgage burden, etc.) is only qualitatively noted and not systematically analyzed.
4. The "leisure time" indicator does not further distinguish freely disposable time from household labor time, introducing potential gender bias.
5. The analysis is a cross-sectional static comparison and does not include intergenerational longitudinal dimensions.
6. This paper treats national relaxation as a macro-average indicator, without in-depth analysis of intra-national variations by class, region, or urban-rural divide.
7. This paper focuses on the distribution-side fiscal transmission mechanism; systematic analysis of the production side (labor process, labor-capital relations) is outside its scope.

**Counterfactual statement**: This paper does not conduct counterfactual inference, nor does it argue that "reducing rigid expenditures will necessarily improve relaxation." It only reveals the structural characteristics of the current transmission path; what policy mix would optimally balance security, development, and relaxation is beyond the scope of this paper.

## VI. Structural Lock-in Analysis

The comparative data from the four countries clearly shows a pattern: China is not "underperforming" in any single dimension; rather, it faces simultaneous pressure across all links of the transmission chain (high upstream rigid expenditures, squeezed midstream livelihood expenditure, and high downstream expected discount factor). This is a typical structural feature of "internally absorbed growth."

China's "tension" can be understood through the following structural lock-ins. It should be emphasized that the following analysis focuses on distribution-side fiscal transmission mechanisms; discussion of production-side factors (labor processes, labor law enforcement, corporate culture) is only auxiliary and beyond the systematic scope of this paper.

**Lock-in 1: Security self-reliance is a rigid pre-deduction item.** The U.S. can externalize security costs through seigniorage and alliance systems; Germany relies on NATO; Japan partly depends on U.S. military presence. China has none of these options—its 1.3% military spending, though fully self-borne, more importantly represents a 100% non-compressible fiscal pre-deduction, coupled with the world's highest geopolitical pressure, directly reducing the total pie available for midstream livelihood allocation. This lock-in will not ease in the foreseeable future.

**Lock-in 2: Geographic connection costs are at their peak, crowding out current livelihood expenditure.** The revised net burden range ($1,500–2,100/person/year) remains significantly higher than that of the U.S. and Germany. These expenditures will continue to act as rigid deductions until the completion of the "Eight Vertical Eight Horizontal" backbone network (around 2030). Before the inflection point, every infrastructure dollar directly crowds out livelihood expenditure. Even after the backbone network enters maintenance phase around 2030, accumulated debt and amortization will persist, so the actual net burden decline may not occur until after 2035. Hence this paper's window of 2024–2035 covers the full transition from peak to post-peak.

**Lock-in 3: The external rule dividend cliff-like reversal creates a fiscal black hole of 3.5% of GDP.** This is the most direct financial trigger of current "tension." The net gap from +2.0% to -1.5% annually means losing resources equivalent to a large-scale fiscal stimulus package each year. This loss cannot be easily offset by domestic policies, as it is rooted in shifts in global power structures.

**Lock-in 4: Profit transformation is constrained by "employment rigidity" and cannot be rapidly upgraded.** China's high-value-added industry share (28%) is far below Germany's (47%), yet its manufacturing sector absorbs hundreds of millions of jobs. The high employment absorption elasticity (0.65) means rapid transformation would trigger massive unemployment risks. Therefore, profit quality improvement must cautiously balance "job preservation" and "value-added upgrading," making transmission improvement inevitably gradual and prolonged.

**Lock-in 5: The demographic dependency ratio is about to surge, combined with high mortgage burden and high U value, jointly locking the last mile.** This is the most underestimated long-term variable and the fundamental difference between China and the historical experiences of the U.S. and Germany. At their industrialization peaks, the U.S. and Germany had dependency ratios far below China's current level, and could dilute pressures through immigration and colonial resources. China will face a total dependency ratio of 67% by 2035, when livelihood fiscal expenditure is only 38% of the budget and household mortgage burden is about 55%. High dependency means future fiscal resources will be heavily locked by pensions and healthcare; high mortgage burden means household disposable income is already pre-cut; high U value (0.60) means the population has internalized this expectation into current savings behavior. Together, these ensure that even if upstream and midstream conditions improve in 10 years, the release of micro-level relaxation will be seriously delayed.

At this point I realize that Germany and the U.S. were also "tired" in their past. Is China's current tiredness a necessary cost for future relaxation? This question cannot be answered too hastily.

It should be added that other important factors affecting relaxation—such as housing system differences (Germany's strong tenant protection vs. China's high mortgage burden), gender distribution of unpaid household labor, labor law enforcement, and corporate culture—have all been identified as methodological limitations and await future research. Moreover, the static cross-sectional analysis cannot capture intergenerational changes—20 years ago China's GDP was only one-tenth of today's, and workers then may have experienced a different kind of "tiredness." This longitudinal dimension is also beyond the scope of this paper.

Combining the above five lock-ins, China's "tiredness" is not a policy failure, but a structural necessity of the "internally absorbed growth" path during the phase of globalization retreat and rapid aging. This tension is the historical position of this generation during the 2024–2035 window, not an eternal attribute of the system—but its duration will depend on when the three inflection points (geographic connection, profit quality, and dependency curve flattening) converge.

At the same time, this paper acknowledges the uncertainty of technological variables. If general-purpose technologies like AI significantly boost total factor productivity around 2030, enabling more value creation with the same inputs, the five lock-ins may nonlinearly loosen. This possibility implies that the "long-term" judgment should be understood as a projection under current technological trends, not a cross-technology eternal law.

It should be especially noted that this paper's analysis focuses on the transmission effect of structural constraints on individual relaxation, and does not deny the possibility of reverse causality—namely, China's high labor participation and high-savings cultural tradition may also objectively provide the fiscal space for large-scale security and infrastructure investment. The interaction between cultural and institutional factors is another important path for understanding the "strong state – relaxed people" relationship, but due to data and space constraints, this paper does not systematically explore the cultural dimension, leaving it for future research.

## VII. Conclusion

This paper is not a conclusion that can answer the question. It merely provides an explanatory framework—a reasoning scaffold for those who find themselves puzzled. That is its sole value.

Through the three-stage transmission path and the typological distinction between "internally absorbed" and "externally shared" growth, this paper demonstrates that there is a structural decoupling between a strong state and a relaxed people. This decoupling is most pronounced in China—full security self-reliance, geographic connection peak, external dividend cliff reversal, profit transformation constrained by employment rigidity, and the world's highest dependency acceleration—five lock-ins叠加, making the transmission dilemma exhibit long-term structural features during the 2024–2035 window.

The Japanese case reminds us that even with relatively better conditions across five dimensions, extreme deterioration in a single dimension (aging) can drag down overall transmission efficiency. This framework offers no policy prescriptions, only a starting point for discussion—turning the sources of "tiredness" from vague emotions into traceable transmission chains.

## VIII. Falsification Conditions

This paper's core judgment contains two layers:
1. The three-stage transmission path is valid: upstream rigid expenditures transmit through midstream livelihood fiscal expenditure share to downstream individual relaxation;
2. China's current transmission dilemma has long-term structural features of the "internal absorption" type during the 2024–2035 window, and cannot be reversed by a single policy in the short term.

The terms "structural necessity" and similar expressions used herein refer to tendency results under current structural conditions, not cross-contextual absolute laws. The falsification conditions listed below are tests of the boundaries of that tendency.

If any of the following conditions occurs within 5–10 years, the core conclusions of this paper need to be revised:

1. If China's livelihood fiscal expenditure share rises above 50%, and the national savings rate simultaneously drops by more than 5 percentage points—then the core mechanism of the "fiscal intermediation squeeze" hypothesis is broken, and the judgment of "prolonged transmission blockage" should be revised upward.

2. If China's geographic connection net burden (per capita annual infrastructure spending net of 30–50 year depreciation) falls below $1,200, and during the same period the livelihood expenditure share does not correspondingly increase, but the average weekly working hours significantly drop below 48 hours—then the main source of "tiredness" is not fiscal allocation but labor institutions or cultural factors, and the model needs to incorporate new variables.

3. If China's high-value-added industry share exceeds 38% and labor compensation as a share of GDP simultaneously rises above 52%, but workers' annual working hours show no significant decline and savings rates do not decrease—then there is an unidentified blocking mechanism in the "profit quality improvement → livelihood improvement → relaxation release" transmission chain, requiring further revision of the five-dimensional transmission path.

4. If, when China reaches the aging peak around 2035, the old-age dependency ratio exceeds 45% but the livelihood fiscal expenditure share exceeds 45%, and national subjective well-being indicators show no decline—then the core hypothesis that "dependency ratio squeezes relaxation" does not hold, and the demographic variable should be down-weighted or its direction of effect re-examined.
