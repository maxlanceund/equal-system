# Between a Strong Nation and Its Tired People: The Internally Absorbed Conduction Dilemma of Great Power Rise
## — A Comparative Political Economy Analysis Based on Fiscal Intermediary and Typology

> **Core Argument**: There is no automatic transmission mechanism between a strong nation and its relaxed people. Adopting the epistemological stance of critical realism, this paper operationalizes "national relaxation" as a composite vector of "per capita disposable income as a share of GDP" and "annual leisure time," and introduces "fiscal expenditure structure (share of livelihood-oriented expenditure)" as the core intermediary variable through which macro-level state capacity transmits to micro-level individual welfare. By constructing a logical transmission pathway of "upstream rigid expenditures → midstream fiscal compression → downstream household net welfare," this paper argues that China's current state of "tightness" is rooted in the inevitable cost of an "internally absorbed growth" path — where multiple rigid costs (security, geography, external rules) cannot be externalized and must be internally absorbed through high public savings rates and low livelihood-oriented expenditures, manifesting at the micro level as an equilibrium of high working hours, high savings, and low leisure. This dilemma is further locked in by rapid aging, exhibiting significant long-term structural characteristics within the 2024-2035 window.

# I. Abstract

Does a "strong nation" mean a "relaxed people"? The premise for answering this question lies in the operationalization of "relaxation." This paper defines "national relaxation" as the composite vector of "per capita disposable income as a share of GDP" (reflecting the share of economic surplus that goes to individuals) and "annual per capita leisure time" (reflecting temporal autonomy). Neither alone is sufficient: high income without leisure (e.g., overwork) or leisure without income (e.g., structural unemployment) does not constitute a state of "relaxation." This paper adopts a "leisure-first" weighting logic, assuming that the marginal contribution of leisure time exceeds that of a proportional increase in disposable income — a judgment grounded in Sen's (1999) theoretical tradition that "temporal autonomy is the ultimate freedom."

This paper makes three core theoretical advances:
1. **Identifying the micro-transmission intermediary**: Establishing "fiscal expenditure structure (share of livelihood-oriented expenditure)" as the necessary intermediary link between the macro and micro levels, effectively avoiding the "ecological fallacy" of directly equating macro aggregates with micro experiences;
2. **Revising core variable measurements**: Adjusting geographic connection costs to "current net burden (estimated on a 30-50 year weighted average depreciation range)" and external rule costs to "net globalization gain-loss gap (from +2.0% to -1.5%)";
3. **Introducing a typological distinction**: Proposing a preliminary typological framework of "internally absorbed growth" versus "externally apportioned growth," identifying cost-transfer capacity and demographic structural acceleration as key structural moderators.

Through a typological comparison of the experiences of the United States, Germany, Japan, and China, this paper finds that the "relaxation" of the United States and Germany stems in part from their "externally apportioned" pathways (global seigniorage, NATO security outsourcing, EU labor market complementarity) that free up fiscal space; China's "tightness" is rooted in the inevitable cost of the "internally absorbed" pathway — lacking new external cost-transfer channels during the era of deglobalization, compounded by unprecedented rapid aging (total dependency ratio projected to reach 67% by 2035), the five-dimensional rigid costs cannot be diluted and must be internally absorbed through the compression of livelihood-oriented fiscal expenditures, ultimately corresponding at the micro level to a high precautionary savings rate (44.5%) and a high expectation discount factor (U≈0.60, treated as a descriptive indicator rather than an independent causal variable).

Adopting the epistemological stance of critical realism, this paper aims to reveal structural generative mechanisms rather than conduct statistical causal inference. The causal language in this paper should be understood as "structural tendencies" rather than trans-temporal deterministic laws.

**This paper's core academic contributions are:**
- Establishing "fiscal expenditure structure (share of livelihood-oriented expenditure)" as the necessary intermediary variable in macro-to-micro transmission, opening the black box of fiscal distribution;
- Proposing the typological distinction between "internally absorbed" and "externally apportioned" growth, revealing the structural role of cost-transfer channels;
- Explicitly limiting the paper's conclusions to the 2024-2035 window and acknowledging the possibility of non-linear changes driven by technological variables (e.g., artificial intelligence).

# II. Introduction: From "We've Won Big" to "Too Tired to Love" — A Macro-Narrative That Needs Intermediation

Open any Chinese social media platform, and grand narratives of "China wins again" are everywhere: GDP second in the world, the world's longest high-speed rail network, 80% of the global photovoltaic market, the world's largest new energy vehicle exports... Yet switching back to real life, another voice is equally strong: ever-lengthening working hours, crushing mortgage burdens, the "35-year-old career crisis," educational hyper-competition, persistently high savings rates. There is a clear crack between these micro-level experiences and the macro-level narrative of "we've won big."

This crack is the starting point of this paper.

This paper's core judgment is that there is no automatic transmission between a strong nation at the macro level and relaxed individuals at the micro level. From "GDP superpower" to "relaxed citizens," there must pass through the crucial intermediary link of "the political distribution of the fiscal budget." The money the nation earns is first carved up by rigid expenditures on security, geographic integration, and external frictions; only the remainder enters livelihood-oriented distribution (social security, healthcare, education, leisure infrastructure), and ultimately reaches individuals' wallets and leisure time.

Therefore, this paper does not attempt to argue whether "China is strong" or whether "Chinese people should feel tired." Rather, it attempts to construct an observable, comparable, and discussable analytical framework to disaggregate the structural sources of "tiredness" — and to open the black box of "fiscal intermediation."

**The core research question this paper attempts to answer is:** How do the state-level rigid expenditure structures transmit, through fiscal distribution mechanisms, into micro-level individual tightness? In this transmission process, which structural factors play key moderating roles?

**The structure of this paper is as follows:** Section II reviews the existing literature and situates this paper's academic dialogue; Section III presents the theoretical framework, including transmission pathways, intermediary mechanisms, typological distinctions, and methodological explanations; Section IV presents comparative empirical data from four countries, disaggregating the five-dimensional variables and moderators item by item; Section V focuses on discussing the structural locks of China's "internally absorbed transmission dilemma"; Section VI concludes, summarizing findings, stating limitations, and listing falsification conditions.

# III. Literature Review and Scholarly Dialogue

Existing research on the relationship between "state capacity and human welfare" is distributed across the following four main lineages, each with critical blind spots:

1. **The development economics tradition** (Kuznets, 1955): Assumes that economic growth will ultimately benefit people's livelihoods through "trickle-down effects." The blind spot of this theory lies in its neglect of the political process of fiscal distribution — the fiscal increments generated by growth do not necessarily flow toward people's livelihoods but may be absorbed by security, infrastructure, or administrative costs.

2. **Welfare state theory** (Esping-Andersen, 1990): Emphasizes the intermediary role of distribution systems. The contribution of this school lies in revealing that "the same GDP, different welfare regimes produce vastly different welfare experiences." However, its analytical objects are concentrated on mature Western welfare states, lacking explanatory power for the "fiscal compression under security and integration pressures" faced by late-developing great powers.

3. **Critical political economy** (Piketty, 2014): Focuses on the imbalance between capital returns and labor returns. However, this tradition primarily focuses on outcome inequality in income distribution and rarely asks "how state rigid expenditures pre-cut the fiscal pie available for distribution."

4. **Chinese livelihood fiscal studies** (Gao Peiyong, 2018; Liu Shangxi et al., 2020): Domestic fiscal scholarship has accumulated rich work on the definition and measurement of "livelihood-oriented expenditures," but its analysis mainly focuses on optimizing fiscal expenditure structures, and has not yet incorporated the share of livelihood-oriented expenditure as an intermediary variable in macro-to-micro transmission within a comparative political economy framework of "state capacity-individual welfare."

This paper does not deny the explanatory power of welfare state regime typologies (Esping-Andersen, 1990) for cross-national differences in welfare expenditure levels. However, welfare regime theory struggles to explain significant differences in relaxation within the same regime type — for example, between Germany and Japan, both conservative welfare regimes, where Germany rates "extremely high" in comprehensive relaxation while Japan rates only "medium-low." The "five-dimensional transmission framework" proposed in this paper provides complementary explanatory dimensions by introducing additional variables such as cost-transfer channels (security outsourcing vs. self-reliance), geographic connection cost phases (maintenance phase vs. peak phase), and demographic structural acceleration.

Building on the above, this paper proposes a "fiscal-intermediary five-dimensional transmission pathway," shifting the analytical focus from "whether growth benefits people's livelihoods" to "how growth is pre-cut by various structural rigid expenditures before being converted into welfare, and how this cutting transmits to individuals through fiscal intermediary mechanisms." The unique contribution of this paper lies in incorporating four variables — geographic connection costs (net burden basis), external rule costs (dynamic gap basis), demographic dependency ratio acceleration, and fiscal expenditure structure (livelihood share) — into a single analytical framework, and clearly distinguishing between two growth typologies: "internally absorbed" and "externally apportioned."

# IV. Theoretical Framework: Transmission Pathways, Intermediary Mechanisms, and Typology

## 1. Logical Starting Point: The Macro Cannot Directly Imply the Micro

This paper adopts the ontological stance of critical realism (Bhaskar, 1975), holding that social reality is constituted by deep, unobservable "generative mechanisms." There is no direct linear determinant relationship between the macro-level "state expenditure structure" and the micro-level "individual relaxation"; rather, transmission must occur through a series of intermediary mechanisms, especially fiscal distribution mechanisms.

The causal language in this paper should be understood as "structural tendencies" — that under specific historical conditions and institutional environments, certain structural factors tend to produce certain outcomes — rather than trans-temporal "deterministic laws." From the perspective of critical realism, structures may be transformed by agents, but the conditions, pathways, and timing of such transformation are uncertain. This paper does not pursue statistical extrapolation nor engage in counterfactual inference.

The simplified narrative of "strong nation → relaxed people" implies three untested assumptions, which this paper deconstructs one by one:

- **Assumption 1**: The money the nation earns can all be used to improve people's livelihoods. **Reality**: Before entering livelihood distribution, state finances must first deduct rigid expenditures on security, geographic integration, and external frictions.

- **Assumption 2**: A strong nation does not need to pay additional "security costs" or "demographic support costs." **Reality**: Security self-reliance and rapid aging represent two massive rigid expenditure curves.

- **Assumption 3**: National territorial integration has been completed and no longer requires massive sustained investment. **Reality**: China is still in the "peak investment phase" of geographic connection costs, with current net burdens significantly higher than in the US and Germany, which have entered maintenance phases.

**Terminological clarification**: This paper uses "relaxation" as its core theoretical concept, with "tiredness," "tightness," and "low relaxation" as its common-language opposites. These four terms all refer to different intensities of the same conceptual spectrum and are not treated as typologically distinct. The "tightness" discussed in this paper refers to a structural state, not clinical burnout — the latter belongs to the domain of individual psychopathology and falls outside this paper's scope.

## 2. Logical Transmission Pathway

The "transmission pathway" constructed in this paper refers to a logical-level structural tendency, rather than statistical causal inference based on micro-level panel data. This paper aims to reveal structural constraints rather than make deterministic attributions of individual behavior.

The core analytical framework of this paper is a **three-stage transmission pathway**, rather than a mathematical equation. This pathway clearly distinguishes three levels of analysis: macro, meso, and micro.

**Stage One: Upstream — The National Rigid Expenditure Pool**

Each year, the GDP created by the nation first faces four types of rigid expenditure demands:
- Net security costs (military expenditure as a share of GDP, net of transfer/sharing mechanisms);
- Net geographic connection burden (annual infrastructure investment minus 30-50 year weighted average depreciation);
- Net external rule gap (the difference between gains during the globalization dividend period and current barrier-period expenditures);
- Demographic dependency pressure (the rigid demand for fiscal transfers from the non-working-age population).

These four items constitute the "pre-commitments" of the total fiscal pie.

**Stage Two: Midstream — The Political Distribution of Fiscal Expenditure Structure**

After pre-commitments are deducted, the remaining fiscal space enters the political distribution stage. The core variable at this stage is the "share of livelihood-oriented fiscal expenditure" — i.e., the proportion of total fiscal expenditure allocated to social security, healthcare, education, and housing support. The higher the share of livelihood-oriented expenditure, the smoother the channel through which fiscal resources transfer to households; conversely, when fiscal resources are locked into construction, defense, or administrative expenditures, households cannot obtain direct transfer income from national growth.

**Stage Three: Downstream — Household Net Welfare and Relaxation**

The share of livelihood-oriented expenditure directly determines two micro-level indicators:
- The portion of household disposable income derived from fiscal transfers (pensions, healthcare subsidies, education subsidies, etc.);
- The precautionary savings rate that households are compelled to raise due to expectations of future risks (elderly care, healthcare, children's education).

When transfer payments are insufficient and precautionary savings are high, households make two rational responses: increase labor force participation and working hours (to boost income sources), and increase savings rates (reducing current consumption and leisure). The combination of these two responses manifests as the "low relaxation" defined in this paper — high working hours, high savings, low leisure.

**Three Key Moderators**:

- **Moderator A (Demographic Dependency Ratio Acceleration)** : Rapid increases in the dependency ratio simultaneously expand upstream "pre-commitments" (rigid increases in elderly-care expenditures) and compress midstream "fiscal space for livelihood-oriented expenditure," producing a double-magnification effect.

- **Moderator B (Expectation Discount Factor U, Descriptive Indicator)** : U reflects the public's anxiety discount rate regarding future major expenditures (healthcare, elderly care, education, housing), indirectly observed through behavioral indicators such as savings rates. The higher U is, the more households choose to convert increased current income into savings rather than consumption or leisure — even when current disposable income has grown — directly blocking the final kilometer of transmission from "midstream livelihood expenditures" to "downstream relaxation." This paper does not make causal inferences about U, treating it only as a descriptive indicator reflecting micro-level agents' responses to structural conditions.

- **Moderator C (Housing System, Supplementary Variable)** : The rent-to-purchase ratio, mortgage burden rate, and strength of tenant protection constitute independent institutional moderators. The gap between China's high mortgage burden and Germany's robust tenant protection system may be an important explanatory variable for cross-national differences in relaxation. Due to space and data constraints, this paper only provides qualitative indication of Moderator C without systematic elaboration.

## 3. Typological Distinction: Internally Absorbed vs. Externally Apportioned Growth

Building on the above transmission pathway, this paper proposes a preliminary typological framework to explain the systematic differences in transmission efficiency across the four countries. It should be noted that this typological distinction does not assign value judgments but identifies two different structural pathways. Internally absorbed growth is not necessarily "worse" — it simply means that, in the absence of external transfer channels, it necessarily entails higher public savings rates and longer individual tightness periods. Furthermore, this typology's description is based on current technological conditions (2024-2025 productivity growth rates); if general-purpose technologies such as AI significantly raise total factor productivity in the next decade, the tightness-relaxation relationship of the two growth models may undergo nonlinear restructuring.

It should be particularly noted that the typology in this paper (internal/external apportionment) describes the state of a specific historical phase (the 2024-2035 window), not a permanent label for any country. Late-developing great powers, during the peak period of their industrialization and sovereignty consolidation, all historically enter the "internally absorbed" phase. The United States during its transcontinental railroad construction in the 19th century and Germany during its post-WWII reconstruction both experienced similar "internally absorbed" phases. This paper does not intend to make "internal absorption" an exclusive label for China, but rather treats it as a universal structural state for late-developing great powers at a particular developmental stage.

| Typological Dimension | Externally Apportioned Growth (US, Germany) | Internally Absorbed Growth (China) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Security Costs | Transferred or shared through alliance systems, seigniorage, or military umbrellas | Fully self-borne, no sharing mechanisms |
| Geographic Integration Costs | Completed in early industrialization; currently only low-level maintenance | At peak construction phase; current net burden high |
| External Rule Costs | Rule-makers or system beneficiaries; historical dividends accumulate | Transitioning from globalization dividend period to barrier period; cliff-like gap |
| Demographic Dependency | Smooth transition through immigration or long-term welfare systems | Growing old before getting rich; globally highest dependency acceleration |
| Cost Absorption Method | External transfer + high internal welfare distribution | Internal high savings rate + low livelihood expenditure share |
| Micro Relaxation Characteristics | High disposable income share + low working hours (Germany) or high income + high consumption (US) | Low transfer income + high working hours + high savings rate |

## 4. Methodological Explanation

This paper adopts a comparative case study and typology-construction methodological strategy, selecting the United States, Germany, Japan, and China as comparative cases.

**Case Selection Strategy**: While following the logic of Mill's (1843) comparative method, given the systematic differences among the four countries in population size, sovereignty status, geographic area, and international standing, this paper's comparative strategy falls under "Most Different Systems Design" — seeking common structural mechanisms and typological patterns across extremely heterogeneous cases, rather than controlling variables across similar cases. The purpose of this strategy is not statistical extrapolation but the revelation of deep mechanisms through the contrast of extreme cases.

Case selection is based on two criteria: (1) the four countries exhibit maximum variation in core independent variables (cost-transfer channels, fiscal expenditure structure, demographic structure), suitable for MDSD logic; and (2) data availability — all four countries have systematic, continuously comparable data from the OECD SOCX welfare database, SIPRI military expenditure database, and IMF infrastructure investment database. Other "internally absorbed" late-developing great powers such as India and Brazil are not included in this analysis due to the lack of OECD SOCX-comparable social expenditure data, constituting a direction for future research.

**Time Window Declaration**: The empirical data in this paper is based on the 2024-2025 benchmark cross-section, and future projections (such as dependency ratio forecasts) are based on the UN Population Division's medium-fertility variant estimates. The conclusions of this paper are only applicable to the specific historical window of 2024-2035 and do not claim validity over longer time spans.

**Methodological Limitations Statement**: This paper has the following clear limitations, which constitute directions for future research:
1. The security cost indicator uses nominal military expenditure share as a proxy, without incorporating geopolitical risk premiums (such as number of neighbors, territorial disputes, nuclear threat distance), potentially underestimating the true weight of China's security burden;
2. Labor market institutions (union coverage, collective bargaining levels, labor law enforcement) are not within the scope of this analysis;
3. Housing system (rent-to-purchase ratio, mortgage burden rate, etc.) is only qualitatively indicated, not systematically analyzed;
4. The "leisure time" indicator does not further distinguish freely disposable time from household labor time, introducing gender bias;
5. This paper's analysis is a cross-sectional static comparison and does not incorporate intergenerational longitudinal dimensions;
6. This paper treats national relaxation as a macro-level average indicator without deeply analyzing intra-national variation by class, region, or urban-rural divide;
7. This paper focuses on the distribution-side fiscal transmission mechanism; systematic analysis of the production side (labor processes, industrial relations) falls outside its scope.

**Counterfactual Statement**: This paper does not engage in counterfactual inference and does not claim that "reducing rigid expenditures would necessarily improve relaxation." It only reveals the structural characteristics of the current transmission pathway. The question of which policy combinations could optimally balance security, development, and relaxation lies beyond this paper's argumentative scope.

# VI. Discussion: Structural Locks of China's "Internally Absorbed Transmission Dilemma"

The comparative data from four countries clearly reveal a pattern: China is not "underperforming" on any single dimension, but is under pressure on **all links** of the transmission chain simultaneously (high upstream rigid expenditures, compressed midstream livelihood expenditures, high downstream expectation discount factors). This is the structural signature of the "internally absorbed growth" path.

China's "tightness" can be understood through the following structural locks. It should be emphasized that the following analysis focuses on the distribution-side fiscal transmission mechanism; discussion of the production side (labor processes, labor law enforcement, corporate culture) is only supplementary, as systematic analysis falls outside this paper's scope.

**Lock 1: Security self-reliance as rigid pre-commitment.** The US can externalize security costs through seigniorage and alliance systems; Germany relies on NATO; Japan partially relies on US troop stationing. China has none of these options — the 1.3% military expenditure, while fully self-borne, is more importantly a 100% non-compressible fiscal pre-commitment, facing the world's highest geopolitical pressure, directly reducing the total fiscal pie available for midstream livelihood distribution. This lock will not loosen in the foreseeable future.

**Lock 2: Geographic connection costs at peak phase, crowding out current livelihood expenditure.** The revised net burden range ($1,500-2,100/person/year), while lower than the original estimate, remains significantly higher than in the US and Germany. This expenditure will continue to exist as a rigid deduction until the completion of the "Eight Vertical, Eight Horizontal" backbone network (around 2030). Until that inflection point, every infrastructure dollar means direct crowding out of livelihood expenditure.

**Lock 3: External rule dividend cliff-reversal, creating a 3.5% GDP fiscal black hole.** This is the most direct financial trigger of current "tightness." The drop from an average annual +2.0% to -1.5% net gap means an annual loss equivalent to a large-scale fiscal stimulus package. This loss cannot be easily hedged through domestic policy, as it is rooted in global power structure shifts.

**Lock 4: Profit transition constrained by "employment rigidity," unable to accelerate.** China's high-value-added share (28%) lags far behind Germany (47%), but manufacturing employs hundreds of millions. High employment absorption elasticity (0.65) means rapid transition would trigger massive unemployment risk. Therefore, profit quality improvement must cautiously balance "employment protection" and "value-added enhancement," ensuring that transmission improvement will inevitably be gradual and protracted.

**Lock 5: Demographic dependency set to surge, combined with high mortgage burden and high U, jointly locking the final kilometer.** This is the most underestimated long-term variable and the fundamental factor distinguishing China from the historical experiences of the US and Germany. At their industrialization peaks, both the US and Germany had dependency ratios far below China's current level, and could dilute pressures through immigration and colonial resources. China will face a 67% total dependency ratio by 2035, when livelihood fiscal expenditure is only 38% and the household mortgage burden rate is ~55%. High dependency means future fiscal resources will be locked into elderly care and healthcare; high mortgage burden means household disposable income is already pre-cut; high U (0.60) means the public has internalized these expectations into current savings behavior — all three together mean that even if upstream and midstream conditions improve in 10 years, micro-level relaxation release will be significantly delayed.

It should be added that other important factors affecting relaxation — such as housing system differences (Germany's strong tenant protection vs. China's high mortgage burden), intra-household gendered division of unpaid labor, labor law enforcement, and corporate culture — have all been identified as methodological limitations of this paper, awaiting future research. Furthermore, this paper's static cross-sectional analysis cannot capture intergenerational change — 20 years ago, China's GDP was only one-tenth of today's, and workers of that era likely experienced different forms of "tiredness." This longitudinal dimension also falls outside this paper's scope.

Synthesizing the above five locks, China's "tiredness" is not a policy failure but the structural necessity of the "internally absorbed growth" path under the superposition of deglobalization and rapid aging. This tightness is the historical position of this generation within the 2024-2035 window, not an eternal attribute of the system — but its duration will depend on when the three inflection points of geographic connection, profit quality, and dependency curve flattening converge.

At the same time, this paper acknowledges the uncertainty of technological variables. If general-purpose technologies such as artificial intelligence significantly raise total factor productivity around 2030, enabling greater value creation with the same inputs, the above five locks may experience nonlinear loosening. This possibility means this paper's "long-term" judgment should be understood as "a projection under current technological trends," not a trans-technological eternal law.

It should be particularly noted that this paper's analysis focuses on the transmission effects of structural constraints on individual relaxation and does not deny the possibility of reverse causality — that China's high labor participation and high savings cultural traditions may have objectively provided the fiscal space for large-scale security and infrastructure investment. The interaction between cultural and institutional factors is another important pathway for understanding the "strong nation — relaxed people" relationship, but due to data and space constraints, this paper does not systematically develop the cultural dimension, leaving it for future research.

# VII. Conclusion

This paper's analysis does not treat "relaxation" as a trans-historically universal standard. During industrialization catch-up periods or sovereignty reconstruction phases, "tightness" has instrumental legitimacy — it is the necessary price for rapid accumulation under conditions of extreme resource scarcity. This paper's concern focuses on the special transition window of 2024-2035 — when China's major infrastructure skeleton is largely complete, per capita GDP has crossed the $10,000 threshold, and the structure of popular demand has shifted from "survival/subsistence/construction" to "better life/comprehensive development," making "relaxation" a legitimate and necessary dimension for evaluating institutional performance.

Through constructing the three-stage transmission pathway of "upstream rigid expenditures → midstream fiscal intermediary (livelihood expenditure share) → downstream individual relaxation," introducing the typological distinction between "internally absorbed" and "externally apportioned" growth, and comparing empirical data from the United States, Germany, Japan, and China, this paper demonstrates the following core judgment:

There is a significant structural disconnect between a strong nation and its relaxed people. This disconnect is not unique to China, but in China it manifests most prominently due to the "internally absorbed" growth path — full security self-burden (with highest geopolitical pressure), geographic connection at peak phase (net burden $1,500-2,100/person/year), external dividend cliff-reversal (3.5% GDP net gap), profit transition constrained by employment rigidity (employment absorption elasticity 0.65), globally highest dependency acceleration (45%→67%), extremely high expectation discount factor (U≈0.60) combined with high mortgage burden (~55%) — multiple locks superimposed, exhibiting significant long-term structural characteristics within the 2024-2035 window.

This paper's core theoretical contribution lies in introducing "structural channels of cost transfer" and "fiscal expenditure intermediary mechanisms" into the analytical framework, revealing two key blind spots in the transmission from "strong nation" to "relaxed people" — (1) how state rigid expenditures pre-cut the fiscal cake; (2) how fiscal distribution affects household income expectations and savings behavior through the share of livelihood expenditure. At the same time, this paper explicitly limits its conclusions to a specific time window, acknowledges the possibility of non-linear changes driven by technological variables (such as AI), and systematically states its research limitations.

The special positioning of the Japan case in this paper is worth emphasizing: Japan outperforms China on five-dimensional conditions (profit quality ★★★★ vs ★★★, distribution structure ★★★★ vs ★★), yet its relaxation remains significantly lower than Germany's. This fact demonstrates that even under relatively favorable five-dimensional conditions, extreme deterioration on a single dimension (aging, total dependency 72%) can collapse overall transmission efficiency. This in turn reinforces this paper's judgment of "demographic dependency acceleration" as a key moderator, while also warning China: even if five-dimensional conditions improve in the future, aging's structural drag may independently depress relaxation.

This paper has found no counter-case that fits "internally absorbed growth + high relaxation." This empirically supports the theoretical hypothesis that "internal absorption necessarily entails a tightness period," but also means this theory has not yet undergone stringent counterfactual falsification testing. At the same time, this paper does not engage in counterfactual inference — it does not claim that "cutting rigid expenditures would necessarily improve relaxation," only revealing the structural characteristics of the current transmission pathway.

This paper uses "relaxation" as an analytical concept aimed at describing the structural characteristics of transmission mechanisms, rather than making normative value judgments about "relaxation" versus "tightness." Tightness under specific historical conditions (such as sovereignty reconstruction periods, industrialization catch-up periods) may have legitimacy and necessity, and this paper does not make ethical rulings on this matter. Internally absorbed growth and externally apportioned growth are two different structural pathways, with the former necessarily entailing higher public savings rates and longer individual tightness periods — this is the result of rational choice under structural constraints, not a measure of institutional superiority.

Different from the historical pathways of the US and Germany as "externally apportioned growth," the special dilemma China faces is: lacking the historical window to develop new external transfer channels during deglobalization, while simultaneously encountering the largest and fastest aging process in human history. This means that transmission blockage cannot be relieved in the short term, and may instead enter a "fiscal triple squeeze" (security spending + elderly spending + infrastructure debt repayment) when the dependency peak arrives around 2030-2035, further compressing the space for increasing livelihood expenditure.

This paper does not offer simple policy prescriptions nor attempt to reverse trends; rather, it provides a rigorously revised analytical framework that turns the sources of "tiredness" from vague sentiment into traceable transmission chains — and reminds readers that under the "internally absorbed growth" path, the release of national relaxation is not an automatic consequence of modernization, but a long-term goal that must be approached through repeated bargaining in fiscal distribution politics.

# VIII. Falsification Conditions

This paper's core judgment has two dimensions:
1. The three-stage transmission pathway is valid: upstream rigid expenditures transmit through midstream livelihood-oriented fiscal expenditure share to downstream individual relaxation;
2. China's current transmission dilemma has "internally absorbed" long-term structural characteristics within the 2024-2035 window and cannot be reversed through any single policy in the short term.

If any of the following conditions occur within 5-10 years, this paper's core conclusions require revision:

1. **China's livelihood-oriented fiscal expenditure share rises above 50%, and the national savings rate simultaneously drops by more than 5 percentage points** — then the core mechanism of "fiscal intermediary compression" is broken, and this paper's judgment of "transmission blockage long-termization" requires upward revision.

2. **China's net geographic connection burden (per capita annual infrastructure depreciation expenditure) falls below $1,200, and the national average weekly working hours significantly drop below 48 hours without a corresponding rise in livelihood expenditure share** — then the primary contradiction of "tiredness" lies not in fiscal distribution but in labor institutions or cultural factors, requiring the model to incorporate new variables.

3. **China's high-value-added industry share exceeds 38% with labor compensation as a share of GDP simultaneously rising above 52%, yet average annual working hours show no significant decline and savings rates show no decline** — then there exist unidentified blocking mechanisms in the transmission chain of "profit quality improvement → livelihood improvement → relaxation release," requiring further revision of the five-dimensional transmission pathway.

4. **If by the 2035 aging peak, the old-age dependency ratio exceeds 45% while livelihood-oriented fiscal expenditure exceeds 45%, yet subjective well-being indicators show no decline** — then the core hypothesis that "dependency ratio squeezes relaxation" does not hold, and the demographic variable requires down-weighting or re-examination of its direction of effect.
