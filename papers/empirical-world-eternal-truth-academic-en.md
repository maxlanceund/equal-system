# Empirical World, Eternal Truth

> **Core Thesis**: Any proposition about the empirical world, if assigned the attribute "eternally true", is in a state of **cognitive suspension** with respect to cognitive operations. "Eternal" is not a predicate that can be verified by any empirical procedure; it is a temporal modality—a logical operator that alters the temporal scope of a proposition. It demands an infinite number of verifications within a finite time window, which is **structurally uncompletable**. This paper does not discuss whether "eternal truth exists"; it only discusses whether "eternal truth is accessible to empirical cognition". The conclusion is that, within the framework of operational semantics, eternal truth is not accessible. Within the framework of this paper, between the empirical world and eternal truth there is no passage—only a rupture.

---

## Abstract

Is it true that "the sun will rise tomorrow"? If we ask, "will the sun rise forever?"—everyday language habitually conflates "true" with "eternally true", yet there is a fundamental structural rupture between "true" and "eternally true". This paper argues that the "eternal truth" of an empirical proposition is in a state of cognitive suspension with respect to verification procedures—not a provisional "not yet confirmed", but a state in which the verification procedure is structurally unexecutable.

This paper advances three core theoretical moves: (1) it defines the operational semantics of "eternal", reducing it from a metaphysical concept to a temporal modality—it asserts that a proposition remains true at all future times, and this assertion is unverifiable by any operational procedure; (2) it distinguishes "formal eternity" from "contentual eternity", pointing out that the eternity of logic and mathematics is the eternity of an analytic framework (unchanging form), while the eternity of empirical propositions is the eternity of substantive content (unchanging facts)—the latter is the target of this paper's critique; (3) it introduces the principle of **time-binding of decision procedures**, showing that all empirical judgments must be executed in the present, while "eternal" demands coverage of an infinite future—there is a structural temporal mismatch between the two.

Through the analytic path of operational semantics (Bridgman, 1927; Schlick, 1936), this paper argues that the meta-proposition "empirical proposition P is eternal" is neither true nor false, but is in a state of **cognitive suspension**. The conclusion of this paper depends on a specific epistemological premise—namely, that decidability is a necessary condition for truth to be cognitively accessible. This paper provides an independent defense of this premise (see the Methodological Statement) and directly responds to the challenge of realism.

The core academic contribution of this paper lies in: shifting the problem of "eternal truth" from ontology (existence/non-existence) to epistemology (decidable/undecidable); providing a clear operational-semantic definition of "cognitive suspension" in the field of empirical philosophy, and establishing a dialogue with contemporary epistemological discussions on "suspension of judgment"; and explicitly delimiting the effective scope of the argument (empirical synthetic propositions).

**Keywords**: empirical world; eternal truth; cognitive suspension; operational semantics; temporal modality

## Literature Positioning and Scholarly Dialogue

Before proceeding to formal analysis, it is necessary to clarify the position of this paper within the existing scholarly landscape.

Discussions on "eternal truth" have mainly followed three trajectories:

**1. Classical Metaphysical Tradition (Plato, Descartes, Leibniz)**

Plato anchored "eternal truth" in the world of Forms, holding that the mutable sensible world cannot bear genuine knowledge; only the Forms (e.g., "Beauty itself", "Justice itself") are eternal and unchanging objects of truth. Descartes attempted to derive an eternal truth system guaranteed by God, starting from the *cogito*. Leibniz distinguished between "truths of reason" (necessarily true, e.g., logic/mathematics) and "truths of fact" (contingently true, e.g., empirical propositions), holding that the former are eternal, while the latter have certainty only in God's omniscient perspective.

It is worth pointing out that Leibniz's system provides a clear historical target for this paper: although "truths of fact" are regarded as contingent at the human level, in God's omniscient perspective, all true propositions (including empirical ones) possess a kind of eternity—they are eternally contained in the divine intellect. This paper does not discuss whether the divine perspective exists; it only points out that if the cognitive subject is finite and temporal, the operational procedure for this presupposition has never been specified. The object of attack in this paper is precisely this kind of claim—that "the eternity of an empirical proposition can be confirmed from some transcendental perspective."

This paper's treatment of Leibniz does not stop at merely pointing out the omission. A Leibnizian might respond: "God's omniscient perspective need not be specified by human operational procedures, because God is not a finite cognitive subject." This paper's reply is: if the divine perspective transcends all human cognitive procedures, then the assertion "eternal truth holds in God's perspective" provides **no discernible information gain** for human cognition—it is operationally indistinguishable from the assertion that "eternal truth does not exist at all." This is the core point of engagement with the Leibnizian tradition.

**2. Kant's Transcendental Philosophy (Kant, 1781/1787)**

Kant shifted "eternity" from empirical content to cognitive form. The forms of space-time intuition and the categories of causality are *a priori*, unchanging frameworks that make experience possible. In this sense, the eternity of the framework guarantees the unity of experience.

Kant's contribution lies in revealing the structural relationship between "unchanging form" and "changing empirical content." But his framework cannot guarantee the "eternal truth" of any empirical proposition—the framework is the window, not the view outside. This paper is in agreement with Kant on this point, but further argues that even if the framework is eternal, no concrete content within the framework can possess eternity—and that is precisely the problem under discussion.

**3. Logical Positivism and Operationalism (Schlick, 1936; Bridgman, 1927; Ayer, 1936)**

The Vienna Circle used the "verifiability principle" to demarcate meaningful from meaningless statements. Metaphysical statements (e.g., "the thing-in-itself exists") were considered cognitively meaningless because they lacked an operable verification procedure. Bridgman's operationalism went further: the meaning of a concept is equal to the totality of operations associated with it.

This tradition provides the methodological starting point for this paper. However, it must be noted that operationalism is not without controversy—Putnam (1960) and Hempel (1950) criticized it for failing to explain the identity of a concept across different measurement conditions (e.g., the definition of "length" in general relativity and its variation with measurement methods). This paper borrows only the core intuition of operationalism—that meaning is bound to operations—and confines it to the specific issue of "executability of decision procedures," without taking on its full theoretical commitments. This paper also differs from logical positivism in a key respect: this paper does not claim that "eternal truth is meaningless" (which is too broad), but rather that "eternal truth is in a state of cognitive suspension"—that is, its cognitive status is neither true nor false, because the verification procedure it requires is structurally unexecutable. This is a more precise and more restrained judgment than "meaningless."

**4. Contemporary Epistemology and Philosophy of Time**

In contemporary analytic philosophy, the distinction between "eternal propositions" and "temporal propositions" (Kaplan, 1989; Perry, 1979) provides important background. Eternal propositions are defined as those whose truth value does not change over time—paradigmatically, logical and mathematical propositions. This paper does not discuss such propositions, but focuses on those hybrid cases that appear to be "eternal" in form but are assigned empirical content—namely, the Leibnizian claim that "truths of fact possess eternity from a transcendental perspective."

In addition, the debate between "eternalism" and "presentism" in the philosophy of time (McTaggart, 1908; Prior, 1967) concerns whether the future has ontological status. This paper does not engage with this ontological debate, but points out that even if the future ontologically "exists", the cognition of the future by a finite subject is still constrained by time-bound decision procedures—this observation is independent of any ontological stance on time.

Dummett (1978) proposed that "meaning is verification conditions," binding the meaning of a proposition to its verifiable procedures. Putnam's (1981) "brain-in-a-vat" thought experiment challenges the simple correspondence between truth and experience. Williamson (2000) insists on a "knowledge-first" approach, attempting to separate knowledge from justified true belief.

**Dialogue with the literature on suspension of judgment**: The core concept of this paper, "cognitive suspension", is closely related to contemporary epistemological discussions on "suspension of judgment." Friedman (2013, 2017) points out that suspension of judgment is a cognitive attitude—neither belief nor disbelief, but a posture of "withholding" in the face of insufficient evidence. Rosen (2001) further analyzes the conditions under which suspension of judgment is rational, arguing that when evidence is insufficient to support either belief or disbelief, suspension is the only rational cognitive attitude.

The "cognitive suspension" of this paper shares an intuitive affinity with the above discussions, but differs in the following respects: (1) Friedman and Rosen discuss suspension due to **insufficient evidence**, whereas this paper discusses suspension due to **structural unexecutability of the procedure**—the source of "undecidability" is different (the former is provisional and can be changed by new evidence; the latter is principled and determined by the structure of the cognitive procedure); (2) this paper extends "suspension" from a cognitive attitude to a cognitive state—the state in which "eternal truth" resides in empirical statements—an extension not yet fully explored in the existing literature. The contribution of this paper is to extend the epistemological analysis of suspension of judgment from contexts of insufficient evidence to contexts of **procedural unexecutability**.

**5. Relationship to Popper, Peirce, and Kuhn**

This paper inherits from and differs from these thinkers on three key points:

**Relation to Popper (1934)**: Popper's falsificationism demarcates science from non-science by falsifiability, pointing out that universal propositions can be falsified but not verified. This paper inherits Popper's insight that "finite experience cannot verify a universal proposition," but extends it from the problem of demarcation ("what is science") to the problem of cognitive decidability ("what is decidable"). This is a **cross-domain migration**: applying Popper's tool to a problem that Popper himself did not address.

**Relation to Peirce (1878)**: Peirce's pragmatic maxim states that the meaning of a concept lies in its conceivable practical effects. This paper inherits this core intuition, but operationalizes it as "the executability of a decision procedure"—that is, the cognitive meaning of a concept lies in whether its verification procedure can be executed within finite time and terminate with a definite output. This is an **operationalization** of Peirce's maxim.

**Relation to Kuhn (1962)**: Kuhn's incommensurability thesis describes how core concepts undergo radical meaning shifts during paradigm changes. This paper borrows this concept as a supplementary argument, pointing out that "eternal truth" not only demands infinite verification, but also requires linguistic identity to remain stable over time—and Kuhn's work shows that such stability is historically rare. This is a **cross-domain application** of Kuhn's concept.

**6. Scholarly Position of This Paper**

In summary, the scholarly position of this paper is: within the methodological framework of operational semantics, it focuses on the **temporal dimension of empirical propositions**—specifically, it argues that "eternal" as a temporal modality cannot acquire an executable decision procedure in empirical statements. This paper does not engage with the ontological question of "whether truth exists"; it only asks whether truth can be crowned as "eternal."

## Theoretical Framework and Methodological Statement

### Definitions of Core Concepts

Before proceeding to the argument, it is necessary to define the core concepts used in this paper rigorously.

**Empirical World**: The domain of phenomena that can be decided within finite time through observation, measurement, inference, etc. The empirical world is constrained by two conditions: (1) temporality—every decision is executed in the present; (2) finiteness—any decision procedure must be completed within finite steps (or be physically cognitively feasible—i.e., the procedure does not violate known physical laws and the finite constraints of the cognitive system). This paper does not discuss logical feasibility (which is a matter of formal systems), but only physical-cognitive feasibility.

**Defense of the finiteness presupposition**: This paper presupposes that the cognitive subject is finite and temporal. The basis for this presupposition is that all known physical systems are operationally constrained by finite time and energy resources—this is a consequence of the second law of thermodynamics and energy conservation, not merely empirical observation. If a fundamental paradigm shift were to occur in the future (e.g., discovery of a non-temporal mode of cognition), the conclusions of this paper would need to be revised.

**Eternal (operational-semantic definition)**: "Eternal", as a temporal modality, has the semantic content: for any future time point, proposition P is true at that moment. This is a universal assertion covering an infinite temporal domain. Its verification procedure would require verifying P's truth at infinitely many future time points. The procedure has two structural features: (1) infinite steps (because the future is infinite); (2) non-terminating (because verification never completes).

**Cognitive Suspension**: This paper introduces this concept to denote a state in which the truth value of a proposition can neither be verified nor falsified within finite time, and this double impossibility arises from structural limitations of the cognitive procedure, not from provisional insufficiency of evidence.

It must be noted that "cognitive suspension" contains two dimensions, which together constitute its full meaning:

- **As a factual judgment (undecidable)**: The verification procedure for an eternal proposition is structurally unexecutable—it can neither be proved true nor false. This is a factual description of the cognitive procedure.

- **As a methodological recommendation (suspend judgment)**: In the face of this undecidability, the rational epistemological stance is to suspend judgment on the truth of the proposition until a new procedure appears (and this paper has argued that such a new procedure is structurally impossible, so this "provisional" suspension may in principle be permanent).

**The rational basis for "suspend judgment"**: This methodological recommendation is not presupposed, but is based on the following argument: when "P is true" and "P is false" are operationally indistinguishable (i.e., they produce identical terminal cognitive states), continuing to insist on either "P is true" or "P is false" lacks any cognitive basis—because there is no executable procedure that can support either side. In this situation, suspension of judgment is the only posture consistent with the evidential state of the cognitive procedure. This argument transforms "suspend judgment" from a definitional feature into an independently supported conclusion.

In the context of empirical epistemology, it is equivalent to a state of "undecidability", but this paper deliberately uses "cognitive suspension" to distinguish it from Gödel-Turing style "undecidability" (a concept within formal systems). It should be pointed out that "cognitive suspension" and "undecidability" are **logically isomorphic**—both are "neither provably true nor provably false." The difference between them lies not in logical structure but in **domain of application**: undecidability is a metamathematical concept within formal systems, with formal derivation as its criterion; cognitive suspension belongs to the domain of empirical epistemology, with executable verification procedures as its criterion. This paper does not claim that the two are logically different; it only clarifies that their domains differ, to avoid terminological confusion.

**The key feature distinguishing "cognitive suspension" from purely formal "undecidability"** is that it not only denotes a procedural state of undecidability, but also includes an epistemological response to that state—namely, the rationality of suspending judgment. Purely formal "undecidability" merely describes a logical state (a proposition is neither provable nor disprovable in an axiom system) and does not include any recommendation about how a cognitive subject should treat that state. "Cognitive suspension" binds this logical state to an epistemological stance (suspension of judgment), thus extending the concept from formal systems to the domain of empirical epistemology. This is the real increment that "cognitive suspension" offers over "undecidability."

**Formal Truth vs. Contentual Truth**: Formal truth (understood here as analytic propositions of logic and mathematics) does not depend on empirical observation; its truth is guaranteed by validity within its axiom system. This paper does not take these as objects of argument, but rather as the **formal scaffolding** for the argument—that is, the argument depends on the validity of logical inference, but its subject is the eternity of empirical content.

### Methodological Statement

The core argument of this paper rests on the premise that **decidability is a necessary condition for truth to be cognitively accessible**. That is, if the decisional state of a proposition is in principle unattainable by any finite cognitive procedure, then that proposition cannot be considered "cognitively accessible." This premise is not uncontroversial—realists (e.g., Putnam, Davidson) reject it, holding that truth can be independent of any verification procedure.

This paper offers the following defense of this premise:

(1) **The finitude of cognition**: Human cognition is physically constrained by finite time and energy resources (a consequence of the second law of thermodynamics and energy conservation). Any content claimed to be "cognizable" must be reachable within the operational window of a physical cognitive agent. If a decisional state is in principle never reachable, then claiming that it is "cognizable" has no cognitive significance.

(2) **Operational indistinguishability**: For the two cases "P is true but undecidable" and "P is false but undecidable," human cognition cannot make any operational distinction—they yield identical terminal output states. Following Peirce's pragmatic maxim (Peirce, 1878), the meaning of a concept lies in its conceivable practical effects; if in any executable verification procedure the two are indistinguishable, then the distinction is cognitively suspended.

These two layers together constitute the defense of the premise that "decidability is a necessary condition for cognitive accessibility." This paper acknowledges that this defense does not constitute a complete refutation of realism—a realist can still insist that "truth is independent of decidability." But the defense at least shows that accepting the premise is reasonable, not dogmatic.

### Direct Response to Realist Presuppositions

One possible realist objection is: "A proposition could be true even if we can never verify it—for example, 'there is a stone on the other side of the universe.' Therefore, 'eternal truth,' even if unverifiable, might still be true."

This paper's response is layered:

(1) This paper does not deny the ontological possibility of such "might be true." This paper's stance is epistemological, not ontological.

(2) "Might be true but unverifiable" and "might be false but unverifiable" are cognitively indistinguishable. Therefore, such a truth-value does not yield any informational increment for the cognitive state of human subjects.

(3) This paper's stance can be traced back to Peirce's pragmatic maxim (Peirce, 1878): the meaning of a concept lies in its conceivable practical effects. If in any executable verification procedure, "P is eternal" and "P is not eternal" are indistinguishable in terminal output, then the distinction is cognitively suspended.

(4) This paper does not claim that "eternal truth does not exist," only that "eternal truth is not accessible to human cognition." Decidability is the operational criterion used by this paper to determine whether a proposition is cognitively accessible.

(5) The realist objection touches a deeper issue: if truth can exist independently of decidability, what is the legitimacy of the framework of "cognitive accessibility"? This paper's reply is: the realist position is legitimate at the metaphysical level, but it does not serve the question of "how do humans cognize?" This paper's framework is not ontological but epistemological—it asks not "what is the world like?" but "what can we know?" If the realist holds that truth is independent of cognition, this paper respects that position, but points out that once that position is accepted, we lose the ground for discussing the status of "eternal truth" in human cognition. The argument of this paper is addressed only to those who hold that "truth is relevant to cognition."

(6) Therefore, the argumentative strategy of this paper is: **not to refute realism, but to point out that once realism is accepted, the discussion of "the cognitive status of eternal truth" becomes meaningless.** This paper chooses to analyze within the framework of "truth is relevant to cognition," and this choice is justified (see the two-layer defense above), but it is not the only legitimate choice.

**Scope Statement**: The conclusions of this paper apply only to the "eternal" assertion of empirical synthetic propositions in their temporal dimension. They do not apply to analytic truths of logic and mathematics. The argument depends on the epistemological premise that "decidability is a necessary condition for cognitive accessibility"; if that premise is rejected, the conclusions do not apply.

## I. Introduction: Empirical World, Eternal Truth — That Comma

In everyday language, "truth" and "eternal" are frequently bound together. "Eternal truth"—the phrase sounds solemn, profound, as if pointing to something unchanging beyond time. Throughout the history of philosophy, countless systems have attempted to fill this phrase with content: Plato's Forms, the Christian God, Hegel's Absolute Spirit, and various versions of "historical laws."

But when we turn our gaze from the history of philosophy to everyday cognition—consider a concrete empirical proposition such as "water boils at 100 degrees Celsius under standard atmospheric pressure"—how do we judge that it is "eternally" true? Can we, in the present, complete the verification of "eternal"?

This question echoes in the philosophy of science. Popper (1934) demarcated science from non-science by falsifiability, pointing out that universal propositions cannot be verified by finite experience, only falsified. But this paper does not ask about "verification"; it asks a deeper question: if a proposition cannot even be falsified—because it claims not "true for all past and present" but "true for all future times"—then what cognitive state is it in? This is not a question of "insufficient evidence." This is a question of "the verification procedure being structurally uncompletable."

It is worth noting that the object of attack in this paper is not a straw man. Leibniz (1714) explicitly argued in his system that although "truths of fact" (e.g., "Caesar crossed the Rubicon") are contingent and empirical at the human level, they are eternally contained in God's intellect from God's omniscient perspective, and thus possess a kind of eternity in a transcendent sense. This presupposition posits a non-temporal cognitive agent (God) capable of stepping outside time to confirm the eternity of empirical propositions. This paper does not discuss whether this presupposition is "true"; it only points out that if the cognitive subject is finite and temporal, the operational procedure for this presupposition has never been specified.

My core judgment is this: "truth" within the empirical world is always local, temporal, and revisable; "eternal" points to an infinite temporal axis. There is no gradual passage from truth to eternal truth—from truth within finite time to eternal truth across infinite time is not a matter of "accumulation of quantity" but a matter of "qualitative change."

**Dialogue with the Chinese Philosophical Tradition**:

Before entering the formal argument, it is necessary to briefly indicate the relevance of this paper's problematic to the Chinese philosophical tradition.

Chinese philosophy approaches the questions of "eternal" and "truth" from a different epistemological angle than the Western tradition. In the Daoist tradition, Laozi says "The Dao that can be spoken is not the eternal Dao; the name that can be named is not the eternal name" (Daodejing, Chapter 1), pointing out that the ultimate Dao lies beyond language and conceptual grasp. The "eternal Dao" is intuitively akin to the "eternal" discussed in this paper, but Laozi's concern is the inherent limitation of language, whereas this paper's concern is the execution structure of cognitive procedures—the difference lies precisely in the analytic path, not in the opposition of conclusions. In the Buddhist tradition, "all conditioned phenomena are impermanent" (Platform Sutra) fundamentally denies the eternity of all phenomenal worlds, while simultaneously pointing to a "dharma-nature abiding forever" beyond birth and death—this dialectical understanding aligns in direction with this paper's move of anchoring "eternal" as a temporal modality and thereby separating it from empirical content, but this paper does not engage with the soteriological dimension of Buddhism. In the Confucian tradition, Zhu Xi says "Heaven does not change; the Dao does not change" (Collected Commentaries on the Four Books), but the "unchanging" here is an eternal in the value-theoretic sense (Heavenly Principle, the Dao), not the constancy of truth-values of empirical propositions in the epistemological sense—and precisely this distinction is the central concern of this paper.

The contribution of this paper lies not in repeating these insights, but in providing them with an operable analytic path. This section aims to clarify the potential connection of this paper's problematic to the Chinese philosophical tradition, not to use it as a premise or basis for the argument—the core argument of this paper stands independently within the analytic philosophical framework.

Therefore, this paper does not attempt to answer "does eternal truth exist?" The very formulation of that question is problematic—it presupposes a cognitive subject capable of stepping outside the empirical world. This paper does only one thing: **draws a boundary**. It delimits the legitimate domain of empirical cognition, and points out that the attempt of "eternal truth" to place itself within this domain is invalid—because the infinite verification procedure required by that placement structurally crosses the boundary of the domain.

The comma in the title is the conclusion of this paper—"Empirical World" and "Eternal Truth" are juxtaposed, but no connective word marks a passage between them. No "and", no "leads to", no "transcends"—only a silent comma. The reader will understand the meaning of this comma after reading the full text: it marks an uncrossable gap.

## II. The Time-Binding of Decision Procedures

The judgment of the truth or falsity of any empirical proposition depends on a cognitive-operational procedure. This procedure has the following characteristics:

1. **It has a beginning**: the judgment begins at some moment.
2. **It has an end**: the judgment outputs a result (true/false/uncertain) at some moment.
3. **Finite steps**: the procedure is completed within finite observations, measurements, and inferences.

Example: "The temperature in Beijing today is 25 degrees Celsius"—the decision procedure is: measure the temperature in Beijing with a thermometer at a certain time on that day, read the value. The procedure is completed within finite time.

Even the most complex scientific judgment (e.g., "is global warming caused by human activity?") follows the same principle of time-binding—data collection, model building, testing—all steps are executed within finite time. The judgment is a state **output at some temporal point**.

Corollary: every empirical judgment bears a timestamp. No empirical judgment can be executed "outside time."

It should be noted that this principle of "time-binding" is a core premise of this paper. It is based on physical fact: any judgment procedure's output is an event; an event occurs within a finite time interval; outputting a signal of "all verification complete" requires the procedure to enter a terminal state. But "infinite verification" means that the terminal state can never be reached—because the very requirement of "completing all infinite steps" is self-contradictory: even after executing an arbitrarily large finite number of steps, there remain infinitely many steps not yet executed, so the signal "complete" can never be output. Therefore, this uncompletability is constrained both by logical structure and by physical law.

## III. The Non-Temporal Requirement of "Eternal"

Now consider an empirical proposition P: "water boils at 100 degrees Celsius under standard atmospheric pressure." P is judged true in the present.

Now combine P with "eternal" to form proposition E: "the fact that water boils at 100 degrees Celsius under standard atmospheric pressure is true at all future times."

What is the verification procedure for E?
- It would require verifying P at "all future times."
- This would require infinitely many measurements executed at infinitely many temporal points.
- But any decision procedure of a cognitive subject is time-bound and finite-stepped.

**Contradiction**: The verification procedure for E requires infinite steps to be completed within finite time—which is structurally impossible.

In other words, the verification procedure for E has no executable termination condition. It is not "not yet completed"; it is physically-cognitively uncompletable. No matter how many years of observational data humanity accumulates, it remains a finite set, never covering "all future times."

## IV. Three Forms of Cognitive Suspension

The cognitive suspension of "eternally true" can be decomposed into the following three dimensions:

| Dimension | Description | Cognitive Status |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Unverifiable | Cannot, within finite time, complete verification of "true at all future times" | Cognitive Suspension |
| Unfalsifiable | Cannot, within finite time, rule out the possibility of "false at some future time" | Cognitive Suspension |
| Combined | Both of the above hold simultaneously | Cognitive Suspension (Complete) |

The core thesis of this paper is: **"eternal truth" (as an attribute of empirical propositions) is in the third state—complete cognitive suspension.** It is not a provisional suspension due to "insufficient evidence" (e.g., "will it rain tomorrow?"), but a state in which the verification procedure is physically-cognitively uncompletable.

It is worth noting that unfalsifiability plays a key role here: Popper (1934) already pointed out that universal propositions cannot be verified, but can be falsified. However, for "eternal" propositions, even the falsification procedure cannot be executed: to falsify "P is true at all future times," one would need to find a future time at which P is false. But "finding a future time" requires traversing an infinite temporal domain to confirm that "none is found"—this is also an infinite operation.

Therefore, unverifiability and unfalsifiability hold simultaneously for "eternal" propositions, jointly constituting a complete state of cognitive suspension.

**The semantic change dimension**: Even if we assume infinite verification capability, "eternal truth" still faces another structural obstacle—semantic change. Kuhn's (1962) work shows that paradigm changes involve **incommensurability** of core concepts: in the chemical revolution from Lavoisier to the present, the concept of "water" itself underwent fundamental reconstructions, such that across paradigms it is no longer the same concept. Core concepts under different paradigms have different meaning networks and referential structures; cross-paradigm truth comparisons thus lose their foundation of identity. If the concept of "water" is redefined in a future scientific revolution, can "water boils at 100 degrees Celsius" still be regarded as "the same" proposition?

This "semantic change" argument is not an independent line of attack separate from the core argument, but rather a **supplement** to it: the core argument shows that "eternal truth" is unverifiable; the semantic change argument further shows that even if verification were possible in principle, the identity of "the same proposition" is itself unstable across time. Both point to the same conclusion—"eternal truth" is cognitively inaccessible—but the former proceeds from the temporal structure of verification procedures, while the latter proceeds from the semantic identity of propositions; they are two supporting dimensions under the same conclusion.

## V. Responses to Possible Objections

**Objection 1: "In the future, AI or superintelligence could execute infinite verification."**

Response: Even if there existed an intelligence with infinite computational capacity, it would still be constrained by the structure of output mechanisms. Even with distributed parallel computation, "infinite execution" is itself a tautology for "never terminates"—it can never output a final result of "all verification complete." The "output" of a verification procedure requires a terminal state; infinite verification means that the terminal state can never be reached. Parallel computation can compress processing time for finite sets, but it cannot change the logical fact that "an infinite set cannot be covered by a finite number of processors within finite time."

**Objection 2: "We can determine 'eternal' through logical inference rather than empirical verification."**

Response: If the proposition is empirical (e.g., "water boils at 100 degrees Celsius"), logical inference cannot replace empirical verification—logic cannot tell us that water boils at 100 degrees; it can only tell us "if defined thus, then the conclusion follows." Empirical content does not come from logical deduction.

**Objection 3: "Eternal truth does not require human verification; it is 'just' true."**

Response: This leaps from "existence" to "decidability." This paper does not discuss whether it "just exists"—only whether it "can be cognized." A "just true" status that cannot be accessed by any cognitive procedure is, for a cognitive subject, cognitively suspended.

**Objection 4: "Realism allows truth to be independent of verification."**

Response: This paper accepts the logical possibility of realism at the metaphysical level. But the subject of this paper is epistemological, not ontological. A truth-value that cannot be accessed by any cognitive procedure—regardless of whether it is true in the world—is suspended for human cognition. The conclusion of this paper is that we have no epistemological reason to upgrade "P is true" to "P is eternally true," because such an upgrade cannot be confirmed by any executable cognitive procedure.

A realist may legitimately reject this conclusion and insist that "truth is independent of decidability." This paper does not attempt to refute that position, but points out that once realism is accepted, the discussion of "the cognitive status of eternal truth" becomes meaningless—because the realist has already removed truth from the domain of human cognition. This paper chooses to analyze within the framework of "truth is relevant to cognition," and this choice is based on the two-layer defense provided above.

## VI. Rupture — Between the Empirical World and Eternal Truth

### 1. Locating the Rupture

The rupture does not occur "in the world itself." The world may have its own mode of operation, and in some sense may "eternally" follow certain laws—this paper does not deny this possibility. The rupture occurs **within the operational structure of finite cognitive subjects**: decision procedures are temporal, while the concept of "eternal" demands a non-temporal confirmation. There is no gradual transition between these two structures.

### 2. The Nature of the Rupture

This is not a defect, but a boundary. Just as one cannot see the back of one's own head with one's eyes—not because "the back of the head does not exist," but because "that perspective does not belong to you." Similarly, "the eternal truth of an empirical proposition" cannot be decided, not because "eternal truth" is too profound, but because "the decision of eternal" is an operation that does not fall within the legitimate domain of human cognition.

### 3. Grammatical Projection

This paper endorses a mild linguistic observation: the tense structure of natural language (past-present-future) tends to produce a grammatical projection—because we can talk about "the future," we mistakenly believe that "the infinite future" corresponds to a substantive attribute "eternal." But linguistic analysis shows that "eternal" is not a container for substantive attributes, but a **temporal modality**—its function is to alter the temporal scope of a proposition, not to add some transcendental certainty to it.

---

## VII. Conclusion: The Meaning of That Comma

At this point, we can return to the title and understand the meaning of that comma.

"Empirical World" and "Eternal Truth" are juxtaposed in the same title, but there is no connective between them. No "and", no "leads to", no "transcends"—only a silent comma, marking an uncrossable gap.

Truth in the empirical world is local, temporal, and revisable. It is not "false," but "does not carry eternity"—just as a person's height does not carry the attribute "forever." Eternal truth, if understood as some super-temporal attribute of empirical propositions, is in a state of cognitive suspension with respect to cognitive procedures. This paper does not claim that it "does not exist," only that within the epistemological framework of operational semantics, it is "not accessible."

The conclusion of this paper is not equivalent to nihilism—the latter negates all meaning; this paper only negates the cognitive accessibility of transcendental eternity within the empirical domain. The legitimate domain of meaning lies within the empirical world; outside that domain, there is no executable decision procedure. And "eternal truth" is precisely an operation that attempts to stand outside the domain, while demanding empirical content within the domain to fill its value.

**From cognitive suspension to analytic conclusion: a chain of derivation**

The conclusion of this paper can be divided into two levels:

**First level (epistemological conclusion)**: Within the epistemological framework of operational semantics, eternal truth is cognitively suspended. The "eternal truth" of an empirical proposition is in a cognitive state of being neither verifiable nor falsifiable.

**Second level (analytic conclusion)**: Based on this epistemological conclusion, within the cognitive-operational dimension of empirical statements, "P is eternal" cannot be understood as "P is cognitively confirmed as temporally invariant," but should be understood as a rhetorical expression of attitude—namely, "P is placed by the speaker in an unshakeable position of belief," rather than a factual statement confirmed by cognitive procedures. The justification for this interpretation is as follows: since "eternal" cannot function as a cognitive category, its executable role in the cognitive-operational dimension of empirical propositions can only be rhetorical—expressing the speaker's attitude, not stating facts about the world.

**Scope and significance of the conclusions of this paper**:

The conclusions of this paper are conclusions within the framework of operational semantics; their validity is limited to that framework.

(1) **In epistemological discussions**: "eternal truth" should not be used as a cognitive category. When we discuss "how do we know the world," "P is eternal" is not a statement that can be confirmed by cognitive procedures; it cannot be used as a knowledge claim.

(2) **In everyday language and rhetorical contexts**: "eternal truth" can still legitimately exist, but should be understood as a rhetorical expression (e.g., "I firmly believe this is eternal" or "this is unshakeable for me"), rather than a factual claim about cognition.

(3) **In moral and aesthetic contexts**: The conclusions of this paper do not apply to the "eternality" of moral judgments or aesthetic judgments—because those domains do not depend on empirical verification procedures; this paper's argumentative framework does not cover them. This paper only deals with empirical propositions.

(4) **Echo with the Chinese philosophical tradition**: The conclusions of this paper have a deep resonance with the insights in the Chinese philosophical tradition regarding "the Dao that cannot be spoken" and "all conditioned phenomena are impermanent"; see the section "Dialogue with the Chinese Philosophical Tradition" in the Introduction for details.

**It must be emphasized again**: The conclusion of this paper depends on the epistemological premise that "decidability is a necessary condition for cognitive accessibility." This paper has provided two layers of defense for this premise (see Methodological Statement) and directly responded to the realist challenge. The conclusion holds within the framework; this paper does not claim it as a cross-framework absolute truth.

Final sentence:

> *"Between the empirical world and eternal truth, there is no bridge. They are two ends suspended in the same title by language, and that comma is the boundary of cognition."*

---

## VIII. Falsifiability Conditions

The core judgment of this paper is: "eternal truth" (as an attribute of empirical propositions) is in a state of cognitive suspension with respect to cognitive operations.

The core conclusion of this paper would need to be revised if any of the following conditions were to occur:

1. **If physics or cognitive science provided conclusive evidence for a cognitive mode capable of completing, within finite time, full verification covering an infinite temporal span (i.e., a "supratemporal" or "non-temporal" cognitive mode)**—this would directly negate the core premise of this paper.

2. **If future research in the history of science showed that core concepts did not undergo Kuhn-style incommensurable reconstructions during paradigm changes, but rather were gradually refined within the same conceptual framework**—this would weaken the supplementary argument in Section IV. But the history of science to date (from Lavoisier to Einstein) suggests that incommensurable reconstructions are the norm, not the exception.

It should be emphasized that the first falsifiability condition above involves fundamental paradigms of modern physics. If those paradigms are not overturned, the core argument of this paper holds. The conclusion of this paper is not a trans-paradigm absolute truth, but a structural analysis based on current physical consensus.
