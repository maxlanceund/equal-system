# Uncertainty Subsumption: The Production, Locking, and Release of Certainty

> **Core Thesis**: Certainty is not an inherent property of the world, but a temporary state that finite systems draw from uncertainty during operation. Once the operation is complete, this state is returned to uncertainty. This process is called "uncertainty subsumption." It constitutes the foundational layer of *The Limit Theory of Meaning* and *Boundary Acceleration*, answering the question: "Why must certainty return to zero?"

---

## Abstract

Certainty is the exception, not the default. Physics has shown across three scales that certainty is merely a temporary marker on a background of uncertainty. At the microscopic scale, quantum mechanics tells us: particles have no definite position before measurement (Wheeler & Zurek, 1983). At the macroscopic scale, thermodynamics tells us: isolated systems inevitably evolve toward entropy increase (Atkins, 2018). At the cosmic scale, cosmology tells us: visible, determinate matter constitutes only about 5% of the universe—dark matter and dark energy indicate that certainty was never locked in to begin with (Perlmutter et al., 1999; Riess et al., 1998). This paper proposes "uncertainty subsumption" as a unified description of this foundational structure, demonstrating how certainty is produced, how it is locked, and how it is inevitably returned. It also examines the position of the observer, defining the observer not as an external witness but as a finite system that participates in the structure's operation. This paper provides the foundational support for *The Limit Theory of Meaning* (Lanceund, 2026a) and *Boundary Acceleration* (Lanceund, 2026b).

**Keywords**: uncertainty subsumption; certainty; entropy; observer; finite interval

## Literature Positioning and Scholarly Dialogue

Before proceeding to the formal analysis, it is necessary to clarify this paper's position within the existing scholarly landscape.

On the origin and destination of certainty, the existing literature primarily contains two paths:

1. **The physics path** (thermodynamics, quantum mechanics, cosmology): The second law of thermodynamics describes the inevitable evolution of determinate structures toward indeterminacy (Atkins, 2018, Ch. 3); quantum decoherence describes how superpositions produce observational certainty through interaction with the environment, and how that certainty is then returned to indeterminacy (Wheeler & Zurek, 1983; Zurek, 2003); dark matter and dark energy in cosmology indicate that observable, determinate matter constitutes only a small fraction of the universe's total mass-energy (Perlmutter et al., 1999; Riess et al., 1998)—the vast majority consists of invisible components. This is not certainty being returned, but certainty never having been locked in.

2. **The philosophy path** (epistemology, phenomenology, philosophy of language): From Kant's epistemological framework to Wittgenstein's linguistic boundaries, philosophy has persistently asked: "How do we know what we know?" But the philosophical tradition has tended to treat "uncertainty" as a cognitive defect rather than as the world's foundational state.

Wittgenstein, in the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1922), drew a boundary between what can be said and what cannot be said—propositions can describe facts, but ethics, aesthetics, and meaning itself fall outside that boundary, consigned to silence. But he did not explain why the boundary exists. He stopped at "must remain silent," without asking further: after silence, what is the boundary itself? The "uncertainty subsumption" framework proposed in this paper can answer this question: the boundary is not the end of logic, but the position where certainty is returned to uncertainty. Wittgenstein's "silence" is not the end, but the zeroing of signal at the boundary.

The contribution of this paper lies in integrating structures already confirmed by physics—entropy increase, decoherence, dark energy dominance—into a unified philosophical description, named "uncertainty subsumption." It argues that certainty is not an achievement of cognition, but a product of operation; uncertainty is not a failure of cognition, but the default state.

This paper is the **foundational structure** paper of the Equal System series. It sits beneath *The Limit Theory of Meaning* (middle layer: how certainty operates within boundaries) (Lanceund, 2026a) and *Boundary Acceleration* (middle layer: the dynamics of certainty's release) (Lanceund, 2026b), providing them with a physical-philosophical foundation.

## Theoretical Framework and Methodological Statement

### Definition of Core Concepts

Before proceeding, it is necessary to strictly define the core concepts used in this paper.

**System**: A finite unit capable of receiving input, processing signals, executing output, and receiving feedback. It may be a physical system (e.g., a particle), a biological system (e.g., an organism), a cognitive system (e.g., a human), or a social system (e.g., an institution). All references to "system" in this paper fall within this definition.

**Uncertainty**: The default state of a system. In uncertainty, the system has not locked onto any specific identifiable signal. Uncertainty is not "nothingness," nor is it "passive blankness"—it is an operable state in which the system has not yet locked onto a signal, possessing potential informational capacity. The quantum superposition state is an instance of this: a particle has no definite position before measurement, existing in a superposition of multiple possibilities (Wheeler & Zurek, 1983).

**Certainty**: An identifiable signal temporarily locked and maintained by a system during operation, drawn from uncertainty. It is not an inherent property of the world, but an intermediate state within the operational process. In quantum mechanics, this is the result of wavefunction collapse to a particular eigenstate (Zurek, 2003).

**Operation**: A closed-loop process in which a system receives input, processes signals, executes output, and receives feedback. Operation requires an "identifiable input signal"—which requires certainty to be temporarily produced.

**Uncertainty Subsumption**: The process by which certainty is produced, locked, and then returned to uncertainty after the operation is complete. It consists of three phases: (1) certainty is produced (the system locks onto a signal); (2) certainty is maintained (the system continuously refreshes the signal); (3) certainty is returned (the system stops refreshing; the signal is reclaimed by entropy increase).

**Boundary**: The position where certainty must be returned. It is not an endpoint, but a node of "return."

### Methodological Statement

The methodological path adopted in this paper is **cross-scale structural correspondence**. Specifically, this paper does not provide new physical evidence, nor does it attempt to replace existing physical descriptions. Its work is to recognize structures already confirmed by physics across three scales—microscopic (quantum), macroscopic (thermodynamic), and cosmic (cosmological)—as manifestations of the same underlying structure at different levels, and to provide it with a unified philosophical naming and description.

The types of evidence used in this paper include:

1. **Physical observational facts**: entropy increase (Atkins, 2018), quantum decoherence (Wheeler & Zurek, 1983; Zurek, 2003), dark energy dominance (Perlmutter et al., 1999; Riess et al., 1998).
2. **Structural correspondence**: integrating the above dispersed physical facts into a single structure's different manifestations.
3. **Mathematical formalization**: using formulas to express the relationships of certainty's production, locking, and return.
4. **Logical derivation**: deriving "certainty is temporarily generated" from "operation requires identifiable signals."
5. **Conceptual definition**: strict definitions of concepts such as uncertainty subsumption, the borrowing of certainty, and the observer's position.

## I. Introduction: Why Certainty Needs to Be Explained

Certainty is the exception, not the default.

Humanity is accustomed to treating "certainty" as the normal state of the world. But physics has shown us across three scales that certainty is merely a temporary marker on a background of uncertainty.

At the microscopic scale, quantum mechanics tells us: particles have no definite position before measurement (Wheeler & Zurek, 1983). At the macroscopic scale, thermodynamics tells us: the determinate structures of isolated systems inevitably evolve toward entropy increase (Atkins, 2018). At the cosmic scale, cosmology tells us: visible, determinate matter constitutes only about 5% of the universe's total mass-energy—the remaining 95% is dark matter and dark energy. They exist, but we cannot see them (Perlmutter et al., 1999; Riess et al., 1998). This is not certainty being returned, but certainty never having been locked in.

This means: if we treat "certainty" as the default state, we cannot explain why it is the exception across all three scales of physics. If we treat "uncertainty" as the default state, then "certainty" requires explanation—where does it come from? Why does it exist? Why is it returned?

This paper attempts to answer these questions. I name this foundational structure: **uncertainty subsumption**.

## II. Certainty Is Drawn from Uncertainty

We assume that certainty is a property that things "inherently have." But in fact, certainty is a temporary state that a system draws from uncertainty during operation.

Uncertainty is not "nothingness," but an operable state—the system has not yet locked onto any signal; superposition or coexistence of signals is possible. It possesses potential informational capacity, but has not yet been converted by the system into identifiable signals. To produce certainty, the system must "extract" or "lock onto" a signal from this operable state.

Why is it necessary to draw certainty from uncertainty? Because operation is essentially a process of distinguishing inputs: the system needs to classify, order, respond to, or transmit inputs. If the input is entirely unidentifiable, the system cannot distinguish "this" from "that," and thus cannot complete any processing. Therefore, operation logically requires that the input be certain within the operational window. The signal must be temporarily fixed—located, marked, assigned an operational meaning—before it can be processed. This process of fixing is the production of certainty.

If certainty were an inherent property of the world, it should exist independently of any observational act. But quantum mechanics has shown that before observation, a particle's position is not certain (Wheeler & Zurek, 1983). This rules out the possibility that "certainty is an inherent property." Therefore, certainty must depend on the observational act—that is, system operation—and operation is a local event, not a universal one.

Thus, certainty is produced by operation, not inherent to the world. Since it is produced by operation, its existence is conditional—once the conditions disappear, it disappears. Therefore, it is temporary and drawn, not owned.

In quantum mechanics, this process is described as "measurement." Before measurement, the particle is in a superposition state (uncertainty), which can be expressed by the wavefunction:

```math
|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i |s_i\rangle
```

(Equation 1)

where |sᵢ⟩ are the various possible determinate states, cᵢ are the corresponding probability amplitudes, and |cᵢ|² gives the probability of observing that state. Before measurement occurs, the particle has no determinate state—it exists in a superposition of all possible states (Wheeler & Zurek, 1983).

Upon measurement, the wavefunction collapses to a particular eigenstate (certainty):

```math
|\psi\rangle \rightarrow |s_0\rangle
```

(Equation 2)

This collapse is not "discovering" a pre-existing fact, but the system forcibly "selecting" a result through the measurement operation. After measurement, the particle interacts with the environment, gradually decoheres, and certainty is returned to uncertainty (Zurek, 2003).

Certainty is not an ontological property of the world. It is an intermediate state within the operational process.

## III. Operation Ends, Certainty Is Returned

Certainty is produced for the sake of completing an operation. But once the operation is complete, certainty is no longer needed.

This is not a matter of "subjective abandonment"—maintaining certainty requires the system to continuously expend energy or information to refresh the signal. When the system stops refreshing, certainty loses its conditions of maintenance and is inevitably reclaimed by environmental noise, random fluctuations, or interactions (Atkins, 2018). This is not because the system "no longer needs" it, but because maintenance itself is a continuous operation—once it stops, return is the default direction.

The direction of entropy increase is determinate. Any ordered structure that has been produced—whether a physical system or an operational signal—will evolve toward disorder without maintenance. This is not "degeneration," but the system's default behavior.

Formally, the second law of thermodynamics can be stated as: the entropy change of an isolated system satisfies

```math
\Delta S \geq 0
```

(Equation 3)

where S is the system's entropy. The equality corresponds to reversible processes (an idealized limit), and the inequality corresponds to irreversible processes—that is, all real processes (Atkins, 2018, Ch. 3). This inequality expresses the inevitable direction of certainty's return: once certainty is produced, unless it is continuously maintained by external operation, it will be returned to uncertainty.

The boundary is that position—the position where certainty must be returned. The Limit Theory of Meaning (Lanceund, 2026a) describes how certainty operates within boundaries: it is defined within the interval and returns to zero at the boundary. But The Limit Theory of Meaning did not explain "why it must return to zero." The answer is here: because the nature of certainty is temporary. It is not "destroyed," but "returned."

Boundary Acceleration (Lanceund, 2026b) describes the increase in signal density before certainty is returned. Let D(t) be the signal density (the amount of identifiable signal carried by the system at time t), and T be the boundary time. Then:

```math
D(T) = 0
```

(Equation 4)

That is, the signal returns to zero at the boundary. But before returning to zero, signal density undergoes a sharp increase—this is the dynamic process that certainty must pass through before being returned at the boundary. Not because it is "struggling," but because it must complete its return within a finite window. (For detailed derivation, see Boundary Acceleration, Section III.)

## IV. Methodological Note: The Relationship Between Entropy and Uncertainty Subsumption

Before proceeding, it is necessary to clarify a key methodological point.

"Uncertainty subsumption" as proposed in this paper is not a synonym for "entropy," nor is it a replacement explanation for entropy. Entropy is a physical concept with a clear mathematical definition (S = k_B ln W) and experimental validation; uncertainty subsumption is a philosophical concept proposed in this paper to describe the philosophical implications of the same class of phenomena within an operational framework.

The relationship is this: entropy is the physical-level description; uncertainty subsumption is the operational-level description. They point to the same class of phenomena—the irreversible evolution of determinate structures toward indeterminacy—but belong to different descriptive levels and are not reducible to one another.

In other words, this paper's work is not "replacing physics with philosophy," but "translating facts already confirmed by physics into the language of the operational framework." Entropy remains valid; uncertainty subsumption is an attempt to name the operational correlate of entropy (signal refreshment stops → locking fails → signal returns to uncertainty).

With this clarification, we may proceed to discuss the implications of entropy within the operational framework.

## V. Entropy Is the Macroscopic Expression of Return

The second law of thermodynamics states: the entropy of an isolated system always increases.

The statistical definition of entropy—the Boltzmann formula—provides a clear correspondence:

```math
S = k_B \ln W
```

(Equation 5)

where k_B is the Boltzmann constant (approximately 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K), and W is the number of accessible microscopic states of the system (Atkins, 2018). When the system is in a determinate macroscopic state, it is locked into a limited set of microscopic states (W is small). When certainty is returned, the number of accessible microscopic states increases (W increases), and entropy increases.

"Entropy" is not a punishment, nor is it destruction. It is merely a direction—ordered, determinate structures necessarily tend to return to indeterminacy. Combustion is not "disappearance," but the return of the certainty of chemical bonds to the indeterminacy of thermal energy. Evolution is not "loss of information," but the filtering, elimination, and transformation of species structures by the environment, ultimately merging into new orders.

In the operational framework, "locking" corresponds to the system's continuous refreshing of the signal to resist entropy increase. When refreshing stops, the system slides from "this state" (fewer microscopic states) into "one of these possible states" (more microscopic states). This is the physical meaning of return.

If you translate entropy increase into the language of certainty's return, it looks like this:

· Production of certainty = The system produces an identifiable signal through operation.
· Maintenance of certainty = The system continuously locks and refreshes the signal.
· Return of certainty = Operation complete, the system stops refreshing, the signal is returned to uncertainty.

These three phases constitute a complete closed loop.

## VI. Uncertainty Subsumption: A Definition

Uncertainty subsumption refers to precisely this process:

Certainty is not a property of the world itself, but a temporary signal drawn by a finite system from uncertainty during operation. After the operation is complete, the signal is returned to uncertainty.

This process is called entropy increase in physics. In philosophy, this paper calls it uncertainty subsumption.

Uncertainty subsumption has four characteristics:

1. Certainty is produced, not discovered.
2. The production of certainty requires operation and boundary.
3. The return of certainty is the direction of entropy increase.
4. The observer is not outside this structure—the observer participates in the structure (see Section VII).

Expressed formally, let D(t) represent the degree of certainty of the system at time t (the extent to which a signal is locked), then:

· At the start of operation, the degree of certainty increases: D(0) > 0—the system locks a signal from uncertainty.
· During operation, the degree of certainty is maintained by the system: dD/dt = maintenance operation.
· At the boundary, the degree of certainty returns to zero: D(T) = 0—the signal is returned to uncertainty.

This is the complete trajectory of "uncertainty subsumption" within the operational framework.

Falsifiability: The core claim of this paper can be falsified by the following condition: if a determinate signal exists that, after the operation is complete and without any external maintenance, still maintains its certainty without being returned by entropy increase, then the "uncertainty subsumption" framework does not hold (Atkins, 2018). No such counterexample has yet been observed.

## VII. The Observer: An Operational Definition

In this framework, the observer is defined as any finite system capable of completing closed-loop operations, thereby producing determinate signals and registering the process of their return to uncertainty.

The observer is not a "person," nor is it "consciousness"—it may be a measuring apparatus, a biological organism, or any system satisfying the closed-loop definition. The "measuring apparatus" in quantum mechanics (Wheeler & Zurek, 1983) is an instance of an observer; the "finite system" defined in The Limit Theory of Meaning (Lanceund, 2026a) is another instance.

The observer's key properties are:

1. Finitude: Its operational window is finite. Within the window, it can lock onto determinate signals and register their return to uncertainty; outside the window, its operation terminates and its signals return to uncertainty.
2. Participation: The observer is not outside the process of uncertainty subsumption, but within it. The reason the observer can "see" certainty being returned is precisely because it participates in the production and locking of certainty. There is no external perspective.
3. Subsumability: The observer is not an exception. When the observer stops observing, the determinate signals it has produced—including its theories, memories, and identity—are all returned to uncertainty.

This framework does not address the question of "observer as conscious subject." Whether the observer can be equated with human consciousness lies beyond the scope of this paper. This paper provides only an operational definition: the observer is the system that produces certainty and, within its window, registers the return of that certainty to uncertainty.

This definition avoids entanglement with philosophy of mind while remaining compatible with both physics (quantum measurement apparatuses) and philosophy (finite cognitive systems).

## VIII. Conclusion: Boundary and Silence

The Limit Theory of Meaning (Lanceund, 2026a) deals with "how certainty operates within boundaries"—that is a middle-layer structure. Uncertainty Subsumption deals with "why certainty is returned"—that is the foundational structure.

The two are not contradictory, but operate at different levels. The foundational structure explains why the middle-layer structure must have boundaries. The "return to zero" in The Limit Theory of Meaning is not a sudden disappearance, but the moment when certainty is returned to uncertainty. The boundary is where certainty must be returned, not an arbitrary endpoint. The connection lies in the concept of "boundary" in The Limit Theory of Meaning: the boundary is not arbitrarily drawn, but is the position where certainty must be returned. Without uncertainty subsumption, boundaries would not need to exist.

Thus, this paper provides the physical foundation for The Limit Theory of Meaning: the boundary is not accidental, but a necessity of the direction of entropy increase.

However, this derivation, in completing its own construction, unavoidably touches upon a boundary question it cannot resolve: if all certainty is drawn from uncertainty, then who or what performed the first drawing—the operation by which the universe drew the first certainty from uncertainty at t = 0?

This paper has no answer to this question. But a crucial logical distinction must be made here:

"I have no answer" is not a dissolution of the question, nor is it a silent presupposition of some "trigger" beyond the question. It is merely an honest boundary marking: this question lies outside the range of this framework.

The very formulation "the first drawing" already presupposes that "drawing" is an event with a traceable origin—and that presupposition is itself a determinate assertion. This framework does not address questions outside itself. I therefore leave it beyond the boundary: undefined, unlocked, unreturned.

"Uncertainty Subsumption" completes its final operation here: it describes the full cycle of certainty's production, locking, and return—and then, at the boundary, recognizes itself as one more drawn form of certainty. This paper itself is a temporary drawing. It will eventually be forgotten, reclaimed by entropy increase, subsumed by uncertainty. But within its finite interval, it accurately described the structure in which it found itself.

What can be said has been said. The rest is left to silence.
