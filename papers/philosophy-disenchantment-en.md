# The Disenchantment of Philosophy: History's Deserter and Power's Most Perfect Servant

## Abstract

This paper begins with a question: why has philosophy become what it is today? Analytic philosophy cultivates the labyrinth of logic and language, continental philosophy entangles itself in the abyss of deconstruction and différance, and ethics is forced to perform the duty of proving to the outside world that "philosophy still has use."

This paper argues that this configuration is not accidental, but the result of three forces: historical trauma (philosophical systems once provided legitimacy for totalitarianism), institutional pressure (academic capitalism turned abstraction into a survival strategy), and the unresolved problem of capitalism (labor alienation was never solved, only amplified).

This paper further argues that the endpoint of this retreat—the abstract game—is not a failure to be mourned, but an acceptable endpoint. Philosophy's exemption from the disaster of guiding reality is already sufficient gain.

But the conclusion does not end there: philosophy's retreat into the abstract game is not philosophy's failure, but philosophy's disenchantment. Philosophy has never "intervened in reality"—not because it cannot, but because it dares not. It is abstract not because it seeks profundity, but because abstraction is the most respectable way of escaping reality. It is not an innocent victim of power; it is power's most perfect servant—because it pretends to resist, thereby making power appear resistible. Philosophy is not history's victim. Philosophy is history's deserter.

The abstract game has returned philosophy to where it always belonged: an intellectual game for a small circle, a concept-production workshop that does not resonate with power, an activity that honestly admits its own "uselessness." This is not failure. This is return. Philosophy does not need to be saved, because it was never "lost." It has simply finally admitted what it is.

**Keywords**: disenchantment; abstract game; ethics; academic capitalism; pragmatic arrogance; labor alienation; lightness of being; sex work; philosophy and reality; deserter

## Literature Positioning and Academic Dialogue

This paper has three polemical targets.

**First, the abstract game of hardcore philosophers.** Analytic philosophy retreats into the fortress of language and logic; continental philosophy sinks into the abyss of deconstruction and text. Their common feature: refusal to intervene in reality, refusal to speak plainly, refusal to bear ethical responsibility. This paper argues that this retreat is not an individual choice, but the result of historical trauma and institutional pressure. This paper does not condemn this retreat, but understands its necessity.

**Second, the awkward position of ethics.** Ethics is the only branch of philosophy still attempting to "speak plainly," but it is forced to serve as the "intermediary" and "security guard" between philosophy and reality. It knows that the foundation of morality has been deconstructed, yet it must still preach "justice," "rights," and "responsibility" from the podium. This paper argues that ethics' awkwardness is precisely the symptom of philosophy's overall retreat.

**Third, pragmatic arrogance.** Universities cut philosophy departments on the grounds of "uselessness," believing that philosophy, the humanities, and the social sciences cannot produce direct economic benefits. This paper argues that this pragmatic arrogance is itself a pathology that needs to be diagnosed. It cannot see the value of philosophy in "not guiding reality"—the value of avoiding disaster.

But this paper's polemical target does not end there. This paper ultimately challenges **philosophy's self-understanding over two thousand years**: philosophy believes itself to be "the love of wisdom," "the pursuit of truth," "the essence of the spirit of the age," "the sharp weapon of social critique." This paper's conclusion is: this self-understanding is itself an illusion. Philosophy has never been what it thought it was. And the more cruel truth is: philosophy is not evading what it is; it actively chose not to face what it is. It was not frightened into the study; it fled into the study on its own. It was not kidnapped by power; it actively served power—because it pretended to resist, thereby making power appear resistible.

## Introduction: A Question

Why has philosophy become what it is today?

This is not a rhetorical question. It points to a concrete historical fact: contemporary philosophy researchers—especially those in analytic and continental philosophy—are increasingly detached from reality, increasingly immersed in the abstract game. Analytic philosophers deduce modal logic in their studies; continental philosophers deconstruct power in texts; ethics is forced to stand at the front, proving to the outside world that "philosophy still has use."

How did this configuration form? What historical logic and structural pressures lie behind it? This paper attempts to answer this question.

This paper does not offer policy recommendations. It does not attempt to "save" philosophy, nor to "reform" the discipline. It does only one thing: diagnose—and then disenchant. Diagnose why philosophy has become what it is, and disenchant philosophy's misunderstanding of itself over two thousand years.

The conclusion is: **this is not a problem.** Philosophy's retreat into the abstract game is the result of historical trauma, institutional pressure, and disciplinary logic, but it is not a failure to be mourned. On the contrary, philosophy's exemption from the disaster of guiding reality is already sufficient gain. The real problem is not in philosophy, but in the pragmatic arrogance that keeps cutting philosophy departments on the grounds of "uselessness."

But the conclusion does not end there. This paper ultimately argues: **philosophy's retreat into the abstract game is not philosophy's failure, but philosophy's disenchantment.** Philosophy has never "intervened in reality"—not because it cannot, but because it dares not. It is abstract not because it seeks profundity, but because abstraction is the most respectable way of escaping reality. It does not guide reality not because it is incapable, but because it dares not. It is not an innocent victim of power; it is power's most perfect servant—because it pretends to resist, thereby making power appear resistible. Philosophy is not history's victim. Philosophy is history's deserter.

## I. Phenomenal Description—What Philosophy Looks Like Now

### Chapter One: What Is the Abstract Game

#### 1.1 Definition of the Abstract Game

Abstract game: the process by which philosophy researchers shift their research activity from "intervening in reality" to "formalized performance." Its features include: narrowing of research objects, increasing density of terminology, obsession with "clarity" or worship of "obscurity," and complete detachment from real life.

It is not a theoretical position, but a mode of existence—philosophy researchers no longer ask "how to change the world," but "whether the reference of this proposition is clear" or "how the différance of this text operates."

#### 1.2 The "Game-ness" of the Abstract Game

Rules: citation format, peer review, terminology system.

Competition: impact factor, citation rate, academic reputation.

Pleasure: the pleasure of puzzle-solving, the pleasure of subversion, the pleasure of intellectual climax.

Community: the analytic philosophy circle and the continental philosophy circle do not interact, each closed off.

#### 1.3 The Relationship Between the Abstract Game and "Reality"

Not escaping reality, but running parallel to it.

Not denying reality, but refusing to touch it.

Not betraying reality, but suspending it.

#### 1.4 Two Ways of "Influencing Reality"

**Way One: Grand Narrative Guidance**

Using big words like "absolute spirit," "historical necessity," "ultimate goal," directly telling people "how to live" and "how society should be organized." Goal: provide action plans, institutional design, historical direction. Language: clear, direct, addressed to the public. Consequence: easily appropriated by power, becoming totalitarian ideology.

**Way Two: Abstract Game Manufacturing**

Using obscure terms like "différance," "rhizome," "micro-physics of power," not directly telling people "how to live," but manufacturing new concepts, new languages, new ways of thinking. Goal: change the way people understand reality, not tell people what to do. Language: obscure, professional, addressed to peers. Consequence: difficult to appropriate by power, because it refuses to give clear plans.

**Key distinction:** The difference between "manufacturing reality" and "guiding reality" does not lie in "whether it provides action plans"—Foucault's concept of "discipline" also indirectly provides a way of understanding schools. The real difference lies in **the mode of influence**:
- **Guiding reality**: directly influencing policy through political power. Requires power, is "top-down."
- **Manufacturing reality**: indirectly influencing cognition through concept dissemination. Requires time, is "diffuse."

The former is the direct combination of philosophy and political power; the latter is philosophy's indirect infiltration through the academic system and knowledge networks. The former easily leads to disaster, because philosophy directly provides legitimacy for political action; the latter is safer, because the influence of concepts is gradual, dispersed, and uncontrollable.

#### 1.5 How the Abstract Game Manufactures Reality: Foucault's "Discipline" as Example

Core argument: the abstract game is not "escaping reality," but "manufacturing reality."

Reality is not given, but constructed. The way we understand the world depends on the concepts, languages, and frameworks we use.

**Foucault's concept of "discipline" is a typical case.**

In 1975, Foucault published *Discipline and Punish*. In this book, he proposed the concept of "discipline," describing a new power technology—one that does not use violence, but surveillance, examination, and normalization to produce "docile bodies." Foucault argued: schools, hospitals, prisons, barracks—these seemingly different institutions actually share the same disciplinary logic.

What was the path of this concept's dissemination?

**First, academic absorption.** Within ten years of Foucault's publication of *Discipline and Punish*, the concept of "discipline" was widely absorbed by sociology, education, criminology, management, and other disciplines. Sociologists used "discipline" to analyze factory management; educators used it to analyze classroom discipline; criminologists used it to analyze the prison system. By the 1990s, "discipline" had become a basic concept in these disciplines.

**Second, disciplinary reorganization.** The concept of "discipline" changed the research direction of these disciplines. Previously, education studied classroom management, criminology studied prison reform, sociology studied factory organization—they went their separate ways. The concept of "discipline" allowed researchers to discover: these seemingly different fields actually share the same power logic. This produced a batch of interdisciplinary research, such as "discipline and education," "discipline and medicine," "discipline and urban space."

**Third, institutional influence.** Although the concept of "discipline" did not directly lead to specific policy changes, it changed the framework of policy discussion. When people discuss prison reform, they no longer only ask "is the prison humane," but ask "how does the prison reproduce disciplinary logic." When people discuss education reform, they no longer only ask "is the school fair," but ask "how does the school produce docile bodies." This framework transformation is itself a kind of "manufacturing reality."

**Must distinguish "descriptive manufacturing" from "constructive manufacturing."** Foucault's concept of "discipline" is closer to "descriptive manufacturing"—he described a reality people had not previously been conscious of, and this description itself changed people's behavior. This is not "creating something out of nothing," but "naming is changing." When school administrators realize they are "disciplining" students, they may reflect on or even change their management methods. This reflection itself is the new reality manufactured by the concept of "discipline." The philosophical significance of this distinction is: most abstract games belong to "descriptive manufacturing" rather than "constructive manufacturing." They do not create new reality, but name existing reality, and through naming change people's understanding of reality. This is precisely why they are safe—they do not manufacture new power structures, only new frameworks of understanding. The abstract game is safer than grand narrative precisely because it does not construct new power structures, only provides new frameworks of understanding.

**Conclusion:** Foucault did not tell anyone "what to do." He did not provide action plans, did not design institutions, did not give historical direction. He merely manufactured a concept, and then this concept was absorbed, disseminated, and applied through the academic system, ultimately changing the way people understand the world. This is "manufacturing reality"—through manufacturing concepts, indirectly changing cognitive frameworks, thereby changing reality itself.

#### 1.6 Summary

The abstract game is a mode of existence, not a theoretical position. It is not escaping reality, nor guiding reality, but manufacturing reality. Philosophy should be exempt from guiding reality through grand narratives, and should instead manufacture reality through abstract language games.

## II. Causal Analysis—Why It Became This Way

### Chapter Four: Historical Trauma—How Philosophy Was Frightened Away from Reality

#### 4.1 The Arrogance of the Nineteenth Century: Philosophy Once Tried to Guide Everything

The ambition of grand systems: Hegelian "absolute spirit" subsuming everything. The entanglement of philosophy with power, nationalism, ideology.

#### 4.2 The Bloody Backlash of the Twentieth Century

Grand narratives became killing explosives. Philosophical systems provided legitimacy for totalitarianism. Philosophy paid the price for its own arrogance.

**Case One: Heidegger and Nazism.** Heidegger's philosophy of "Dasein" attempted to provide a new foundation for human existence. But when he tried to use this philosophy to guide reality, he joined the Nazi Party, put on the Nazi uniform, and bound the "authenticity" of "Dasein" to the "historical mission of the German people." The result: his philosophy became the philosophical decoration of Nazi ideology. **This is not philosophy betraying its essence, but philosophy exposing its essence—it never had the ability to "guide" reality; it was merely appropriated by reality at certain moments.**

**Case Two: Hegel and German Nationalism.** Hegel's "absolute spirit" attempted to subsume history, morality, and politics under a grand narrative. But he regarded the Prussian state as "the highest embodiment of divine will," and this judgment was used by right-wing nationalism, ultimately providing philosophical legitimacy for totalitarianism.

**Case Three: Schmitt and Fascist Jurisprudence.** Schmitt's "concept of the political" attempted to provide an ontological foundation for politics. But he defined the essence of politics as "distinguishing friend and enemy," and this theory was directly absorbed by Nazi jurisprudence, becoming a theoretical tool of fascist dictatorship.

**Case Four: Sartre and Stalinism.** Sartre's existentialism attempted to provide a philosophical foundation for human freedom. But during the Cold War, Sartre long defended the Soviet Union, regarding Stalinism as "historical necessity." The result: his "philosophy of freedom" became entangled with a totalitarian practice.

These four cases collectively expose a fact: philosophical systems themselves are profound, but once appropriated by power, they become garments of legitimacy. This is not philosophy betraying its essence, but philosophy exposing its essence—its concepts were never "neutral analytical tools"; they can be appropriated by power at any time as killing weapons.

#### 4.3 Positive Cases: Is Indirect Influence Possible?

However, we must directly respond to a challenge: are there cases where philosophy influenced reality without causing disaster?

**Case One: Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* and Public Policy.** Rawls's *A Theory of Justice*, published in 1971, had a profound impact on contemporary political philosophy. Its two principles of "justice as fairness"—the equal liberty principle and the difference principle—became important ethical foundations for evaluating public policy, especially in the areas of distributive justice and equality of opportunity. But note: Rawls's influence is mainly at the level of academic discourse, not direct policy-making. His theory provided a conceptual framework for policy discussion, but policy-makers rarely directly cite Rawls to justify specific policies. In other words, this is an **indirect, academic-level influence**, not direct "guidance."

**Case Two: Amartya Sen's Capability Approach and the Human Development Index.** Sen introduced philosophical concepts (freedom, capability, well-being) into development economics, and collaborated with Mahbub ul Haq to develop the Human Development Index (HDI), transcending the traditional GDP-centric view of development. This is a rare case of philosophical concepts directly influencing international policy evaluation indicators. But again, note: Sen's influence was possible precisely because his "philosophy" was presented in the form of economics papers—he influenced policy as an economist, not as a philosopher.

**Case Three: Arendt and the Manufacturing of the Concept of "Totalitarianism."** Arendt's *The Origins of Totalitarianism* and *The Human Condition* had a profound impact on political philosophy. But she herself did not directly "guide reality"—her influence was achieved through concept manufacturing ("totalitarianism," "the banality of evil," "the vita activa"). This is precisely what this paper calls "manufacturing reality"—changing the way people understand the world through manufacturing concepts.

**Revised Conclusion:** The historical record of philosophy guiding reality is not "occasionally making mistakes," but "catastrophic"—**when it directly intervenes in politics in the form of grand narratives**. Indirect, academic-level influence (such as Rawls, Sen, and Arendt) not only did not cause disaster, but produced positive effects. But such influence is limited, accidental, and often requires philosophers to cross over into other disciplines (such as economics, law) to achieve. Philosophy as "philosophy" itself, in its direct influence on real politics, has a catastrophic historical record.

#### 4.4 Post-Traumatic Stress Response

Analytic philosophy: retreat into language, refusal to talk about good and evil. Continental philosophy: turn to deconstruction, refusal of positive construction. Common feature: no longer touching reality, no longer guiding practice.

Adorno wrote in 1949, "To write poetry after Auschwitz is barbaric." This sentence itself became the landmark declaration of philosophy's retreat—philosophy declared the bankruptcy of its own expressive capacity in the face of extreme violence. Foucault in *Discipline and Punish* explicitly refused to propose "alternatives," believing that any positive construction might repeat the mistakes of power. Derrida in *Writing and Difference* similarly refused to give a "way out," doing only "deconstruction" and no "construction."

#### 4.5 The Warning of Bodily Cost

In the history of philosophy, there was a philosopher who threw his body against the void, attempting to find a cruel respite in the hypocritical world of morality. But he was ultimately consumed by this "lightness"—syphilis and madness. His madness became the warning monument of the entire history of philosophy. Later philosophers saw this end and collectively chose "safe abstraction" over "dangerous bodily experiment." They no longer touched the flesh of reality, but they still conducted equally dangerous thought experiments in their studies—only these experiments no longer had the risk of syphilis, nor the cost of madness. The cost became something else: complete detachment from reality.

#### 4.6 Transition: Trauma Is Philosophy's Perception of Its Own Failure

A key distinction must be made here: the "trauma" described in this chapter is **philosophy's perception of its own failure**, not objective failure. What philosophy encountered in the twentieth century was not the failure of "guiding reality"—because it never truly guided reality. What it encountered was **the shattering of the illusion that it had guided reality**.

Philosophy once thought itself important, once thought its concepts were shaping history. When it discovered that its concepts were merely appropriated by power, it felt trauma. But the premise of this trauma—that "philosophy once guided reality"—is itself an illusion. Chapter Eleven will argue: the shattering of this illusion is precisely philosophy's disenchantment.

#### 4.7 Summary: Philosophy Was Frightened by Reality; Retreat Is Post-Traumatic Self-Protection

### Chapter Five: Institutional Pressure—Abstraction as Survival Strategy

#### 5.1 The Survival Logic of Academic Capitalism

Universities no longer retain disciplines based on "intellectual value," but on output indicators—paper impact factor, international ranking, funding acquisition capacity.

Harvard University's Faculty of Arts and Sciences, facing a budget gap of approximately $350 million, announced a 60% reduction in doctoral admissions for the arts and humanities, and 50% to 70% for the social sciences (American Academy of Arts and Sciences 2024 report). Specifically for the philosophy department, according to a December 2025 report in *The Harvard Crimson*, the department will be allowed to admit only 4 graduate students over the next two years, whereas it previously admitted 12 per year. Philosophy professor Edward J. Hall described the situation as "severe and desperate."

Indiana's public higher education institutions plan to cancel, suspend, or merge more than 400 degree programs, approximately 20% of the total degree programs in the state's public universities (Indiana Commission for Higher Education, 2026). This cutting stems from a 2025 state law setting "minimum graduation numbers" thresholds: associate's degrees must average 10 graduates per year, bachelor's 15, master's 7, doctoral or specialist degrees 3, or they face termination. The programs cut include philosophy, history, anthropology, sociology, political science, English, fine arts, religious studies, and many other humanities programs at six public universities including Indiana University Bloomington and Purdue University. Specific case: the philosophy undergraduate program at Indiana State University had an average of only 1 enrollee over three years and was directly terminated.

Kennesaw State University's philosophy program averaged only 6.3 graduates over three years and was therefore terminated. The University of Toledo, Kennesaw State University, and others have directly eliminated their philosophy programs.

But what these data reveal is not "philosophy is useless," but the selective elimination of the system. The key point: universities do not retain disciplines based on "intellectual value," but on output indicators. Abstract papers can boost international rankings, bring citation rates, and help universities rise in QS rankings, so hardcore philosophers are retained. Ethics, although "useful" (can write policy reports, can do ethics training), cannot produce high-impact-factor papers, so its position is awkward.

#### 5.2 Abstraction as "Academic Currency"

The "abstract labyrinths" that hardcore philosophers produce in their studies—"différance," "rhizome," "transcendental apperception," "micro-physics of power"—are completely incomprehensible to the general public and completely unusable by the system. But these abstract papers can boost international rankings, bring citation rates, and help universities rise in QS rankings. They are like the "hard currency" of academia—having no practical value themselves, but being the most valuable in the indicator system of academic capitalism.

University management treats analytic and continental philosophy as "paper mining machines." The more abstract, obscure, and inhuman these researchers are, the more valuable the "paper coins" they produce. Universities use these "paper coins" to exchange with the state for funding, with society for reputation, and with the international ranking system for status. As for what those papers actually say? Management doesn't read them at all; they only look at impact factors and citation rates.

#### 5.3 The Awkwardness of Ethics: Cannot Produce "Abstract Papers"

Ethics plays an extremely inglorious yet indispensable role in contemporary philosophy departments. It is mocked by hardcore philosophers as "vulgar," yet pushed to the front by university management to serve as the "security guard" proving to the government and society that "philosophy still has use."

Ethicists know that the transcendental foundation of morality has been completely deconstructed, yet they must still preach "justice," "rights," and "responsibility" from the podium. This is a cognitive rift. He lives in contradiction every day: in papers, he secretly cites those thinkers who deconstruct morality; on the podium, he must play the moral believer.

More cruelly, ethics is caught in the middle in the academic system: facing the government, it must pretend that philosophy still has real-world concern; facing hardcore philosophers, it must endure the mockery of "you're still talking about morality?" It is like a cable car suspended in mid-air on a cliff, with abstract researchers above and secular reality below, swaying in the wind.

Ethics cannot escape this position because it still retains a sense of responsibility to reality in its bones—it cannot retreat into logic like analytic philosophy, nor sink completely into deconstruction like continental philosophy. It is precisely this sense of responsibility that makes ethics the only branch in the philosophy department that still resembles a "human." But it is also precisely this sense of responsibility that makes it the most "worthless" in the indicator system of academic capitalism.

#### 5.4 The Shrewd Calculations of University Management

University management certainly knows what kind of researchers they are keeping. But they need exactly this kind of person. Because in this utilitarian society, "inscrutable abstraction" is an expensive luxury, a hard currency for universities to exchange for funding and rankings.

Management is like a zoo director. He clearly knows that the animals in the cages are not human. He knows that analytic philosophers are pangolins gnawing on logical wood, and continental philosophers are ouroboros eating their own tails. But the zoo retains these monsters because society needs to see "rare animals."

When government officials visit, the director takes them to the philosophy building: "Look, we have people studying 'the thing-in-itself,' deconstructing 'the micro-physics of power,' how advanced!" The researchers think they are resisting the system, but they are actually just exhibits in the zoo, responsible for attracting tickets for the system.

#### 5.5 Transition: Academic Capitalism and Labor Alienation

The survival logic of academic capitalism is essentially a logic of alienated labor. University management measures the philosophy department by "graduation numbers," just as capitalists measure workers by working hours—"usefulness" becomes the currency of academia, just as wages become the currency of the factory.

The commodification of academic labor is similar to the commodification of factory labor: both strip the worker's product from the worker, turning it into an exchangeable commodity. But academic alienation is more hidden than factory alienation: workers know they are exploited; scholars think they are "free." Workers know they are selling their labor power; scholars think they are "pursuing truth." Workers know they are alienated; scholars think they are "thinking." This hiddenness makes academic alienation harder to identify and harder to resist than factory alienation.

This alienated logic is not an isolated phenomenon, but the continuation and amplification of the unresolved problem of labor alienation in the academic field. Chapter Six will elaborate on this judgment.

#### 5.6 Summary: The System Forces Philosophy Toward Abstraction; Abstraction Becomes a Survival Strategy

### Chapter Six: Capitalism's Unfinished Problem—Labor Alienation Was Never Solved

#### 6.1 Marx's Theory of Labor Alienation: A Problem Declared "Solved"

In the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*, Marx proposed four dimensions of labor alienation:

**First dimension: alienation of the worker from the product of labor.** The product the worker produces does not belong to the worker; it belongs to the capitalist. The more the worker produces, the poorer he himself becomes. The product becomes an alien, hostile force.

**Second dimension: alienation of the worker from the activity of labor.** Labor is not voluntary, but forced. Labor is not the satisfaction of a need, but a means to satisfy needs. In labor, the worker does not affirm himself, but denies himself.

**Third dimension: alienation of the worker from species-being.** Man's species-being is free conscious activity. But under capitalist conditions, labor becomes a means of survival. Man loses his essence as a human and becomes an animal.

**Fourth dimension: alienation of the worker from himself.** Man is alienated from man. Each person treats himself and others as tools. Ultimately, man is alienated from himself.

Marx's solution was communism: abolish private property, let labor return to its essence. But this solution was never realized in reality—or rather, it became another form of alienation in twentieth-century practice.

#### 6.2 Capitalism's Arrogance: Claiming the Problem of Alienation Is "Solved"

Capitalism claims to have "solved" the problem of alienation through material abundance. Workers have cars, houses, phones, consumer freedom. But this is a false solution: alienation has shifted from the "sphere of production" to the "sphere of consumption," spreading from the "working class" to "everyone."

#### 6.3 Amplification of Alienation: From Factory to Academia

The hardcore philosopher's abstract game is essentially a form of alienated labor.

**Commodification of academic labor:** Papers are commodities, impact factors are prices, citation rates are profits, academic publishers are capitalists.

**Alienation of scholars from the product of labor:** After publication, papers no longer belong to the scholar. Papers become a number in a database. Scholars must pay to read their own papers. Papers are cited, but scholars receive no economic return.

**Alienation of scholars from the labor process:** Research is no longer an intellectual adventure. Research becomes "paper production." "Publish or perish." Scholars are forced to produce "publishable" papers, not "meaningful" papers.

**Alienation of scholars from species-being:** The species-being of a thinker is free thought. But under academic capitalism, thinking becomes "output." Scholars are separated from thought itself. Scholars become "paper workers."

**Alienation of scholars from themselves:** The "I" in the study is an alienated "I" employed by academic capitalism. Scholars are alienated from themselves. Scholars are alienated from peers (competitive relationships). Scholars are alienated from students (consumer relationships).

#### 6.4 The Specificity of Academic Alienation

**Similarities between academic alienation and factory alienation:** Both involve the separation of the worker from the product of labor, from the labor process, and from himself.

**Differences between academic alienation and factory alienation:** Different object (manual labor vs. mental labor), different product (material commodity vs. symbolic commodity), different price (market price vs. impact factor), different consequence (worker poverty vs. scholar detachment).

**The hiddenness of academic alienation:** Workers know they are exploited; scholars think they are "free." Workers know they are selling their labor power; scholars think they are "pursuing truth." Workers know they are alienated; scholars think they are "thinking." This hiddenness makes academic alienation harder to identify and harder to resist than factory alienation.

#### 6.5 From Academic Alienation to Pragmatism

**Logical chain:** Capitalism turns all value into exchange value. Exchange value requires a universal equivalent (money). The logic of money penetrates the academic field, becoming "paper impact factor" and "graduation numbers." Academic managers internalize the logic of money, forming the "useful/useless" evaluation standard. This evaluation standard is naturalized, objectified, and becomes "self-evident" common sense. Pragmatic arrogance is the ideological expression of this naturalized money logic in the academic field.

**Specific manifestations:** University management measures the philosophy department by "graduation numbers," just as capitalists measure workers by working hours. "Usefulness" becomes the currency of academia, just as wages become the currency of the factory. Ethics is forced to serve as "security guard," just as workers are forced to serve as part of the machine. Hardcore philosophers produce "paper coins," just as workers produce commodities.

#### 6.6 Summary: Capitalism never solved labor alienation; it only amplified it. Academia is the latest theater of amplified alienation. Pragmatic arrogance is the ideological expression of alienated labor.

## III. Endpoint Evaluation—Is the Abstract Game a Problem?

### Chapter Seven: Why the Abstract Game Is an Acceptable Endpoint

#### 7.1 What Is "Guiding Reality"

Not "talking about reality," not "criticizing reality," not "inspiring reality." Rather: providing action plans (telling you what to do), providing institutional design (telling you what kind of society to build), providing historical direction (telling you where history should go), providing ultimate goals (telling you what future to pursue).

"Guiding reality" is an extremely strong requirement. It requires philosophy not only to say "the world is wrong," but also to say "how the world should be changed."

#### 7.2 The Historical Record of Philosophy Guiding Reality Is Catastrophic

**Case One: Heidegger and Nazism.** Heidegger's philosophy of "Dasein" attempted to provide a new foundation for human existence. But when he tried to use this philosophy to guide reality, he joined the Nazi Party, put on the Nazi uniform, and bound the "authenticity" of "Dasein" to the "historical mission of the German people." The result: his philosophy became the philosophical decoration of Nazi ideology.

**Case Two: Hegel and German Nationalism.** Hegel's "absolute spirit" attempted to subsume history, morality, and politics under a grand narrative. But he regarded the Prussian state as "the highest embodiment of divine will," and this judgment was used by right-wing nationalism, ultimately providing philosophical legitimacy for totalitarianism.

**Case Three: Schmitt and Fascist Jurisprudence.** Schmitt's "concept of the political" attempted to provide an ontological foundation for politics. But he defined the essence of politics as "distinguishing friend and enemy," and this theory was directly absorbed by Nazi jurisprudence, becoming a theoretical tool of fascist dictatorship.

**Case Four: Sartre and Stalinism.** Sartre's existentialism attempted to provide a philosophical foundation for human freedom. But during the Cold War, Sartre long defended the Soviet Union, regarding Stalinism as "historical necessity." The result: his "philosophy of freedom" became entangled with a totalitarian practice.

**Conclusion:** The historical record of twentieth-century philosophy guiding reality is not "occasionally making mistakes," but "catastrophic"—when it directly intervenes in politics in the form of grand narratives.

#### 7.3 Response to Four Objections

**Objection One: If philosophy does not guide reality, what is philosophy for?**

Response: Philosophy can criticize reality, but criticism is not guidance. Criticism reveals problems; guidance provides solutions. Philosophy should only do the former. Philosophy can expose "this system has problems," but does not need to say "what system should be established." Exposure itself is value.

**Objection Two: If philosophy does not intervene in reality, isn't it shirking responsibility?**

Response: The cost of intervention is disaster; the cost of non-intervention is "uselessness." Compared to each other, "uselessness" is more acceptable. When intervention means possibly providing theoretical ammunition for tyranny, "uselessness" is not escape, but a kind of responsibility—to oneself, to thought, to those who might be harmed.

**Objection Three: If philosophy does not guide reality, can it at least inspire reality?**

Response: Inspiration is indirect, accidental, uncontrollable. Philosophy does not pursue inspiration, only clarity. Inspiration is a byproduct, not a goal. If philosophy tries to pursue inspiration, it will begin to cater to the masses, simplify thought, and manufacture slogans—precisely what it should avoid.

**Objection Four: If philosophy does not guide reality, what is its value?**

Response: Its value is itself. Thought itself is a value, and does not need to be proven by "usefulness." Just as a poem does not need to prove its "usefulness," just as music does not need to prove its "usefulness," philosophy does not need to either. It is part of human cultural achievement, not a tool.

#### 7.4 The Middle Path: Is the Public Intellectual Possible?

Between "grand narrative guidance" and "abstract game manufacturing," there is a middle path: the public intellectual.

Public intellectuals (such as Sartre, Foucault, Chomsky) attempt to use philosophy to intervene in public discussion. They use **public reason**—using language the public can understand to discuss public issues. They neither provide "absolute spirit"-style grand plans, nor retreat completely into the obscure terminology labyrinth.

But this path also faces difficulties:
- Simplified by the media: complex philosophical arguments compressed into slogans
- Appropriated by politics: public intellectuals' statements selectively cited
- Misunderstood by the public: philosophical concepts lose precision in dissemination

Therefore, under current conditions, the "public reason" path also faces risks. The abstract game remains the safest choice—not because it is the best, but because it is the least likely to be abused.

#### 7.5 Summary: The Abstract Game Is Not Failure; It Is the Safe Position Philosophy Found After Paying a Painful Price

## V. The Real Problem—Pragmatic Arrogance

### Chapter Nine: Pragmatic Arrogance—Concept and Application

#### 9.1 Distinguishing "Pragmatism" from "Pragmatic Arrogance"

**Philosophical pragmatism** (Pragmatism): The philosophical tradition of Dewey, James, Peirce. Core claim: the meaning of an idea lies in its practical effects; truth is "useful." It is a theoretical position with a complete argumentative system. It does not necessarily lead to cutting philosophy departments.

**Pragmatic arrogance** (Pragmatic Arrogance): Not a philosophical theory, but a power posture. Core claim: I have the right to judge what is "useful" and what is "useless," and this judgment is objective and self-evident. It is an ideology that does not need argument, only execution. It directly leads to cutting philosophy departments.

Key distinction: philosophical pragmatism says "truth is useful"; pragmatic arrogance says "what is useless should die." The former is theory; the latter is power.

#### 9.2 Definition of Pragmatic Arrogance

Pragmatic arrogance is a power posture that assumes the right to judge the value of all disciplines by a single economic efficiency standard, and naturalizes this judgment as "objective law," thereby providing legitimacy for cutting "useless" disciplines.

This definition contains four elements: power posture (not a theoretical position, but a power operation), single standard (economic efficiency, graduation numbers, funding output), naturalization (calling one's own judgment "objective law"), and legitimacy function (providing reasons for cutting).

#### 9.3 The Four Levels of Pragmatic Arrogance

**Structural arrogance:** Capitalism turns all value into exchange value. This is the most fundamental level. It is not a subject, but a structure. It is not someone's "arrogance," but the entire system operating.

**Institutional arrogance:** State laws and university policies operationalize this logic. For example, Indiana sets a "minimum graduation numbers" threshold. This is the manifestation of structural arrogance at the institutional level.

**Individual arrogance:** The specific decisions of management and politicians. They choose to cut philosophy departments, choose to allocate resources to areas that can produce "returns." This is arrogance at the individual level.

**Social basis:** The choices of students and parents. When everyone measures majors by "employment prospects," pragmatic arrogance has legitimacy. But this level needs more careful handling—the choices of students and parents are constrained by structure and are not entirely "arrogance." They are making rational choices in a system that directly links tuition and employment prospects.

**Conclusion:** Pragmatic arrogance is not the attitude of a single subject, but a structural phenomenon distributed across four levels. Criticism of "structural arrogance" cannot be simply transformed into moral condemnation of "individual arrogance."

#### 9.4 The Three-Layer Distinction of "Usefulness"

The problem with pragmatic arrogance is that it narrows "usefulness" to a single standard. Three different kinds of "usefulness" need to be distinguished:

- **Instrumental usefulness:** Can produce direct economic benefits. Such as computer science helping companies make money, engineering producing products.
- **Cognitive usefulness:** Can help understand the world. Such as philosophy clarifying concepts, training critical thinking, providing analytical frameworks.
- **Existential usefulness:** Can make people live more lucidly. Such as philosophy helping people face the void, question meaning, resist self-deception.

The problem with pragmatic arrogance is not that it pursues "usefulness," but that it **narrows "usefulness" to the first kind**, and thereby denies the value of the other two kinds of "usefulness." When Indiana measures the philosophy department by "graduation numbers," it is using precisely this narrowed standard of usefulness.

**The position of the unemployment rate data in the argument:** Federal Reserve Bank of New York 2024 data shows that the unemployment rate for undergraduate philosophy majors is 5.1%, while for computer science majors it is 7%. This data can only serve as an auxiliary counterexample, proving that the assumption "philosophy is useless" is "at least not obviously true." It cannot prove that philosophy is "more useful" than computer science, nor can it constitute the core argument against pragmatic arrogance. The core argument should be placed on the three-layer distinction of "usefulness": even if philosophy is "useless" in economic indicators, it is still useful at the cognitive and existential levels.

#### 9.5 The Essence of University Cuts to Philosophy Departments

Cutting philosophy departments on the grounds of "uselessness." Measuring all disciplines by economic efficiency.

Harvard University's Faculty of Arts and Sciences, facing a budget gap of approximately $350 million, announced a 60% reduction in doctoral admissions for the arts and humanities, and 50% to 70% for the social sciences (American Academy of Arts and Sciences 2024 report). Specifically for the philosophy department, according to a December 2025 report in *The Harvard Crimson*, the department will be allowed to admit only 4 graduate students over the next two years, whereas it previously admitted 12 per year.

Indiana's public higher education institutions plan to cancel, suspend, or merge more than 400 degree programs (Indiana Commission for Higher Education, 2026). Indiana law sets "minimum graduation numbers" thresholds: undergraduate programs must average 15 graduates per year, master's 7, doctoral 3, or they face termination.

Kennesaw State University's philosophy program averaged only 6.3 graduates over three years and was therefore terminated. The University of Toledo, Kennesaw State University, and others have directly eliminated their philosophy programs.

#### 9.6 Who Is Cutting Philosophy Departments?—Subject Analysis

**State legislators:** Set "minimum graduation numbers" thresholds. This is the operationalization of pragmatic arrogance: it uses "graduation numbers" as the single standard to judge the value of all disciplines.

**University management:** Allocate budgets, cut "low-output" disciplines. Harvard's endowment exceeds $50 billion, but the philosophy department's doctoral admissions dropped from 12 to 4. This is not "no money," but "money flowing to areas that can produce returns."

**Students and parents:** Vote with their feet, choose "useful" majors. This is not pragmatic arrogance itself, but it is the social basis of pragmatic arrogance.

**The general logic of capitalist production relations:** Turn everything into exchange value. This is the ultimate root of pragmatic arrogance.

#### 9.7 The Comparative Perspective of Pragmatic Arrogance

Pragmatic arrogance is not the patent of the Anglo-American academic system. It has different manifestations in different contexts.

**American context:** Centered on quantitative indicators. University rankings, paper impact factors, graduate employment rates constitute the "trinity" of academic capitalism. The philosophy department is judged as "low-output" in this system and is therefore compressed. Notably, pragmatic arrogance in the American academic system often appears under the name of "market choice"—it does not say "we want to cut the philosophy department," but says "students are not choosing philosophy majors."

**European context:** Centered on fiscal austerity. Philosophy departments in European universities face not "ranking pressure," but "budget cuts." Governments, citing deficit reduction, compress funding for the humanities. Philosophy departments are forced to prove their "social relevance" or face closure. Pragmatic arrogance in the European context is closer to "administrative arrogance"—it is a government decision, not a market choice.

**Chinese context:** Centered on the dual logic of politics and economics. Chinese universities also face "publish or perish" pressure, but unlike the United States, China's quantitative assessment is more directly linked to titles, salaries, and housing. At the same time, Chinese philosophy departments face a special problem: Marxist philosophy occupies a dominant position, and analytic and continental philosophy are confined to a specific range. They can exist as "academic research," but cannot touch top-level design questions like "where should China go." This configuration saves Chinese philosophy departments from the Western "three-way split of analytic/continental/ethics," but also makes the philosophy department as a whole more dependent on the political system. Pragmatic arrogance in the Chinese context is more closely tied to the political system; Marxist philosophy is both the "meta-structure" and the "protective umbrella."

**Comparative framework:** The pragmatic arrogance in the three contexts can be explained by the "state-market-academy" triangle. The United States is "market-led"—market logic penetrates the academic field, manifested as "student choice" and "ranking pressure." Europe is "state-led"—state fiscal logic dominates the academic field, manifested as "budget cuts" and "social relevance." China is "state-market hybrid"—state political logic and market logic jointly dominate the academic field, manifested as "quantitative assessment" and "political dependence." The common point of the three forms: narrowing "usefulness" to economic efficiency, measuring disciplinary value by quantitative indicators. The difference: the United States leans toward "market arrogance," Europe toward "administrative arrogance," China toward "political-economic dual arrogance."

#### 9.8 The Danger of Pragmatic Arrogance

When all disciplines are forced to be "useful," thought dies. When the university becomes a vocational training center, it loses the meaning of "university." This arrogance itself is a breeding ground for totalitarian thinking: only one standard is allowed to exist. When pragmatists think they have the right to judge all value, they are becoming another form of totalitarianism.

#### 9.9 Summary: The Real Problem Is Not in Philosophy, But in the Pragmatic Arrogance That Cannot Tolerate "Uselessness"

## VI. Conclusion

### Chapter Ten: The Endpoint Picture

#### 10.1 The Daily Picture Within Academia

The analytic philosopher's daily life centers on paper production. They spend years revising a paper, responding to anonymous reviewers' comments, repeatedly refining technical details. These works have no direct connection to any real problem, but they must do this because publication is the only way to obtain tenure. Their work is not "thinking," but "producing."

The continental philosopher's daily life centers on text deconstruction. They spend years writing a paper about a thinker, repeatedly deducing in the labyrinth of terminology. These works have no direct connection to any public discussion, but they must do this because publication is the only way to obtain academic reputation. Their work is not "thinking," but "performing thinking."

The ethicist's daily life centers on policy consulting. They spend a great deal of time writing "ethics guidelines" and "policy recommendations," handing these documents to the government or corporations. These works have no direct connection to any moral philosophy, but they must do this because it is the only way for the philosophy department to prove "usefulness." Their work is not "thinking," but "translating."

**Conclusion:** The daily lives of the three branches share a common structure—they are all producing some kind of "product" (papers, reports, books), but none of them is sure what these products are actually for. They are not "philosophers," but "academic workers."

#### 10.2 The Structural Picture at the Institutional Level

The structure of the university: The university is a giant academic capitalist machine. Its core goals are: boost rankings, obtain funding, enhance international reputation. To achieve these goals, it needs: paper miners (analytic philosophers, continental philosophers) to produce high-impact-factor papers and help the university rise in QS rankings; security guards (ethicists) to write policy reports, provide ethics training, and prove to the government and society that "philosophy still has use"; decorations (a few star professors) to win awards, appear in the media, and add luster to the university; marginal people (most young scholars) who cannot get tenure, wander between different schools, and become "academic gig workers." "Academic gig workers" refers to those young scholars in the academic system who lack stable positions, lack welfare protection, and lack academic freedom—they move between institutions as adjunct professors, postdocs, visiting scholars, undertake large teaching loads, but have almost no research resources.

The structure of funding: Funding flows to areas that can produce "academic currency." Abstract papers can boost citation rates, so hardcore philosophers are retained. Ethics, although "useful," cannot produce high-impact-factor papers, so its position is awkward. The philosophy department as a whole is compressed because its "output" is insufficient.

The structure of evaluation: The evaluation standard is single: number of papers, impact factor, citation rate. Different disciplines are evaluated by the same standard. The philosophy department and the computer science department use the same indicators. The philosophy department's "graduation numbers" are compared with the computer science department's, and then judged as "low-output."

#### 10.3 The Psychological Picture at the Level of Experience

The analytic philosopher's psychology: He has a profound anxiety. He knows no one reads his papers, he knows he is just producing "paper coins," he knows he has no relationship with "thought" anymore. But he cannot say it. He must pretend to be "thinking," must pretend his work is "meaningful." His anxiety is transformed into a refined cynicism—he mocks everything, including himself.

The continental philosopher's psychology: He has a profound narcissism. He believes his obscurity has depth, he believes his terminology is the mark of "thought," he believes he is a genius "misunderstood" by society. But beneath his narcissism is a profound emptiness—he does not know what these things he writes are actually for, nor whether he is really "thinking" or "performing thinking."

The ethicist's psychology: He has a profound rift. He knows what he writes is "political massage," he knows he is "taking bullets" for the entire discipline, he knows he is betraying his own sincerity. But he cannot say it. He must pretend that "morality still exists," must pretend that "philosophy still has use." His rift is transformed into a lucid self-masochism—he knows he is cannon fodder, but he has no choice.

**Conclusion:** The psychological picture at the level of experience is three kinds of anxiety: cynicism, narcissism, rift. No one is satisfied, no one is truly happy. But no one can leave.

#### 10.4 The Picture of the Relationship with the World

Philosophy and science: Scientists and engineers define physical reality. Philosophy cannot make a screw, nor calculate capital flows. The scientific community does not need philosophy's "guidance," and the philosophical community no longer attempts to "guide" science. The two operate separately, without interfering with each other.

Philosophy and politics: Politicians define power consensus. Philosophy neither participates in politics nor attempts to influence politics. Politicians do not need philosophy's "legitimacy endorsement," and the philosophical community no longer attempts to "guide" politics. The two operate separately, without interfering with each other.

Philosophy and capital: Capital defines exchange value. Philosophy neither participates in economic activity nor attempts to influence the economy. Capital does not need philosophy's "critique," and the philosophical community no longer attempts to "critique" capital. The two operate separately, without interfering with each other.

Philosophy and the public: The public does not read philosophy papers, does not care about philosophical questions, does not know what the philosophy department is doing. The relationship between philosophy and the public is: the philosophy department occasionally issues some "ethics guidelines" to the public through ethics. The public takes a look, then continues their own lives.

#### 10.5 Why This Endpoint Is Inevitable

Historical trauma cannot be erased. Institutional pressure cannot be eliminated. Disciplinary differentiation cannot be reversed. Capitalist alienation cannot be solved.

#### 10.6 Why This Endpoint Does Not Need to Be Changed

The risk of change outweighs the benefit. The possibility of change is extremely low. This endpoint is already acceptable.

### Chapter Eleven: The Meaning of the Diagnosis

This paper begins with a question: why has philosophy become what it is today?

After layers of deduction—historical trauma, institutional pressure, capitalist alienation, phenomenological samples, pragmatic critique—this paper's conclusion is diagnostic: **philosophy's aphasia toward reality is not due to intellectual deficiency, but to the collapse of nineteenth-century grand narratives, twentieth-century historical trauma, and contemporary academic capitalism.**

#### 11.1 The Value of the Abstract Game

What does this diagnosis mean? It means: **the abstract game itself contains value worth affirming, and this value lies precisely in its "non-intervention."**

The value of the abstract game lies not in what it "does," but in what it "refuses"—it refuses to provide legitimacy for power, refuses to provide economic benefits for the market, refuses to provide cheap meaning for the masses. This refusal itself is a value. It is not "uselessness," but "non-participation in the hypocritical game of usefulness."

**The value of safety and purity:** When philosophy no longer attempts to guide reality, it no longer provides a garment of legitimacy for any political power. The abstract game cuts off the direct channel between philosophy and power. This cutting itself is an achievement—not a negative "uselessness," but a positive "immunity." At the same time, when philosophy no longer needs to serve any real goal, it can freely pursue those "useless" questions—the meaning of existence, the boundaries of language, the nature of consciousness. These questions do not produce economic benefits, but they constitute the core of human self-understanding. The abstract game has returned philosophy to its oldest, purest mission: not to change the world, but to understand it.

**The value of concept creation:** The abstract game does not produce nothing. It produces concepts—Foucault's "discipline," Derrida's "différance," Deleuze's "rhizome," Arendt's "totalitarianism." These concepts do not provide action plans, but they change the way people understand the world. When sociologists use "discipline" to analyze schools, when political scientists use "totalitarianism" to analyze regimes, when cultural researchers use "différance" to analyze texts—these concepts are already "manufacturing reality." This is an indirect, long-term, uncontrollable influence, but it is a real influence.

**The value of resistance and honest exchange:** When the entire society measures everything by "usefulness," philosophy chooses to retreat into the abstract game, chooses "uselessness," chooses not to participate in that hypocritical exchange. This refusal is not escape, but a silent protest. The loyally exchanging prostitute reminds us: honesty in exchange is a virtue—she does not lie, does not pretend, does not pretend to be superior to anyone. Philosophy maintains this honesty by refusing self-beautification. When hardcore philosophers admit they do not know what these abstract concepts are for, when ethicists admit they are serving as "security guards," when the entire discipline admits it has retreated into the abstract game—this admission itself is a kind of honesty.

#### 11.2 The Meaning of the Diagnosis

What else does this diagnosis mean?

**First, it means philosophy does not need to feel ashamed of its own "uselessness," and criticism of pragmatic arrogance cannot remain at the level of moral condemnation.** Uselessness is not failure, but safety. Under historical conditions where philosophy guiding reality often leads to disaster, uselessness is a form of self-protection. Pragmatic arrogance is not someone's "badness," but the entire system operating. When a student in rural Indiana chooses computer science over philosophy, he is not being "arrogant"—he is making a rational choice in a system that directly links tuition and employment prospects. To change pragmatic arrogance, we need to change the system, not the individual.

**Second, it means the ethicist's awkwardness is not personal failure, but structural, and philosophy's retreat into the abstract game is not an end, but a transformation.** The ethicist is forced to pretend morality still exists in a system that does not recognize the foundation of morality. This is not his fault, but the system's fault. At the same time, philosophy has shifted from "guiding reality" to "manufacturing reality," from "grand narrative" to "concept production," from "public intellectual" to "academic worker." This transformation has costs—philosophy has lost public influence, lost the ability to intervene in reality, lost moral authority—but it also has gains. It has gained safety, gained purity, gained the possibility of free creation in the conceptual space.

**Third, it means Marx's problem of labor alienation was never solved, only amplified, and philosophy does not need to be saved; what needs to be saved is the world that cannot tolerate "uselessness."** The fourfold alienation described by Marx in the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844* was never solved by capitalism. It was only displaced: from the sphere of production to the sphere of consumption, from the working class to everyone, from the factory to academia. Pragmatic arrogance is the ideological expression of this naturalized money logic in the academic field. When pragmatists think they have the right to judge all value, they are becoming another form of totalitarianism. When all disciplines are forced to be "useful," thought dies. Philosophy does not need to feel ashamed of its own "uselessness"; what should feel ashamed is the world that cannot tolerate "uselessness."

#### 11.3 Disenchantment: Philosophy Is Not Unable to Intervene in Reality, But Afraid to Intervene

**But the conclusion does not end there. This paper ultimately argues: philosophy's retreat into the abstract game is not philosophy's failure, but philosophy's disenchantment.**

The term "disenchantment" is borrowed from Weber, originally referring to modernity's removal of the world's mystery. This paper transforms the concept: it is not the world that is disenchanted, but philosophy's illusion about itself that is removed.

Since its birth, philosophy has had a deep-rooted self-understanding: it is "the love of wisdom," "the pursuit of truth," "the essence of the spirit of the age," "the sharp weapon of social critique." This self-understanding runs through the entire history of philosophy from Plato to Hegel to Sartre to Foucault.

But the argument of this paper exposes a more cruel truth: **philosophy has never been what it thought it was.**

**We must distinguish "the failure of guiding reality" from "the achievement of manufacturing reality."** Philosophy has never succeeded in "guiding reality"—Heidegger's "Dasein" did not guide the Nazis; it was merely appropriated by them. Hegel's "absolute spirit" did not guide Prussia; it was merely used by Prussia. Schmitt's "concept of the political" did not guide fascism; it was merely absorbed by fascism. Sartre's "philosophy of freedom" did not guide the Soviet Union; it was merely tolerated by the Soviet Union. But philosophy has indeed achieved something in "manufacturing reality"—Foucault's "discipline" did not directly deconstruct power, but it changed the research direction of sociology, education, and criminology. Derrida's "différance" did not directly change politics, but it changed the basic terminology of literary theory and cultural studies. Arendt's "totalitarianism" did not directly stop totalitarianism, but it changed the basic framework of political philosophy and political discussion.

**Philosophy's true history is not the history of "guiding reality," but the history of "being appropriated by reality," and the history of "manufacturing reality."** It thought it was guiding the world, but it was merely used by the world as decoration, a garment of legitimacy, theoretical ammunition. When power needed it, it was placed on the altar; when power did not need it, it was swept into the trash. But the concepts it produced were absorbed, disseminated, and applied in the academic system, ultimately changing the way people understand the world.

**But this is not the most cruel truth. The most cruel truth is: philosophy is not evading what it is; it actively chose not to face what it is.**

It is abstract not because it seeks profundity. It is abstract because abstraction is the most respectable way of escaping reality. In concepts, it can be king. In reality, it cannot save a single concrete human life, cannot stop a single concrete tyranny. So it flees into concepts. Then, under the name of "pursuing truth," it beautifies this escape as a noble solitude.

**We must distinguish "unable to intervene" from "afraid to intervene."** Philosophy is not incapable of intervening in reality—it has conceptual tools, analytical frameworks, critical traditions. It is capable of intervening, but it chose not to. Why? Because it knows that once it truly tries to intervene, it will expose its own incapacity. It knows that once it truly faces that living, mortal, concrete world, it is nothing. So it chose abstraction. It chose safety. It chose to be a general who never goes to the battlefield, a doctor who never treats a patient, a saint who never saves anyone.

**Philosophy is not history's victim. Philosophy is history's deserter.**

There is a historical moment that illustrates this. In 1933, Heidegger joined the Nazi Party and became rector of the University of Freiburg. His colleague Karl Jaspers—at the time one of Heidegger's closest friends—refused to cooperate with the Nazis. Jaspers was not incapable of intervening; he chose to intervene. He paid the price: he was dismissed from his teaching position, banned from publishing, and his wife faced persecution because she was Jewish. Heidegger chose not to intervene—no, he chose to actively serve. He bound his philosophy to Nazi ideology, using the "authenticity" of "Dasein" to provide philosophical decoration for the "historical mission of the German people." He was not an innocent victim appropriated by power; he was power's most perfect servant.

**Philosophy, at the moment humanity needed it most—facing totalitarianism, facing violence, facing the void—chose to turn away. It fled into language, fled into texts, fled into concepts. Then, with beautiful words like "lightness of being," "différance," "micro-physics of power," it packaged itself as a hero. But it is not a hero. It is just a person who fled into a library on the battlefield, and then wrote a book saying "war is fiction."**

**It is not an innocent victim appropriated by power; it is power's most perfect servant—because it pretends to resist, thereby making power appear resistible.**

This is philosophy's disenchantment: philosophy's retreat into the abstract game is not philosophy's failure, but philosophy finally exposing its truth all along. It never "intervened in reality." It just played a concept game in a small circle, and then, under the name of "pursuing truth," packaged this game as a noble cause. It pretends to resist power, thereby making power appear resistible. In this way, power is no longer absolute and unchallengeable. It has an "opponent"—philosophy. And this "opponent" will never truly threaten it, because philosophy only resists in concepts, never in reality. This is power's most perfect arrangement.

The abstract game has returned philosophy to where it always belonged: an intellectual game for a small circle, a concept-production workshop that does not resonate with power, an activity that honestly admits its own "uselessness." This is not failure. This is return. Philosophy does not need to be saved, because it was never "lost." It has simply finally admitted what it is—history's deserter, power's most perfect servant, an intellectual game for a small circle.

## Afterword

This paper's writing stems from observation of the living conditions of contemporary philosophy researchers and from persistent questioning of the relationship between philosophy and reality. It does not attempt to "save" philosophy, nor to "reform" the discipline. It does only one thing: diagnose—and then disenchant. Diagnose why philosophy has become what it is, and disenchant philosophy's misunderstanding of itself over two thousand years.

The methods used in this paper are mainly conceptual analysis and phenomenological description. Conceptual analysis is used to distinguish "pragmatism" from "pragmatic arrogance," "guiding reality" from "manufacturing reality," and "instrumental usefulness," "cognitive usefulness," and "existential usefulness." Phenomenological description is used to present the daily picture, institutional structure, and psychological experience of academia.

It must be noted: the "judicial" tone of this paper is itself a method. It is not doing "objective academic research," but "disenchantment." Disenchantment does not need caution, but sharpness. It needs to cut open philosophy's self-narrative with one stroke, exposing the concealed truth. Therefore, this paper does not pursue "balance," does not pursue "sympathetic understanding," does not pursue "academic norms." It pursues a single fatal blow.

The limitations of this paper are: first, it is mainly based on observation of the Anglo-American and continental European academic systems, without involving the academic ecology of other regions; second, the analysis of "pragmatic arrogance" mainly remains at the structural level, lacking micro-level research on specific decision-making processes; third, the defense of the "abstract game" is a functional argument, without involving evaluation of the content of the abstract game itself; fourth, the description of the prostitute group has an idealizing tendency—although internal differences have been acknowledged, the "loyal exchanger" is still used as the analytical model, and this comparison is heuristic rather than strictly argumentative.

The conclusion of this paper is: this is not a problem. Philosophy's exemption from the disaster of guiding reality is already sufficient gain. Philosophy does not need to be saved, because it was never "lost." It has simply finally admitted what it is—history's deserter, power's most perfect servant, an intellectual game for a small circle.
