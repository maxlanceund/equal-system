## Abstract

The *Limit Theory of Meaning* (Zhong, Shanzhen, 2026a) has established that meaning is a signal generated by finite systems within finite intervals. At the boundary, meaning zeroes out — but motion does not cease. However, zeroing is not always smooth. In some cases, signal density rises sharply before the boundary, and only then zeroes out. This paper terms this phenomenon "boundary acceleration" and demonstrates, through mathematical derivation and a geometric diagram, that it is a testable structure — not a cross-domain analogy. Boundary acceleration is a conditional consequence of "meaning zeroes out, but motion does not cease." The paper further shows that boundary acceleration requires a *validation signal* to trigger the release process, rather than occurring automatically. Appendix A reports numerical verification of this claim.

**Keywords**: boundary acceleration · signal density · zeroing · motion reorganization · finite interval · limit theory of meaning

## Literature Positioning and Scholarly Dialogue

This paper is a supplementary essay to the *Limit Theory of Meaning* (Zhong, Shanzhen, 2026a), focusing on the structural details of the zeroing process. The *Limit Theory of Meaning* argued that meaning is defined only within finite intervals, and that at the interval's boundary meaning zeroes out, but motion does not cease. This paper further asks: how does zeroing occur? Is it a smooth decline to zero, or is there a sharp release of signals just before the boundary?

In systems theory, Luhmann (1984, S. 35-45) defines the system's boundary as the point where communicative connectivity ceases — beyond the boundary, the system no longer operates. This paper's concept of "boundary" formally corresponds to Luhmann's system boundary: both refer to the termination point of system operations. However, Luhmann's theory focuses on describing how systems reproduce themselves through communication, and does not directly address how signal density changes as the system approaches its boundary. This paper's contribution lies in extending Luhmann's concept of system boundary by formally describing the dynamics of signal density before the boundary. The derivation in this paper does not depend on any specific assumptions of systems theory — it proceeds solely from the definition of signal density and the non-crossability of the boundary, and is therefore independent of Luhmann's framework.

This paper serves as a bridge within the Equal System series, connecting the *Limit Theory of Meaning* and the *Limited-Interval Principle*.

## Theoretical Framework and Methodological Statement

### Definition of Core Concepts

Before proceeding, it is necessary to strictly define the core concepts used in this paper.

**Finite interval**: a temporal or spatial range within which a system operates, with a clear starting point and endpoint. Beyond this interval, the system's signal-processing function is no longer defined.

**Signal density `D(t)`**: the amount of signal that the system generates or carries at time `t`. Signals can be information, energy, resources, attention, or any other quantifiable element that flows within the system.

**Total remaining signal `R(t)`**: the total signal the system has yet to release from the current moment to the boundary. Defined as:

`R(t) = ∫ₜᵀ D(s) ds`

**Boundary**: the endpoint `T` of the interval, where the system's signal processing terminates. At the boundary, signal density zeroes out: `D(T) = 0`.

**Boundary acceleration**: the phenomenon in which signal density rises sharply before the boundary and then zeroes out. Geometrically, it appears as a spike.

**Validation signal**: the internal judgment that triggers boundary acceleration — the moment when the system recognises that "the window has opened" or "conditions are mature." Before the validation signal appears, signal density remains low; after it appears, the system enters a high-density state.

### Methodological Statement: Formal Derivation and Structural Description

The methodological path adopted in this paper is a combination of **formal derivation** and **structural description**. Specifically, starting from the definition of signal density and the non-crossability of the interval boundary, this paper uses mathematical derivation to prove the conditional necessity of boundary acceleration, and uses a geometric diagram to visualise it. This paper does not rely on empirical cases to "prove" the existence of boundary acceleration — its conditional necessity has already been derived mathematically. Empirical cases are used only to demonstrate how this structure manifests in different domains. Appendix A reports a falsifiable test of the structure.

The types of evidence used in this paper include:

1. **Formal definitions**: strict mathematical definitions of signal density, remaining signal, and related concepts.
2. **Mathematical derivation**: deriving the conditional necessity of boundary acceleration from the definitions.
3. **Geometric diagram**: visualising the derivation results.
4. **Condition analysis**: specifying the conditions under which boundary acceleration occurs and the mechanism of its trigger.
5. **Falsifiability conditions**: proposing testable conditions under which the theory would be falsified.
6. **Numerical verification**: falsifiable testing of the core claim using a task-queue model (see Appendix A).

## III. Mathematical Derivation

Let `D(t)` be the signal density function, defined on the interval `[0, T]`.

Define the **total remaining signal** as:

`R(t) = ∫ₜᵀ D(s) ds`

That is: the total signal the system has yet to release from the current moment to the boundary.

The system's behavior is constrained by the following conditions:

1. Within the interval, `D(t) ≥ 0` (signal density cannot be negative).
2. At the boundary, `D(T) = 0` (signal zeroes out).
3. The system must release all signals before the boundary: `R(0) = ∫₀ᵀ D(s) ds < ∞`.

If the signal release rate is roughly constant (i.e., `D(t)` changes slowly), then signal density should gradually decrease to zero at the boundary.

However, if the system still holds unreleased signals near the boundary, and signals cannot be released after the boundary, they must be released before the boundary. This requires signal density to be higher than normal in a period before the boundary.

**Derivation:**

Let the signal release rate be `dD/dt`. Suppose the system holds unreleased signals near the boundary, i.e., `R(t₀) > 0` for some `t₀` close to `T`.

Since signals cannot be released after `T` (the interval has ended), `R(t₀)` must be released within a finite window of length `T - t₀`.

This means that within this window, `D(t)` must be higher than the system's average release capacity — otherwise the signal cannot be fully released before the boundary.

**Therefore: unreleased signal + non-crossable boundary + system recognizes the boundary ⇒ signal density rises before the boundary.**

This is the mathematical basis of boundary acceleration.

**Important qualification**: The final premise of the above derivation — "system recognizes the boundary" — is necessary. If the system has no mechanism for recognizing the boundary, it will not automatically accelerate because "signals cannot be released after the boundary"; instead, it will leave tasks unprocessed and stop. The task-queue experiment in Appendix A confirms this. Therefore, boundary acceleration is not unconditionally necessary, but **conditionally necessary**; the condition is that the system recognizes a validation signal (see Section 4.3).

## IV. Structural Properties

### 4.1 Conditionality

Boundary acceleration is not inevitable. It occurs only when:

1. The system still holds unreleased signals near the boundary: `R(t) > 0`.
2. Signals cannot be released after the boundary: `D(T) = 0`.
3. The release window is finite: `T - t < ∞`.
4. The system recognizes a validation signal (see Section 4.3).

If the system has already released all signals in advance (i.e., `R(t) = 0` is already satisfied before the boundary), the boundary may be smooth.

If the system still holds unreleased signals before the boundary but does **not** recognize a validation signal, the boundary may also be smooth — the system simply leaves tasks unprocessed and stops. The experiments in Appendix A confirm this case.

### 4.2 Conditions for Signal Density Increase

From the derivation above, it follows:

If `R(t₀) > 0` and `T - t₀` is sufficiently small, **and the system recognizes a validation signal**, then there exists a subinterval `[a, b] ⊂ [t₀, T]` such that `D(t)` is higher than `D(t₀)`.

Otherwise, the signal cannot be fully released before `T`.

**Therefore: boundary acceleration is the conditional necessity that arises when signals must be released within a finite interval and the system recognizes a validation signal.**

**Counter-case**: If the system does not recognize a validation signal, then even if `R(t₀) > 0`, signal density will not rise. The system will leave unreleased signals at the boundary and simply stop. This is not a counterexample to boundary acceleration, but a manifestation of its **conditionality**.

### 4.3 Trigger Condition: The Validation Signal

The derivation above shows that if a system still holds residual signals at the boundary, signal density will rise before the boundary. However, this theorem does not specify when the rise begins, nor what happens when no validation signal is present.

Observation and experiment show that boundary acceleration is not triggered by the boundary itself, but by the system recognising a **validation signal**.

A validation signal is an internal judgment that indicates:

1. "The window has opened" — the system has obtained the conditions to operate.
2. "The conditions are mature" — the system has confirmed its position relative to the target.

Before the validation signal appears, signal density remains low. After the validation signal appears, the system enters a high-density state and continues to run until it reaches its peak at the boundary, then zeroes out.

The complete structure of boundary acceleration is:

`Validation signal appears → acceleration initiates → sustained operation → boundary approaches → peak → zeroing`

This revision refines the "conditionality" of the theory: boundary acceleration requires not only that the system still holds residual signals at the boundary, but also a validation signal to trigger the release process.

**Corollary:** The appearance of a validation signal is a sufficient condition for acceleration to begin. If at any moment before the boundary the system recognises a validation signal, acceleration will start and continue until the boundary, where it zeroes out.

If the system never recognises a validation signal throughout the entire interval, no acceleration will occur. The boundary may be smooth. The experiments in Appendix A confirm this case.

## V. Possible Objections and Falsifiability Conditions

Any theoretical argument must be open to questioning of its core claims. This chapter outlines the main objections this paper may face, responds to each, and then proposes testable falsifiability conditions.

### 5.1 Responses to Possible Objections

**Objection 1: This derivation assumes signal density can rise arbitrarily, but in reality systems may have upper bounds.**

**Response:** The derivation does not require infinite density. It only requires that density rises above normal levels near the boundary. If a system has an upper bound, that upper bound only means acceleration will occur earlier or more intensely — it does not cancel acceleration itself.

**Objection 2: If the system has already released all signals before the boundary, boundary acceleration does not occur.**

**Response:** Correct. This is precisely the conditionality of boundary acceleration. It is not a universal law, but a conditional structure. Its presence or absence is itself an observable signal — a smooth boundary means signals were already released in advance.

**Objection 3: Is the concept of a "validation signal" too vague? Could anything trigger it?**

**Response:** The definition of a validation signal is formal — it is an internal judgment that indicates the system recognises that "the window has opened" or "the conditions are mature." In different systems, the specific content of this judgment varies: in a game, it is "the winning condition has been met"; in a relationship, it is "I have decided to end this relationship." The specific content varies across systems, but its function is uniform: to trigger the onset of acceleration. This definition is precise enough for identification.

**Objection 4: The experiments in Appendix A show that boundary acceleration does not occur without a validation signal. Does this constitute a counterexample to the paper?**

**Response:** No. The experiment in Appendix A tests a **system without a validation signal**. It confirms the judgment of Section 4.3: boundary acceleration requires a validation signal to trigger. This is support for the **weak version** of the paper, not a refutation. The paper no longer claims the strong version ("unconditionally necessary"). The strong version has been falsified by the experiment; the weak version ("if a validation signal is present, then necessarily") still awaits direct testing.

### 5.2 Falsifiability Conditions

The **weak version** of boundary acceleration (conditional necessity) is falsified if the following is observed:

> **In a finite system with a clearly identifiable boundary, where the system still holds unreleased signals before the boundary and the system clearly recognizes a validation signal, signal density does not rise before the boundary but instead continuously decreases to zero.**

Specifically, if a system satisfies:

1. The interval boundary is well-defined.
2. The system still holds residual signals before the boundary (i.e., `R(t) > 0` has already been established before the boundary).
3. The system clearly recognizes a validation signal.
4. As the boundary approaches, signal density `D(t)` does not rise, but monotonically decreases or remains at a low level.
5. Zeroing occurs at a low signal density state.

Then the system constitutes a counterexample to the weak version of boundary acceleration.

**Note**: If the system does not recognize a validation signal, it is not within the scope of the weak version. In that case, a smooth boundary does not constitute a counterexample.

**Status of the strong version**: The paper originally claimed the strong version ("unreleased signals imply inevitable acceleration"). The task-queue experiment in Appendix A shows that fixed rate + hard boundary + `R(t) > 0` does not spontaneously produce boundary acceleration. Therefore, the strong version has been falsified by the paper's own experiment, and the paper has been revised to the weak version.

## VI. Conclusion

Boundary acceleration is not a cross-domain analogy. It is a structure that can be derived from the definition of signal density, the non-crossability of the interval boundary, and the system's recognition of a validation signal.

- Geometrically, it is a spike before the boundary.
- Mathematically, it is the conditional-necessity result of signal release within a finite window when the system recognizes a validation signal.
- Conditionally, it occurs only when the system still holds residual signals and recognizes a validation signal.

**Before zeroing, there is always one last manifestation — if the system recognizes the boundary.**

This is not a claim — it is a testable prediction: if a system holds unreleased signals at the boundary and the system recognizes a validation signal, its signal density curve must have a spike before the boundary.

This paper is a supplementary essay to the *Limit Theory of Meaning* (Zhong, Shanzhen, 2026a), focusing on the structural details of the zeroing process. It also provides the mathematical derivation for the claim in the *Limited-Interval Principle* (Zhong, Shanzhen, 2026b) that "boundary acceleration is an instance of the limited-interval principle."

## Appendix A: Experimental Verification

This appendix reports numerical experiments testing the core claim of *Boundary Acceleration*. The experiment code and raw outputs are located in the `uncertainty-subsumption/` directory of the project repository.

### A.1 Experimental Design

The experiment uses a task-queue model. The system must process W tasks before a deadline T. The processing rate is fixed; the model contains no acceleration mechanism. Signal density D(t) — the amount actually processed per step — is measured as T approaches.

Three regimes:

| Regime | W | T | Fixed rate | Outcome |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | 1000 | 100 | 10 | Finished exactly |
| 2 | 1000 | 100 | 5 | 495 left unprocessed |
| 3 | 1000 | 100 | 1 | 899 left unprocessed |

### A.2 Results

| Regime | D(t) behavior | R(T) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Constant 10.00 throughout; zero at T | 0 |
| 2 | Constant 5.00 throughout; still 5.00 at T | 495 |
| 3 | Constant 1.00 throughout; still 1.00 at T | 899 |

In all three regimes, D(t) remains constant; there is no rise before the boundary.

### A.3 Conclusion

**Fixed rate + hard boundary + R(t) > 0 does not spontaneously produce boundary acceleration.**

The derivation in Section III of the main text carries an implicit assumption: that the system will accelerate automatically because "signals cannot be released after the boundary." But the experiments show that, **without a mechanism that recognizes the boundary, the system does not accelerate — it simply leaves tasks unprocessed and stops.**

This corresponds exactly to the notion of a "validation signal" in Section 4.3. Within the paper there are two formulations:

- Section 4.2: boundary acceleration is a logical necessity (strong version).
- Section 4.3: boundary acceleration requires a validation signal to trigger (weak version).

**The experiments support the weak version and falsify the strong version.**

Therefore the position of the paper is revised to: boundary acceleration is **conditionally necessary**; the condition is that the system recognizes a validation signal. Without a validation signal, R(t) > 0 does not spontaneously produce a spike. The main text has been revised accordingly.

### A.4 Direction for Future Verification

The weak version itself has not yet been directly tested. To test it, one needs a system capable of recognizing the boundary: a system that can observe remaining time and remaining tasks and adjust its rate accordingly. If such a system spontaneously accelerates as it approaches T, the weak version holds. This is future work.
