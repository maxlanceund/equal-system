# Boundary Acceleration: Signal Compression and Release Before the Boundary

> **Core Thesis**: Before reaching the boundary, a system that still holds unreleased signals will experience a sharp rise in signal density, followed by a return to zero. Geometrically, it is a spike; mathematically, it is the logical necessity of "remaining signals must be released within a finite window." Boundary acceleration is not a cross‑domain analogy, but a testable structure — before zeroing, there is always a last manifestation.

---

## Abstract

The *Limit Theory of Meaning* (Lanceund, 2026a) has established that meaning is a signal generated by finite systems within finite intervals. At the boundary, meaning zeroes out — but motion does not cease. However, zeroing is not always smooth. In some cases, signal density rises sharply before the boundary, and only then zeroes out. This paper terms this phenomenon "boundary acceleration" and demonstrates, through mathematical derivation and a geometric diagram, that it is a testable structure — not a cross‑domain analogy. Boundary acceleration is a necessary consequence of "meaning zeroes out, but motion does not cease." The paper further shows that boundary acceleration requires a *validation signal* to trigger the release process, rather than occurring automatically.

**Keywords**: boundary acceleration · signal density · zeroing · motion reorganization · finite interval · limit theory of meaning

This paper's contribution lies in extending Luhmann's concept of system boundary by formally describing the dynamics of signal density before the boundary. The derivation in this paper does not depend on any specific assumptions of systems theory — it proceeds solely from the definition of signal density and the non‑crossability of the boundary, and is therefore independent of Luhmann's framework.

## Theoretical Framework and Methodological Statement

### Definition of Core Concepts

Before proceeding, it is necessary to strictly define the core concepts used in this paper.

**Finite interval**: a temporal or spatial range within which a system operates, with a clear starting point and endpoint. Beyond this interval, the system's signal‑processing function is no longer defined.

**Signal density `D(t)`**: the amount of signal that the system generates or carries at time `t`. Signals can be information, energy, resources, attention, or any other quantifiable element that flows within the system.

**Total remaining signal `R(t)`**: the total signal the system has yet to release from the current moment to the boundary. Defined as:

`R(t) = ∫ₜᵀ D(s) ds`

**Boundary**: the endpoint `T` of the interval, where the system's signal processing terminates. At the boundary, signal density zeroes out: `D(T) = 0`.

**Boundary acceleration**: the phenomenon in which signal density rises sharply before the boundary and then zeroes out. Geometrically, it appears as a spike.

**Validation signal**: the internal judgment that triggers boundary acceleration — the moment when the system recognises that "the window has opened" or "conditions are mature." Before the validation signal appears, signal density remains low; after it appears, the system enters a high‑density state.

### Methodological Statement: Formal Derivation and Structural Description

The methodological path adopted in this paper is a combination of **formal derivation** and **structural description**. Specifically, starting from the definition of signal density and the non‑crossability of the interval boundary, this paper uses mathematical derivation to prove the necessity of boundary acceleration, and uses a geometric diagram to visualise it. This paper does not rely on empirical cases to "prove" the existence of boundary acceleration — its necessity has already been derived mathematically. Empirical cases are used only to demonstrate how this structure manifests in different domains.

The types of evidence used in this paper include:

1. **Formal definitions**: strict mathematical definitions of signal density, remaining signal, and related concepts.
2. **Mathematical derivation**: deriving the necessity of boundary acceleration from the definitions.
3. **Geometric diagram**: visualising the derivation results.
4. **Condition analysis**: specifying the conditions under which boundary acceleration occurs and the mechanism of its trigger.
5. **Falsifiability conditions**: proposing testable conditions under which the theory would be falsified.

## III. Mathematical Derivation

Let `D(t)` be the signal density function, defined on the interval `[0, T]`.

Define the **total remaining signal** as:

`R(t) = ∫ₜᵀ D(s) ds`

That is: the total signal the system has yet to release from the current moment to the boundary.

The system's behavior is constrained by the following conditions:

1. Within the interval, `D(t) ≥ 0` (signal density cannot be negative).
2. At the boundary, `D(T) = 0` (signal zeroes out).
3. The system must release all signals before the boundary: `R(0) = ∫₀ᵀ D(s) ds < ∞`.

If the signal release rate is roughly constant (i.e., `D(t)` changes slowly), then signal density should gradually decrease to zero at the boundary.

However, if the system still holds unreleased signals near the boundary, and signals cannot be released after the boundary, they must be released before the boundary. This requires signal density to be higher than normal in a period before the boundary.

**Derivation:**

Let the signal release rate be `dD/dt`. Suppose the system holds unreleased signals near the boundary, i.e., `R(t₀) > 0` for some `t₀` close to `T`.

Since signals cannot be released after `T` (the interval has ended), `R(t₀)` must be released within a finite window of length `T - t₀`.

This means that within this window, `D(t)` must be higher than the system's average release capacity — otherwise the signal cannot be fully released before the boundary.

**Therefore: unreleased signal + non‑crossable boundary ⇒ boundary acceleration.**

This is the mathematical basis of boundary acceleration.

## IV. Structural Properties

### 4.1 Conditionality

Boundary acceleration is not inevitable. It occurs only when:

1. The system still holds unreleased signals near the boundary: `R(t) > 0`.
2. Signals cannot be released after the boundary: `D(T) = 0`.
3. The release window is finite: `T - t < ∞`.

If the system has already released all signals in advance (i.e., `R(t) = 0` is already satisfied before the boundary), the boundary may be smooth.

### 4.2 Necessity of Signal Density Increase

From the derivation above, it follows:

If `R(t₀) > 0` and `T - t₀` is sufficiently small, then there exists a subinterval `[a, b] ⊂ [t₀, T]` such that `D(t)` is higher than `D(t₀)`.

Otherwise, the signal cannot be fully released before `T`.

**Therefore: boundary acceleration is a logical necessity of signal release within a finite interval — no external cases are needed to argue for it.**

### 4.3 Trigger Condition: The Validation Signal

The original derivation demonstrated that if a system still holds residual signals at the boundary, signal density must rise before the boundary. However, it did not specify when the rise begins.

Observation shows that boundary acceleration is not triggered by the boundary itself, but by the system recognising a **validation signal**.

A validation signal is an internal judgment that indicates:

1. "The window has opened" — the system has obtained the conditions to operate.
2. "The conditions are mature" — the system has confirmed its position relative to the target.

Before the validation signal appears, signal density remains low. After the validation signal appears, the system enters a high‑density state and continues to run until it reaches its peak at the boundary, then zeroes out.

The complete structure of boundary acceleration is:

`Validation signal appears → acceleration initiates → sustained operation → boundary approaches → peak → zeroing`

This revision does not negate the original paper, but refines its "conditionality": boundary acceleration requires not only that the system still holds residual signals at the boundary, but also a validation signal to trigger the release process.

**Corollary:** The appearance of a validation signal is a sufficient condition for acceleration to begin. If at any moment before the boundary the system recognises a validation signal, acceleration will start and continue until the boundary, where it zeroes out.

If the system never recognises a validation signal throughout the entire interval, no acceleration will occur. The boundary may be smooth.

## V. Possible Objections and Falsifiability Conditions

Any theoretical argument must be open to questioning of its core claims. This chapter outlines the main objections this paper may face, responds to each, and then proposes testable falsifiability conditions.

### 5.1 Responses to Possible Objections

**Objection 1: This derivation assumes signal density can rise arbitrarily, but in reality systems may have upper bounds.**

**Response:** The derivation does not require infinite density. It only requires that density rises above normal levels near the boundary. If a system has an upper bound, that upper bound only means acceleration will occur earlier or more intensely — it does not cancel acceleration itself.

**Objection 2: If the system has already released all signals before the boundary, boundary acceleration does not occur.**

**Response:** Correct. This is precisely the conditionality of boundary acceleration. It is not a universal law, but a conditional structure. Its presence or absence is itself an observable signal — a smooth boundary means signals were already released in advance.

**Objection 3: Is the concept of a "validation signal" too vague? Could anything trigger it?**

**Response:** The definition of a validation signal is formal — it is an internal judgment that indicates the system recognises that "the window has opened" or "the conditions are mature." In different systems, the specific content of this judgment varies: in a game, it is "the winning condition has been met"; in a relationship, it is "I have decided to end this relationship." The specific content varies across systems, but its function is uniform: to trigger the onset of acceleration. This definition is precise enough for identification.

### 5.2 Falsifiability Conditions

The theory is falsified if the following is observed:

> **In a finite system with a clearly identifiable boundary, where the system still holds unreleased signals before the boundary, signal density does not rise before the boundary but instead continuously decreases to zero.**

Specifically, if a system satisfies:

1. The interval boundary is well‑defined.
2. The system still holds residual signals before the boundary (i.e., `R(t) > 0` has already been established before the boundary).
3. As the boundary approaches, signal density `D(t)` does not rise, but monotonically decreases or remains at a low level.
4. Zeroing occurs at a low signal density state.

Then the system constitutes a counterexample to boundary acceleration. If such counterexamples are confirmed to exist, the theory does not hold.

To date, no such counterexamples have been observed in the finite systems examined (games, sexuality, relationships, projects, etc.). However, the theory remains open to future testing.

## VI. Conclusion

Boundary acceleration is not a cross‑domain analogy. It is a structure that can be derived from the definition of signal density and the non‑crossability of the interval boundary.

- Geometrically, it is a spike before the boundary.
- Mathematically, it is the logical result of signal release within a finite window.
- Conditionally, it occurs only when the system still holds residual signals, and is triggered by a validation signal.

**Before zeroing, there is always one last manifestation.**

This is not a claim — it is a testable prediction: if a system holds unreleased signals at the boundary, its signal density curve must have a spike before the boundary.
