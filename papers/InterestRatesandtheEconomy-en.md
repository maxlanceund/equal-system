# Interest Rates and the Economy: A Macroeconomic Order Framework from the Classical Perspective

## 1. Abstract
This paper adopts a purely deductive paradigm of classical economics and rejects all statistical fitting and empirical tests.

Starting from four fundamental economic axioms, it derives the natural‑order laws governing interest rates, credit, debt and macroeconomic steady‑state through minimalist logical reasoning.

This paper demonstrates that macroeconomic stability originates from the order of price signals and capital‑maturity matching, rather than discretionary demand‑side stimulus; hierarchical regulation of deposit‑loan interest rates, defending the floor for nominal deposit rates, and maintaining expectations of capital scarcity constitute core classical mechanisms for modern‑economic equilibrium.

The full text unfolds across four layers: axioms, theorems, formulae and conclusions, returning to the original sense of order in economic thought.

**Keywords**: Classical economics; interest‑rate signal; maturity matching; macroeconomic order; government boundary

**JEL Classification**: B12, E43, E58, H11

## 2. Introduction: Stimulus Illusion and the Return of Classical Order

Within contemporary macroeconomic debates, policy makers frequently turn to monetary easing and demand stimulus to smooth out economic fluctuations.

Such approaches treat aggregate‑demand management as the primary solution, yet often overlook the inherent boundaries of price signals, capital maturity, and government conduct.

The classical tradition represented by Adam Smith and David Ricardo does not construct theory from statistical observations, but deduces the immanent natural order of society from self‑evident axioms of human economic behaviour.

Economic systems are not repeatable physical experiments. Human expectations and institutional rules evolve continuously; statistical correlation cannot replace analysis of underlying operating mechanisms.


## 3. Fundamental Axioms: Classical Self‑Evident Premises

All deductions in this paper rest upon four self‑evident classical economic axioms:

1. **Axiom of Purchasing‑Power**: The real wealth of households is determined by real purchasing‑power, not nominal monetary returns.

2. **Axiom of Scarcity**: The essential function of prices is to transmit signals of resource scarcity; distortion of these signals breaks market order.

3. **Axiom of Capital Maturity**: Long‑lived assets ought to be matched with long‑term capital; maturity mismatch generates systemic debt risks.

4. **Axiom of Government Boundary**: The legitimate function of government is to consolidate supply‑side foundations and reduce transaction costs, rather than bailing out private market agents.

## 4. Core Definitions and Minimal Formal Expressions
### 4.1 The Real‑Purchasing‑Power Interest‑Rate Formula

$$
r_r = r_n - \pi
$$

$`r_r`$ denotes the real interest rate, $`r_n`$ the nominal interest rate, and $`\pi`$ the inflation rate.

Theorem: The level of nominal interest rates does not determine societal real wealth; real purchasing‑power lies at the heart of economic equilibrium.

High nominal interest rates mostly represent compensation for inflation and do not signify growth in real returns.

### 4.2 Classical Formula for Long‑run Output

$$
Y=F\left(K,\frac{1}{T}\right)
$$

$Y$ is long‑run output, $`K`$ effective capital stock, and $`T`$ social transaction costs.

Theorem: Long‑run economic growth arises from capital accumulation and falling transaction costs, not monetary expansion or short‑run demand stimulus.

### 4.3 The Floor Rule for Nominal Deposit Rates

Let the floor of positive nominal deposit rates be $`\underline{r_n}=0.75\%`$

Theorem: Nominal deposit rates may decline moderately but must not approach zero.

Near‑zero nominal rates erode savings incentives and signals of capital scarcity, triggering a liquidity trap.

## 5. System of Core Theorems
### 5.1 Theorem 1: Separate‑Order Theorem for Deposit and Loan Rates

Deposit‑rate adjustments serve the repair of banks’ liability side; loan‑rate settings transmit scarcity signals to markets.

Deposit rates may be fine‑tuned moderately, while loan‑rate policy prioritises stable expectations. The two functions are independent and ought not to be mechanically tied together.

### 5.2 Theorem 2: Steady‑State Theorem for Lending Rates

Lending rates should not trend persistently downward. In the long run they ought to stabilise and converge gently toward their equilibrium level.

Permanently cheap capital weakens agents’ incentives for debt restructuring, accumulates maturity mismatch, and constitutes a major source of macroeconomic disequilibrium.

### 5.3 Theorem 3: Inflation Erodes Nominal Returns

Public preference for high nominal deposit rates reflects a widespread economic illusion.

Higher nominal interest rates are generally accompanied by higher inflation; paper gains on deposits are eroded by rising prices. Moderate nominal rates paired with stable inflation are more conducive to genuine growth in household wealth.

### 5.4 Ultimate Theorem of Debt Order

The root cause of most macro‑debt crises: **short‑term credit money is persistently allocated to long‑lived fixed assets**.

Artificial monetary easing, distorted interest‑rate signals, and government bailout expectations amplify maturity mismatch. Economies then survive on rolling over debt and become decoupled from constraints imposed by real output.

## 6. Conclusion

A healthy macroeconomy does not rely on discretionary policy to eliminate all fluctuations; it achieves equilibrium through self‑adjusting natural order.

Four classical cornerstones of stability are: a floor for deposit rates, steady‑state lending rates, undistorted price signals, and matched capital maturities.

Recurring inflation, debt overhang and economic sluggishness in modern societies usually stem not from excessively high interest rates per se, but from broken interest‑rate signals and collapsed economic order.

Optimal macroeconomic governance follows simple principles and returns to the natural order uncovered by classical economics.
