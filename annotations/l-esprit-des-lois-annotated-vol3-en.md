# Annotations on *The Spirit of the Laws* · Volume III

● Montesquieu  
○ Lanceund


## Ⅰ

● The ends of the external fibers are contracted by cold air, which increases their elasticity and helps the blood flow back to the heart. Cold shortens the length of the fibers, thereby increasing their strength. Conversely, hot air relaxes and lengthens the fibers, diminishing their elasticity and strength. Thus, people in cold climates have more vigor. … In northern climates, love has little physiological effect. In warm climates, love is accompanied by many pleasing things that appear to be love but are not love itself. In hot climates, love is sought for its own sake—it is the sole cause of happiness, it is life itself. … When the climate is excessively hot, people become weak, and a state of languor invades the spirit. They have no curiosity, no noble ambition, no generosity. All preferences are negative; idleness is happiness. It is easier to be punished than to think, easier to be enslaved than to guide one's own actions.

○ This passage is the strongest proof of Montesquieu's observational power. He starts from a specific physical phenomenon—cold air contracts the fibers, hot air relaxes them—and then builds a step-by-step chain: physical state affects mental state, mental state affects national character, national character affects laws and government. The entire logical chain is solid, and every intermediate conclusion can be empirically tested. If one only read his first volume on "law derived from the nature of things," one might think he is an a priori thinker. But this passage shows that he is also an empirical observer. He can set aside definitions and return to the world itself to describe differences. His conclusions are simplified—climate divided into cold and hot, national character into hardy and timid—but on that simplified foundation, he constructs a clear signal flow chain: physical climate → bodily state → mental disposition → customs → legal institutions. The direction of this chain is correct. He overlooks two things, but he is not wrong—he simply did not have time to develop them: first, climate is not the only boundary condition; trade, geography, and technological change are equally important; second, humans can modify their own boundary conditions; they are not entirely determined by climate. In this passage, he has already proven one thing: he is a thinker who can shift perspectives. He is not someone with only one system. This passage does not need refutation—only recognition: he is right.


## Ⅱ

● Indians are born without courage—even European children born in India lose the courage of their native climate. Yet how can this state coexist with their cruel practices, their coarse customs, and their barbarous austerities? The men torture themselves with unimaginable pain; the women burn themselves alive. This is the immense force that confronts extreme weakness. Nature has given these people weakness, making them timid by nature, but it has also given them a very active imagination that makes them overreact to everything. Their fragile organs make them fear death, but they also make them fear countless other things even more. The same feeling that makes them avoid all danger also makes them disregard all danger. … In the Roman era, the peoples of northern Europe had neither art, nor education, nor laws. Yet, by virtue of the common sense produced by the coarse fibers of their climate, they resisted the powerful Romans with admirable wisdom—and kept fighting until they emerged from the forests and destroyed them.

○ This passage is another burst of Montesquieu's observational power. He captures a genuine contradiction: southerners are physically weak and naturally timid, yet they behave in extremely extreme ways—self-immolation, austerities, torturing themselves with unimaginable pain. He does not avoid this contradiction; he tries to explain it: timidity and extremism are not opposites, but different expressions of the same soft structure—imagination too active, the sense of fear amplified, the sense of the sacred also amplified. He ends with a concise contrast: people in southern climates need legislators to guide them, while northern Europeans, with only their natural courage, can resist Rome. This is writing in the mode of an observer—not glorifying anyone, not demeaning anyone, only describing differences and then asking why they exist. Direct affirmation.


## Ⅲ

● Orientals, because of the delicacy of their organs, receive the strongest impressions from the world. Physical idleness naturally affects the mind, and the mind, due to idleness, is no longer able to act, make an effort, or even concentrate. If we combine the delicacy of the organs with the idleness of the mind, we can easily understand that once the mind has received an impression, it cannot change. Thus, the laws, customs, manners, and even seemingly trivial things like clothing styles in the East today are still the same as they were thousands of years ago.

○ This passage describes a state of system lock. The laws, customs, and manners of the East have not changed for thousands of years, not because Easterners are "backward" or "ignorant," but because the boundary conditions are too stable: hot climate → delicate body → idle mind → once a conception is formed, it cannot be updated. This observation itself is correct. He describes a state in which signal flow has almost stopped—the system is still running, but it has lost the capacity for self-renewal. This passage needs no refutation and no supplement. His logical chain is solid, his conclusion is observation-based. Direct affirmation.


## Ⅳ

● Indians believe that stillness and emptiness are the foundation and end of all things. Thus, they regard absolute inaction as the highest state and the goal of existence. They call the supreme being "True Thusness." The Siamese believe that not moving the machine, not moving the body—this is the highest happiness. The intense heat of these regions makes people listless; stillness is comfortable, movement is painful. So this system of thought seems natural. The Indian legislator, Buddha, followed this feeling and placed people in a state of extreme passivity. However, Buddha's doctrine, born of climate-induced idleness, in turn reinforced that idleness, causing countless ills. The Chinese legislators were more reasonable. They did not place people in a state without conflict, but in a state of action that would enable them to fulfill their duties—making their religion, philosophy, and laws all practically useful. The more material factors tend to make people static, the more moral factors should push them away from those material factors.

○ This passage is the best proof that Montesquieu approaches problems from reality, not from prejudice. He does not say that "Orientals are ignorant" or "Orientals are backward"; he attributes the problem to climate: heat makes people idle—this is a physical fact. Then he distinguishes two responses: Indian and Siamese religion went along with this idleness, sanctified it, institutionalized it, locking the system into a stagnant state. China did the opposite—the legislators used "moral factors"—duty, law, obligation—to counteract the natural tendency toward stillness. Material conditions pull people down; institutions pull them up. This contrast shows that what he really cares about is not "who is superior to whom," but "how different systems respond to the same boundary conditions." He is observing a real difference, not repeating the cliché of "the West is advanced, the East is backward." This is high-quality comparative work. Direct affirmation.


## Ⅴ

● Cultivating the land is man's most important labor. The more the climate makes people avoid this labor, the more religion and law should encourage them to engage in it. Therefore, the Indian laws that handed the land over to the sovereign, destroying any sense of ownership, worsened the effects of the climate—that is, they exacerbated the innate idleness.

○ This passage is the most precise strike in the entire volume. The climate already makes people tend toward idleness. If the law could encourage cultivation, it could offset the climate's effects. But Indian law did the opposite—it abolished ownership and made idleness worse. Here Montesquieu has touched on a deeper issue than climate: institutions can amplify or correct the effects of climate. Climate is the initial condition; institutions are the response mechanism. A faulty response is more deadly than the climate itself. Well written. Direct affirmation.


## Ⅵ

● Monasticism produced the same ill effects there. This institution originated in the hot regions of the East, where people prefer contemplation to action. The number of monks in Asia seems to increase with the heat—India is extremely hot, so monks are extremely numerous. The same difference can be observed in Europe. To overcome climate-induced idleness, laws should deprive people of unearned means of living. But the laws of southern Europe did the opposite—they provided the leisure-loving with places for contemplative life and endowed those places with great wealth. Wealth became a burden to these people, so they dealt with it correctly: they distributed the surplus to the lower classes. The lower classes, having lost their property and received compensation from the rich, lived in idleness and actually came to love their poverty.

○ This passage is the culmination of the previous two. In the previous two sections he dealt with land tenure (India) and monasticism (hot regions) and how they amplified the climate's idleness effect. This passage brings those observations together and draws a parallel to southern Europe: southern European law did similar things—providing wealth and space for the idle, even distributing surplus to the poor, making them also live without working. Climate → institution → behavior → social consequence—the chain is complete, the force precise. In these few sections, he has already moved from "climatic determinism" to "the interaction between climate and institutions"—this is real social analysis. This passage itself needs no refutation and should not be refuted—it is good observation, good argumentation.


## Ⅶ

● The records speak of the emperor's annual plowing ceremony, a public and solemn ritual intended to encourage cultivation. Not only that, the emperor each year, based on reports, bestowed the eighth rank of office on the best farmer of the year. The ancient Persians called the eighth day of each month "Labor Day," on which the king left his table and ate with the farmers. This was a good way to encourage agriculture.

○ This passage is a concrete elaboration of the previous logic: climate makes people tend toward idleness, but institutions can resist this tendency. Montesquieu cites the Chinese emperor's plowing, the granting of office to the best farmer, and the Persian king's dining with farmers—showing that he noticed that different civilizations recognized the idleness problem caused by climate and responded to it in different ways. This is empirical recording, nothing to refute. Direct affirmation.


## Ⅷ

● I will discuss in Book XIX that lazy nations are usually proud. The effect can be used against the cause—pride can be used to destroy laziness. Southern Europeans greatly value honor, so it might be a good idea to give prizes to the best farmers and workers who contribute most to industry—perhaps this could succeed in all countries. This method has already proved effective in the construction of the largest textile factory in Europe, located in Ireland.

○ This passage shows Montesquieu's institutional design thinking: laziness produces pride, but pride can be redirected to fight laziness—using honor and rewards to motivate labor, rather than punishment or coercion. He touches on a real issue: human motivation can be designed. Climate makes people tend toward idleness, but institutions can redirect behavior. This is another layer of climate-institution interaction: not only can institutions resist climate, they can also use its byproducts—like pride—to resist it. He is someone who asks "what can be done." Direct affirmation.


## Ⅸ

● In hot regions, water in the blood is constantly lost through perspiration, so it must be replenished. The locals prefer water, because wine would coagulate the blood particles after the water has been lost. In cold regions, much water stays in the blood and is not expelled through perspiration, so people there can drink wine without coagulation. Since the body is rich in fluids, it is appropriate to drink strong liquors that accelerate blood flow. Thus, Muhammad's law prohibiting alcohol was clearly a law of the Arabian climate—before him, the Arabs' common drink was also water. The law prohibiting wine among the Carthaginians was also based on climatic reasons. The two climates are in fact very similar. The cold climate seems to encourage a universal taste for drinking, different from individual preferences. Thus, prohibition is a good law in cold climates. … Different needs in different climates produce different lifestyles, and different lifestyles produce different laws. Nations that have frequent contact need certain laws; those that have no contact need others.

○ This passage is another expansion of Montesquieu's climate theory—this time from fluid balance to diet to legal differences. His logical chain is: climate → fluid state → suitable drink (water or wine) → different lifestyles → different laws. He cites Muhammad's prohibition, Carthaginian prohibition, and cold-climate drinking as examples, showing that these laws were not produced out of thin air but were products of climatic conditions. This is typical climate-institution analysis, not moral judgment. Nothing to refute. Direct affirmation.


## Ⅹ

● Herodotus tells us that the Jewish laws on leprosy came from Egyptian practice. Indeed, the same disease requires the same remedy. The Greeks and early Romans knew neither these laws nor this disease. The climate of Egypt and Palestine made these laws necessary; the speed and extent of the disease's spread make us deeply appreciate the foresight of these laws. We have ourselves experienced the effects of these laws. The Crusaders brought leprosy to France, and thanks to these wise regulations, it did not ravage the masses. … The Turks had no such regulations. They watched Christians in the same city escape the plague while they themselves died of it. They bought the clothes of the infected and wore them. Everything else remained the same. The doctrine of "fate cannot be resisted" dominated everything, turning officials into indifferent spectators. They believed that since God had already done everything, they need do nothing.

○ This passage extends Montesquieu's climate theory to the level of disease and law. He makes three comparisons: Leprosy: the Egyptian/Palestinian climate produced effective quarantine laws; brought back to Europe by the Crusaders, these laws contained the spread. Syphilis: brought from the New World, it continued to spread because of constant travel to the Americas. Plague: European nations made laws to stop it; the Turks, because of "fate cannot be resisted," did nothing, and only they died. His conclusion: law is a response to real threats, and the response determines whether the system survives. This is not abstract theory—it is tracking real historical differences, observation first, judgment second. Well written. Direct affirmation.


## Ⅺ

● History records no cases of Romans committing suicide without reason, but the English commit suicide for no reason anyone can imagine. Roman suicide was the result of education, related to their way of thinking and customs. English suicide is a consequence of disease, rooted in the physical state of the body, with no other cause. There are signs that the problem lies in a deficiency in the nervous fluid. The internal drive is often inactive, so people become weary of themselves. The mind feels no pain, but finds existence unbearable. Pain is a local discomfort that creates a desire to eliminate it. The weight of life also causes pain—but one cannot say where it hurts, and so the desire to end life arises. Obviously, the civil laws of certain countries have reason to make suicide dishonorable. But England cannot eliminate suicide without eliminating the consequences of mental disorder.

○ This passage is another proof of Montesquieu's observational acuity. He splits the same act (suicide) into two completely different roots: Roman suicide is cultural, English suicide is physiological. The former can be explained by education, the latter only by the body. He is not arguing whether suicide is right or wrong—he is asking: "Why does the same act look so different in different places?" His theory of nervous fluid may not be correct, but the direction is right: body and mind are not separate; the physical state directly affects how one experiences one's own existence. His conclusion is also practical: law cannot fix what the body produces; England's problem cannot be solved by law. This is his precision as an observer—he sees distinctions others miss, and then finds a reasonable explanation for them. Direct affirmation.


## Ⅻ

● In a certain country, due to the powerful effects of the climate, people are disgusted with everything, even life. For those who cannot endure anything, the best government is one that prevents them from blaming anyone for their troubles—a government of law, not of men. So to change the government, one must first abolish the laws. If the people of this country, because of the climate, also lack patience and cannot tolerate things staying the same too long, then the government just mentioned is even more suitable for them. Impatience itself is not a great matter, but when combined with courage, it becomes something remarkable. … Politics is like a blunt file, slowly wearing down a workpiece little by little. The people just mentioned are not like that. They cannot endure the delays, the pettiness, the calmness of negotiations. They are less likely to succeed in negotiation than any other nation—they often lose in treaties what they won on the battlefield.

○ In this passage, he is not praising the English. He describes a physiological state of disgust induced by climate, and then analyzes the dual consequences of this character under a free system: on one hand, it effectively resists tyranny, because it will not tolerate the slow accumulation of oppression; on the other hand, it makes the nation bad at negotiation—because negotiation requires patience, delay, and calm. He is a sufficiently calm analyst who does not praise a character as perfect just because it is advantageous in one aspect. Good is good. Direct affirmation.


## ⅩⅢ

● Our ancestors, the Germans, lived in a climate of great calm. Their laws only dealt with visible things and did not speculate further. Moreover, their laws judged the victim's injury by the size of the wound and made no finer distinctions regarding harm to women. The Germanic laws on this point were quite peculiar. Forcing a woman to bare her head: fine six sous. Forcing her to bare her leg up to the knee: six sous. Forcing her to bare above the knee: double. The law measured humiliation like a geometric figure—it punished visible crimes, not imagined ones. But when a branch of the Germans migrated to Spain, the climate produced different laws. The Visigothic laws forbade doctors from bleeding a freeborn woman unless her parents, brothers, sons, or uncles were present. When the people's imagination is inflamed, the legislator's imagination is also inflamed. Since the people may suspect everything, the law suspects everything. … The Moors, sharing the customs of the Spanish, entered Spain without difficulty, established themselves there, and delayed the fall of the Moorish empire—we should not be surprised.

○ This passage is one of the most brilliant analyses in the entire volume. He makes a comparison: in the cold climate, Germanic law only punished visible crimes, with precise, geometry-like fines. The same people, after migrating to Spain, experienced a warmer climate—the people's imagination was ignited, and the legislator also began to suspect everything. The law became a tool of excessive suspicion. He is not just saying "the law changed"—he is describing the process by which the entire system's operating mode was rewritten by changing boundary conditions: climate → imagination → law → social structure. The chain is complete, the logic clear, and he uses the same people in different climates—a natural experiment. What he accomplishes here is not just a historical observation, but a structural analysis of how systems respond to changes in boundary conditions. Direct affirmation.


## ⅩⅣ

● The Japanese are cruel by nature, and their legislators and magistrates trust them in nothing. They place before them only judgments, threats, and punishments—every move is a fault, every place is a trap. Their law requires that out of every five households, one be chosen as a headman to oversee the other four. One man's crime punishes the whole family, even the whole district. One man is accused—all are suspected, none is innocent. The purpose of these laws is to make all distrust one another, no one unobserved, no one not observing, everyone the watcher, witness, and judge of others' actions. In contrast, the Indians are mild, kind, compassionate by nature, and trusted by their legislators. The legislators set few punishments, not severe, and often not strictly enforced. They entrust nephews to uncles, orphans to guardians—just as elsewhere they entrust them to fathers. In determining inheritance, they consider the character and ability the heir has shown in public. They seem to think every citizen should trust in the good nature of others. They free slaves readily, arrange their marriages, and treat them like children. Good climate makes people gentle, and law follows.

○ This passage takes the climate → character → trust → law chain to its extreme through a sharp contrast. Japan: climate makes people cruel → legislators distrust the people → law centered on surveillance, collective punishment, and punishment → everyone watches everyone. India: climate makes people gentle → legislators trust the people → law mild, punishments light → even slaves are trusted. He is not repeating a simple formula of "climate determines law." He is saying: climate shapes the basic state of people; legislators design institutions based on their perception of that state; institutions in turn reinforce that state. It is a reciprocal system, not one-way determination. This kind of observation requires more than knowledge—it requires seeing distinctions others miss. This passage can be affirmed directly—good writing is good writing.


## Afterword

○ After reading Volume III, I can say one thing for certain:

Montesquieu is a writer who can shift perspectives. In Volume I he builds a system. In Volume II he classifies governments. In Volume III he puts down his definitions and returns to the world itself. He is not the kind of writer who can only spin around inside his own framework, nor the kind who needs to tear others down in order to lift himself up.

He sees how climate affects the body, how the body affects the mind, how the mind affects institutions. He sees how different civilizations respond to the same boundary conditions. He sees that institutions can amplify or correct natural tendencies. He sees how the same people, in a different climate, produce different laws. He sees that the same kind of behavior can have completely different roots in different places. He sees the spread of disease, the legal response, the cost of inaction.

Not all of his observations are correct. But the direction is always right.

He is not the kind of thinker who works it all out in his study. He has read, traveled, observed, compared. His conclusions come from the world itself, not from his imagination of it. What he does is one of the hardest things in the history of thought: he describes differences without glorifying one side or denigrating the other, and then he asks why those differences exist.

He deserves the title. A real thinker is not someone who never makes mistakes. A real thinker is someone who can see distinctions that others miss, and then has the courage to speak them out loud.

After reading these three volumes, I can admit one thing: he is better than me. But he is not someone I need to bow to. He is someone I can stand beside and look at the world together.
