# Annotations on *The Spirit of the Laws* · Volume II

● Montesquieu  
○ Lanceund


## Ⅰ

● There are three kinds of government: republican, monarchical, and despotic. This is what I call the nature of each government. We must now see what laws follow directly from this nature, and are therefore the fundamental laws.

○ Montesquieu's definition has three problems:

- He does not ask whether the definition itself holds. "The people rule" in a republic, "the monarch rules by law" in a monarchy, are treated by him as fixed and locked premises.
- People are not fixed. People are uncertainty. They change, drift, accelerate at the boundary, and stop at the boundary.
- Inequality is structural. Republics have republican problems, monarchies have monarchical problems, despotisms have despotic problems. Inequality is not a malfunction—it is how government is built.

He stops at "who rules." I ask: what actually keeps a system running? It is signal flow. It is uninterrupted exchange. It is not definitions.


## Ⅱ

● In a democracy, the people express their will through votes. The laws that establish the right of suffrage are therefore fundamental. Who votes, for whom, and on what matters—these things are important. In Athens, a foreigner was put to death for mingling in the assembly, because he usurped the supreme power. The number of citizens in the Roman assemblies was never fixed—and this was one of the great causes of its ruin. The people should do for themselves what they can do well, and what they cannot, they must do through ministers. Ministers must be appointed by the people; otherwise they are not the people's ministers.

○ Montesquieu's analysis of republican government is not wrong. He clearly describes how suffrage, citizenship, and magistracy operate. Who can vote, who cannot—this determines who is a citizen and who is an outsider.

But his analysis stays at the institutional level:

- Electoral rules solve the question of "who rules," not the question of "whether the structure is fair."
- The boundary between citizen and non-citizen is itself a signal distribution.
- The division between internal nodes and marginal nodes does not disappear just because the form of power changes.

I ask: no matter who is in charge, what keeps the system running? It is uninterrupted exchange, not electoral rules. Power changes hands, but the structure does not.


## Ⅲ

● In an aristocracy, the supreme power is in the hands of a certain number of persons. The rest of the people stand to them as subjects to a monarch in a monarchy. Where the number of nobles is large, there should be a senate to prepare proposals. Noble families should place themselves as much as possible among the people. The more an aristocracy approaches democracy, the better it is; the more it approaches monarchy, the more imperfect it becomes. The most imperfect aristocracy is that in which the people become the private slaves of the nobles, as in Poland.

○ Montesquieu says that nobles should draw near to the common people. This is correct.

But the problem is:

- The common people have no power, no property, but the entire ruling order rests on them.
- Without common people working, paying taxes, and obeying, the nobles are nothing.
- "Drawing near" is only a gesture. It does not change the fundamental structure.

Why are the common people always common? Why are the nobles always nobles? That structure remains. Drawing nearer does not change the position.


## Ⅳ

● Monarchical government is that in which a single person governs by fundamental laws. The monarch is the source of all power, but there must be intermediate channels through which power flows. The most natural intermediate and subordinate power is that of the nobility. The fundamental maxim is: no monarch without nobility, no nobility without monarch—but there is a despot.

○ Montesquieu says that monarchy must have nobility standing between the monarch and the people, or it becomes despotism. This description is correct.

But he does not ask:

- Why must the nobility stand in that place?
- If the nobility is not standing in the middle, it is not nobility.
- The identity of the nobility is itself defined by that "middle position."

It is not a chosen function, but an inherited position. He thinks the nobility is a buffer—but the nobility is itself part of the stratified structure.


## Ⅴ

● In monarchies, political affairs are conducted with as little virtue as possible. The state does not depend on love of country, on self-renunciation, on the heroic virtues of the ancients. Law takes the place of all these virtues. Virtue is not the principle of monarchical government.

○ This passage has nothing to say against it. Montesquieu observes that monarchy does not need virtue; law takes its place, and the baseness of courtiers is the norm. This is observation, not argument, without hidden assumptions. Pass.


## Ⅵ

● Monarchy has another spring: honor. Honor replaces political virtue and can inspire the finest actions. In a well-regulated monarchy, nearly everyone is a good citizen, but good men are rare. To be a good man, one must first wish to be a good man, and love the state for its own sake, not for oneself.

○ This passage is fine. Montesquieu says monarchy is driven by honor:

- It produces good citizens, not good men.
- Good citizens obey, submit, act correctly.
- Good men have their own internal standards and are not easily absorbed into the system.

He draws a clear distinction. Pass.


## Ⅶ

● The principle of despotic governments is by no means honor. There, all are equal, and no one can claim to be above another. All are slaves. Honor has its own rules and depends only on itself. A despot cannot tolerate honor, because honor makes a display of contempt for life—while the despot has power only because he can take life. Despotic states often have no word for honor.

○ This passage is also fine. Despotism and honor have nothing to do with each other. This is factual description, not needing rebuttal. Pass.


## Ⅷ

● Republican government requires virtue, monarchical government requires honor, and despotic government requires fear. In despotism, virtue is not needed, and honor is dangerous. As soon as the prince lowers his arm and cannot instantly crush those in high office, all is lost. Fear no longer exists, and the people are left without protection.

○ This passage is also correct. Despotism is sustained by fear:

- The despot must constantly inspire terror.
- If he weakens, the system begins to loosen.

Pure description. Nothing to argue against. Pass.


## Ⅸ

● In despotic states, the nature of government requires absolute obedience. Once the prince's will is known, it must have its effect immediately. There is no room for modification, compromise, or counsel. Man is only a creature that obeys another creature that commands. Natural feelings, honor, health—all are useless; one must simply obey. Though the modes of obedience differ between these two governments, the power is the same. Whichever way the prince turns, he is always obeyed.

○ This passage is accurate:

- Despotic obedience is absolute, mechanical, without negotiation.
- Moderate obedience is constrained by honor, with room for adjustment.

No need to argue against it. Pass.


## Ⅹ

● The principles of the three governments are as I have described. But this does not mean that everyone is virtuous, honorable, or afraid, but that they ought to be. Otherwise the government would be imperfect.

○ Montesquieu adds a qualification here.

He defined virtue, honor, and fear as the core principles of the three governments, but now he says they are not "actually present" but "ought to be present."

- This means what he described earlier is not an observation of reality, but an ideal state.
- This does not make him wrong, but it shows he had no better solution.
- He first defined unverifiable things, locked them into definitions, and then used "ought" to block further questions.

If someone asks, "Does virtue really exist?" he can answer, "I said it ought to exist." But the trouble is: if virtue only "ought to exist" rather than actually exists, then his explanation of the rise and fall of republics using virtue is insufficient—because he is using something that may not exist to explain something that really happened.

He is not wrong. He stopped at a question he could not handle, and closed it with a definition.
