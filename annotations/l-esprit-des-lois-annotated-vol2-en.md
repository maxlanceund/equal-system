# Annotations on *The Spirit of the Laws* · Volume II

● Montesquieu  
○ Lanceund


## Ⅰ

● There are three kinds of government: `republican`, `monarchical`, and `despotic`. Even the least learned men, with the notions they have of them, are sufficiently capable of discovering their `nature`. I lay down three definitions, or rather three facts: `republican government` is that in which the body, or only a part of the people, is possessed of the supreme power; `monarchy`, that in which a single person governs by fixed and established laws; `despotic government`, that in which a single person, without law and without rule, directs everything by his own will and caprice. This is what I call the `nature of each government`. We must now see what laws follow directly from this nature, and are therefore the `fundamental laws`.

○ Montesquieu divides governments into three types: `republic`, `monarchy`, and `despotism`. He defines each and says, “this is the `nature of government`.” But he does not ask whether the definitions themselves hold. He assumes that “the people rule” in a republic, “the monarch rules by law” in a monarchy, and “the despot rules by will” in despotism are fixed and stable. But people are not fixed. People are `uncertainty`. They change, drift, accelerate at the `boundary`, and stop at the `boundary`. More fundamentally: every type of government has its problems. Republics have republican problems, monarchies have monarchical problems, despotisms have despotic problems. `Inequality` is not a malfunction of government—inequality is how government is built. His definitions only say “who rules,” but do not explain what keeps a `system` running. It is not definitions, but `signal flow` and `uninterrupted exchange`. He stops at the surface. I ask deeper.


## Ⅱ

● In a republic, when the whole people have the sovereign power, it is a `democracy`; when only a part of the people have it, it is an `aristocracy`. In a democracy, the people are in some respects the sovereign, and in others the subject. The people can only become sovereign through their votes, which express their will. The will of the sovereign is the sovereign himself. In this government, the laws that establish the `right of suffrage` are therefore fundamental. To regulate who votes, how they vote, for whom, and on what matters, is as important as knowing in a monarchy who the monarch is and how he governs. Libanius said that an Athenian was punished with death for mingling in the assembly of the people because he usurped the supreme power. It is of great importance to fix the number of citizens who compose the assembly; otherwise it might be uncertain whether the whole, or only a part, have expressed their will. At Sparta, the assembly consisted of ten thousand citizens. Rome, which was born small and became great, destined to experience all the vicissitudes of fortune, sometimes had all its citizens within its walls, and sometimes had all Italy and part of the world within them; the number of those who composed its assemblies was never fixed—and this was one of the great causes of its ruin. The people who have the sovereign power should do for themselves all that they can do well, and what they cannot do well, they must do through ministers. The ministers are not the people's ministers if they are not appointed by the people; therefore, in this form of government, it is a fundamental rule that the people appoint their ministers—that is, the magistrates.

○ Montesquieu's analysis of `republican government` is correct. He clearly describes how `suffrage`, `citizenship`, and `magistracy` operate in a republic. He says that who can vote and who cannot determines who is a citizen and who is an outsider. These observations are not wrong. But the problem is: these institutions solve the question of “who rules,” but do not solve the question of `structural inequality`. Even if electoral rules are perfect, citizenship is clear, and magistrates are properly appointed—inequality remains. The boundary between citizen and non-citizen is itself a `signal distribution`. The division between `internal nodes` and `marginal nodes` does not disappear just because the form of power changes. He asks “who is in charge.” I ask: no matter who is in charge, what keeps the system running? What keeps it running is `uninterrupted exchange`, not electoral rules. Power changes hands, but the `structure` does not.


## Ⅲ

● In an aristocracy, the supreme power is in the hands of a certain number of persons. It is they who make and execute the laws, and the rest of the people stand to them as subjects to a monarch in a monarchy. In an aristocracy, the choice by lot should not be used, for it would only cause trouble. In fact, in a government that establishes such painful distinctions of rank, even if lot were used, the people's hatred would not diminish, because it is not the magistrate but the noble that is envied.
