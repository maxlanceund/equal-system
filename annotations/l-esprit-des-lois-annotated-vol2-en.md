# Annotations on *The Spirit of the Laws* · Volume II

● Montesquieu  
○ Lanceund


## Ⅰ

● There are three kinds of government: republican, monarchical, and despotic. Even the least learned men, with the notions they have of them, are sufficiently capable of discovering their nature. I lay down three definitions, or rather three facts: republican government is that in which the body, or only a part of the people, is possessed of the supreme power; monarchy, that in which a single person governs by fixed and established laws; despotic government, that in which a single person, without law and without rule, directs everything by his own will and caprice. This is what I call the nature of each government. We must now see what laws follow directly from this nature, and are therefore the fundamental laws.

○ Montesquieu divides governments into three types: republic, monarchy, and despotism. He defines each and says, “this is the nature of government.” But he does not ask whether the definitions themselves hold. He assumes that “the people rule” in a republic, “the monarch rules by law” in a monarchy, and “the despot rules by will” in despotism are fixed and stable. But people are not fixed. People are uncertainty. They change, drift, accelerate at the boundary, and stop at the boundary. More fundamentally: every type of government has its problems. Republics have republican problems, monarchies have monarchical problems, despotisms have despotic problems. Inequality is not a malfunction of government—inequality is how government is built. His definitions only say “who rules,” but do not explain what keeps a system running. It is not definitions, but signal flow and uninterrupted exchange. He stops at the surface. I ask deeper.


## Ⅱ

● In a republic, when the whole people have the sovereign power, it is a democracy; when only a part of the people have it, it is an aristocracy. In a democracy, the people are in some respects the sovereign, and in others the subject. The people can only become sovereign through their votes, which express their will. The will of the sovereign is the sovereign himself. In this government, the laws that establish the right of suffrage are therefore fundamental. To regulate who votes, how they vote, for whom, and on what matters, is as important as knowing in a monarchy who the monarch is and how he governs. Libanius said that an Athenian was punished with death for mingling in the assembly of the people because he usurped the supreme power. It is of great importance to fix the number of citizens who compose the assembly; otherwise it might be uncertain whether the whole, or only a part, have expressed their will. At Sparta, the assembly consisted of ten thousand citizens. Rome, which was born small and became great, destined to experience all the vicissitudes of fortune, sometimes had all its citizens within its walls, and sometimes had all Italy and part of the world within them; the number of those who composed its assemblies was never fixed—and this was one of the great causes of its ruin. The people who have the sovereign power should do for themselves all that they can do well, and what they cannot do well, they must do through ministers. The ministers are not the people's ministers if they are not appointed by the people; therefore, in this form of government, it is a fundamental rule that the people appoint their ministers—that is, the magistrates.

○ Montesquieu's analysis of republican government is correct. He clearly describes how suffrage, citizenship, and magistracy operate in a republic. He says that who can vote and who cannot determines who is a citizen and who is an outsider. These observations are not wrong. But the problem is: these institutions solve the question of “who rules,” but do not solve the question of “structural inequality.” Even if electoral rules are perfect, citizenship is clear, and magistrates are properly appointed—inequality remains. The boundary between citizen and non-citizen is itself a signal distribution. The division between internal nodes and marginal nodes does not disappear just because the form of power changes. He asks “who is in charge.” I ask: no matter who is in charge, what keeps the system running? What keeps it running is uninterrupted exchange, not electoral rules. Power changes hands, but the structure does not.


## Ⅲ

● In an aristocracy, the supreme power is in the hands of a certain number of persons. It is they who make and execute the laws, and the rest of the people stand to them as subjects to a monarch in a monarchy. In an aristocracy, the choice by lot should not be used, for it would only cause trouble. In fact, in a government that establishes such painful distinctions of rank, even if lot were used, the people's hatred would not diminish, because it is not the magistrate but the noble that is envied. Where the number of nobles is large, there should be a senate to decide matters that the whole body cannot decide, and to prepare proposals for the body to consider. In such a case, one might say that the senate practices aristocracy, the body practices democracy, and the people have no part at all. Therefore, noble families should place themselves as much as possible among the people. The more an aristocracy approaches democracy, the better it is; the more it approaches monarchy, the more imperfect it becomes. The most imperfect aristocracy is that in which the subject people become the private slaves of those who command, as in Poland, where the peasants are slaves of the nobles.

○ Montesquieu says that nobles should draw near to the common people. This is correct. The common people have no power, no property, but the entire ruling order rests on them—without common people working, paying taxes, and obeying, the nobles are nothing. But the problem is: drawing near is only a gesture. It does not solve the fundamental issue. Why are the common people always common? Why are the nobles always nobles? That structure remains. Drawing nearer does not change the position.


## Ⅳ

● Monarchical government is that in which a single person governs by fundamental laws. Its nature requires intermediate, subordinate, and dependent powers. I have spoken of intermediate, subordinate, and dependent powers, because in a monarchy the monarch is the source of all political and civil power. Since there are fundamental laws, there must be intermediate channels through which power flows. For if the state were left to the caprice of a single man, nothing would be stable, and there would be no fundamental laws. The most natural intermediate and subordinate power is that of the nobility. The nobility in a sense enters into the essence of monarchy, whose fundamental maxim is: no monarch without nobility, no nobility without monarch—but there is a despot.

○ Montesquieu says that monarchy must have nobility standing between the monarch and the people, or it becomes despotism. This description is correct. But he does not ask: why must the nobility stand in that place? If the nobility is not standing in the middle, it is not nobility. The identity of the nobility is itself defined by that "middle position." It is not a chosen function, but an inherited position. He thinks the nobility is a buffer—but the nobility is itself part of the stratified structure.


## Ⅴ

● In monarchies, political affairs are conducted with as little virtue as possible, like a good machine that is made to have as few movements, springs, and wheels as possible. The state subsists independently of love of country, of desire for glory, of self-renunciation, of sacrifice of one's dearest interests, and of all those heroic virtues that we have only heard of among the ancients. In monarchies, law takes the place of all these virtues. Virtue is not at all necessary, and the state dispenses with it. An action done in silence is not followed by any consequence. ... It is a true maxim that virtue is not the principle of monarchical government. It is not excluded from it, but it is not its moving force.

○ This passage has nothing to say against it. Montesquieu observes that monarchy does not need virtue; law takes its place, and the baseness of courtiers is the norm. This is observation, not argument, without hidden assumptions. Pass.


## Ⅵ

● If I say that monarchy lacks a spring, it has one: honor. This is the idea that each man and each rank has, of his own worth. Honor replaces the political virtue I have mentioned, and represents it everywhere. It can inspire the finest actions, and with the aid of the laws, it can lead to the same ends as virtue. In a well-regulated monarchy, nearly everyone is a good citizen, but good men are rare, because to be a good man, one must first wish to be a good man, and love the state for its own sake, not for oneself.

○ This passage is fine. Montesquieu says monarchy is driven by honor, and it produces good citizens, not good men. Good citizens obey, submit, act correctly; good men have their own internal standards and are not easily absorbed into the system. He draws a clear distinction. Pass.


## Ⅶ

● The principle of despotic governments is by no means honor. There, all are equal, and no one can claim to be above another. All are slaves, and no one can have any kind of superiority. Moreover, honor has its own laws and rules, and it is stubborn, depending only on itself, not on the opinions of others. Thus it can only have a place where the fundamental constitution is fixed and the laws are reliable. How could a despot tolerate honor? Honor makes a display of contempt for life—while the despot has power only because he can take life. How could honor tolerate a despot? It has fixed rules and constant desires, while the despot has no rule and can destroy any desire at a whim. Despotic states not only do not know honor, they often have no word for it. Honor exists in monarchies, where it gives life to all political bodies, laws, and virtues.

○ This passage is also fine. Despotism and honor have nothing to do with each other. This is factual description, not needing rebuttal. Pass.


## Ⅷ

● Republican government requires virtue, monarchical government requires honor, and despotic government requires fear. In despotism, virtue is not needed, and honor is dangerous. The unlimited power of the despot is delegated to those he trusts. Those who think themselves extraordinary are likely to rebel, so fear must stifle all courage and crush ambition at birth. A moderate government can relax its springs without danger; it is supported by laws and forces. But in a despotism, as soon as the prince lowers his arm, as soon as he cannot instantly crush those in high office, all is lost. For fear, the spring of government, no longer exists, and the people are left without protection. ... History tells us that the dreadful cruelties of Domitian terrified the governors and gave the people some respite during his reign, as a torrent that ravages one side of a river leaves the other side untouched.

○ This passage is also correct. Despotism is sustained by fear; the despot must constantly inspire terror, and if he weakens, the system collapses. Pure description. Pass.


## Ⅸ

● In despotic states, the nature of government requires absolute obedience. Once the prince's will is known, it must have its effect immediately, like one ball striking another in a game. There is no room for modification, remonstrance, compromise, negotiation, proposal, or counsel. Nothing can be presented as better or even equal; man is only a creature that obeys another creature that commands. Men cannot express their fears of the future, nor blame fortune for misfortune. They are like beasts, with nothing but instinct, obedience, and punishment. Natural feelings—respect for fathers, love for wives and children, laws of honor, health—are all useless; one must simply obey. ... Though the modes of obedience differ between these two governments, the power is the same. Whichever way the prince turns, the scale tips, and he is always obeyed. In monarchy, the prince is more enlightened; his ministers are more capable and experienced than those in despotism. That is the entire difference.

○ Despotic obedience is absolute, mechanical, without negotiation; moderate obedience is constrained by honor, with room for adjustment. This is an accurate observation. Pass.


## Ⅹ

● The principles of the three governments are as I have described. But this does not mean that everyone in a republic is virtuous, but that they ought to be. Nor does it prove that everyone in a monarchy is honorable, or that everyone in a despotism is afraid, but that they ought to be; otherwise the government would be imperfect.

○ Montesquieu adds a qualification. He defined virtue, honor, and fear as the principles of the three governments, but now he says they are not "actually present" but "ought to be present." That is, what he described earlier is not an observation of reality, but an ideal state. This does not make him wrong, but it shows he had no better solution. He first defined unverifiable things, locked them into definitions, and then used "ought" to block further questions. If someone asks, "Does virtue really exist?" he can answer, "I said it ought to exist." The trouble is: if virtue only "ought to exist" rather than actually exists, then his explanation of the rise and fall of republics using virtue is insufficient—because he is using something that may not exist to explain something that really happened. He is not wrong, but he stopped at a question he could not handle, and closed it with a definition.
