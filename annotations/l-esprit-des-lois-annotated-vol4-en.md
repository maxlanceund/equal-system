# Annotations on *The Spirit of the Laws* · Volume IV

A Montesquieu  
B Lanceund


## Ⅰ Parody (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
If I were really to defend our right to make Negroes slaves, I would say this:

After the Europeans destroyed the peoples of America, they had to make slaves of the Africans in order to cultivate so much land.

If sugar were not cultivated by slaves, the price would be too high.

These people are black from head to toe, and their noses are so flat that they are hardly to be pitied.

We cannot imagine that a wise God would put a soul, and a good soul, into a black body.

It is natural to regard color as the essence of a human being. The Asians who use eunuchs always emphasize that Negroes have nothing to do with us.

The color of the hair can be used to judge the color of the skin. The Egyptians, the greatest philosophers in the world, attached great importance to the color of hair, and put to death all red-haired people who fell into their hands.

Civilized peoples regard gold as precious, but in the eyes of Negroes, glass necklaces are more valuable than gold necklaces—this is clear proof that Negroes lack common sense.

We cannot regard Negroes as human beings; if we did, we would have to doubt whether we ourselves are Christians.

Narrow-minded people exaggerate the injustice we do to Negroes. For if it were really so, could not the European sovereigns, who have concluded so many treaties, have concluded a general treaty for charity and compassion?

B：
In this passage, Montesquieu puts on the mask of a defender. He does not directly say "slavery is wrong"; instead, he pretends to be a supporter of slavery and lays out all the arguments for it—economic cost, physical appearance, divine will, civilizational hierarchy, historical precedent. Every one of them was a real argument at the time, but he refutes none of them. He only lists them.

The most devastating line is: "We cannot imagine that a wise God would put a soul, and a good soul, into a black body." This line exposes the entire defense of slavery for what it is. The defenders claim to know the will of God—they know where God puts souls, and where He does not. They use an unverifiable premise to support a conclusion that can be overturned by experience.

He is doing parody here. He is not expressing a view; he is pushing the other side's logic to its extreme—to the point where even they would be embarrassed to defend it.


## Ⅱ Voluntary Slavery (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
Now is the time to inquire into the true origin of the right of enslavement. It ought to be founded on the nature of things—let us see whether it arises from certain circumstances.

In all despotic governments, it is very easy to sell oneself into slavery, because political slavery there has already killed civil liberty in a certain sense.

Mr. Perry says that Russians sell themselves very easily. I know why: because their freedom is worthless.

In Acheh, everyone tries to sell himself into slavery; some great nobles have no fewer than a thousand slaves, and these slaves are great merchants who themselves have many slaves, whose slaves have many slaves. Slaves can be inherited and bought and sold. The free people there, being too weak to resist the government, try to become slaves of those who exercise tyranny.

This is the reasonable origin of moderate slavery in certain countries. It should be moderate, because it is founded on a man's free choice of his master in order to play his own role, and thus forms a contractual relation between the two parties.

B：
Montesquieu here distinguishes between forced slavery and voluntary slavery. But the problem is: this distinction does not change the nature of the thing. Whether forced or "voluntary," the result is the same—a person becomes property, can be bought and sold, inherited, transferred. The form of contract does not change the content of slavery.

The reason he does this is clear: what he truly defends is not slavery itself, but the classical order he admires—the slavery of ancient Greece and Rome was the foundation of their institutions. He is willing to theoretically dismantle the foundations of racial slavery, but when it comes to the civilization he wants to preserve, his position becomes ambiguous.

He tries to find a "reasonable" foundation for the right of enslavement, but the premise of that foundation is already unreasonable.


## Ⅲ Climatic Slavery (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
Here is another origin of the right of enslavement—one that gives rise to the most cruel slavery in the world.

In some countries, the heat of the climate enervates the body and depresses the spirit, so that only punishment can make people perform arduous duties. In these places, slavery is less shocking to reason. Since the master is too lazy to rule, the slave is too lazy to serve. There, civil slavery coexists with political slavery.

Aristotle tried to prove that some people are born slaves, but his demonstration did not prove it. I think, if there are any people born slaves, they are those I have just mentioned.

Yet all men are born equal—so slavery is against nature, although in some countries it is founded on natural reason. One must distinguish these countries from those where even natural reason rejects slavery, such as the European countries that have long since abolished it.

Plutarch, in his *Life of Numa*, says that in the time of Saturn there were neither masters nor slaves. In our European climate, Christianity has brought us back to that time.

B：
In this passage, Montesquieu turns his climate analysis from a descriptive tool into a justificatory one.

His observation about hot climate affecting the body and spirit is correct—it aligns with his climate analysis in Volume III. The body does tire more easily in heat. The problem is that he extends this observation to justify slavery. His logic runs: because hot climate enervates, punishment is needed to make people work—so in these places, slavery is "less shocking to reason"—even "founded on natural reason."

This is a leap. Climate can explain why labor differs across regions, but it cannot legitimize slavery. In Volume I he said "law is the necessary relation arising from the nature of things"—here he tries to make slavery also arise from the "nature of things." What he really wants to defend is the classical order of Greece and Rome. He uses climate to make slavery look not like a product of violence, but a product of nature.

This view is not entirely wrong—climate does affect people. But he uses it in the wrong place. Climate can explain difference, not justify oppression.


## Ⅳ Free Men and Mines (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
Therefore, slavery founded on natural causes must be limited to certain countries. I think that in all other countries, no matter how hard the labor required by society, it should be performed by free men.

I think so because before Christianity abolished civil slavery in Europe, mining was considered so arduous that only slaves or convicts could do it. But today we know that mine laborers live happily. Small special incentives encourage this occupation; income increases with labor, so miners love their work more than any other they could find.

As long as reason, not greed, governs labor, there is no labor so arduous that human strength cannot bear it. The machines invented or applied in the arts provide conveniences, so that labor done elsewhere by slaves can be done here by machines. The mines in the governorate of Timișoara in Turkey are richer than those in Hungary, yet yield less—because the Turks have only ever used slaves.

I do not know whether this opinion comes from my mind or my heart. Perhaps there is no climate on earth where free men cannot work. When laws are ill-made, there are idle men; when there are idle men, they are made slaves.

B：
The logic of this passage is sound. He makes two points: free men are willing to do hard work if reasonably paid, and machines can replace slave labor. He compares Hungarian and Turkish mines—Turkey uses slaves and produces less—to show that slavery is not only unnecessary but also less efficient.

These observations are correct. The problem is that he is still trying to find a "legitimate scope" for slavery. His phrase "slavery founded on natural causes must be limited to certain countries" is itself a way of using logic to justify the unjustifiable.

Here is what must be made clear: slavery is unjust not because it is inefficient, not because climate does not require it, not because machines can replace it. It is unjust because it treats persons as property. Montesquieu does not address this level here. He discusses whether slavery is "useful," not whether it is "right." He has advanced—admitting free men can do hard work, admitting machines can replace slaves—but he has not crossed the line he drew for himself.

The logic is sound. But sound logic does not justify slavery.


## Ⅴ The Noise of Slavery (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
Every day people say, "How good it would be if we had slaves."

But to judge correctly, one should not consider whether slaves are useful to a few rich people given to luxury in each nation—they are undoubtedly useful to them. But from another perspective, if in this country it were decided by lot who should be free and who should be slaves, I believe none of these rich people would agree to it. Those who most loudly advocate slavery would be the most afraid, and the poorest would also be afraid. So the noise about slavery is the noise of luxury and dissipation, not the voice of concern for the public good. Everyone likes to be the master of another's property, honor, and life—everyone is excited at the thought. Is there any doubt? In these matters, if we want to know whether everyone's desires are just, let us examine everyone's desires.

B：
In this passage, he finally gets it right.

In the previous passages he was still finding excuses for slavery: climate, contract, efficiency, labor intensity. Here he shifts perspective. He no longer asks whether slavery is reasonable—he asks directly: "What do those who advocate slavery actually want?"

His answer: they want their own advantage, not the public good. "Everyone likes to be the master of another's property, honor, and life"—this line goes straight to the root of the problem. If a lottery were held to decide who becomes a slave, those who shout loudest would be the first to refuse.

The fact that he could write this shows that he knew where the root of slavery lay. Not in climate, not in economics—in human desire. He was just making excuses for what he identified with in the previous passages. Here he returned to his own observational power.


## Ⅵ Property-Slavery and Person-Slavery (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
There are two kinds of slavery: one belonging to property, one belonging to the person. Property-slavery ties the slave to the land; the German slaves described by Tacitus are slaves of this kind—they do not serve in the master's house, but only pay a certain amount of grain, cattle, or cloth to the master. The purpose of using such slaves is nothing more than this. There are also such slaves in Hungary, Bohemia, and southern Germany.

Person-slavery is when the slave performs domestic service, mainly concerning the master's household.

Slavery that combines both kinds is an extreme abuse of slavery. The helots of Sparta were of this kind. All outdoor labor was performed by these slaves, and at home they suffered all kinds of indignities. Thus helot slavery is contrary to the nature of things. Frugal commoners have only one property-slave, because wife and children also share in domestic work. Luxurious commoners have person-slaves, because luxury requires domestic service. The helot system made the same person both the person-slave of the luxurious and the property-slave of the frugal.

B：
Here Montesquieu makes a classification of slavery. He distinguishes between slaves tied to the land and slaves tied to the master, and notes that the Spartan combination of both is "extreme abuse."

The classification itself is accurate. There are indeed different forms of slavery—some closer to serfdom, some to domestic service, some both. He notices these differences and is willing to single out Sparta for criticism.

But he still does not ask the fundamental question. He distinguishes between "better" and "worse" forms of slavery, but never takes the step of saying: "Neither is right." He can see that the Spartan helot system is worse than the Germanic serfdom, but he does not go further. This passage can be let pass—the classification is correct, but he still does not push to the end.


## Ⅶ Law and Slavery (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
But whatever the nature of slavery, civil law should do two things: on the one hand, abolish abuses of slavery; on the other, prevent the dangers of slavery.

B：
From a legal perspective, this is correct. Restricting abuse, preventing danger—these are things the law should do.

But he still does not say the essential thing. He talks about "how to manage slavery well," not "whether slavery itself should be abolished." He accepts the existence of slavery and discusses law within that acceptance—but the more fundamental question—whether one person can be the property of another—he never answers directly.

What he says is all correct. But he just does not say the most important thing.


## Ⅷ Female Slaves and Chastity (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
In Islamic countries, the master rules not only the life and property of his female slaves, but even their so-called virtue and conduct are at his disposal. One of the misfortunes of these countries is that most people are born for nothing other than the luxury of others. The reward of this slavery is the laziness allowed to the slaves. This is a new misfortune for the state.

For those confined, this laziness makes the Oriental harem a paradise—those who fear labor find their happiness in the quiet of the harem. But we see here that this contradicts the very purpose of establishing slavery.

According to reason, the master's power should not exceed the services the slave should provide. Slavery was established for usefulness, not for debauchery. The laws protecting chastity belong to natural law and should be known to all nations.

If laws protecting the chastity of slaves are good in countries where power can indulge itself without restraint, would they not be better in monarchies? Would they not be better in republics?

A provision of Lombard law seems applicable to all governments: "If the master violates the slave's wife, the two slaves together acquire the status of free persons." This is an admirable compromise that prevents the master's excess without being too severe.

I have not found that the Romans had any good law on this matter. They allowed the master every license and even in a certain sense deprived slaves of the right to marry. Slaves are the lowliest class of citizens, but no matter how lowly, they too need to know shame. Moreover, depriving slaves of the right to marry also harms the right of citizens to marry.

B：
Montesquieu is saying here that masters should not require slaves to do things beyond their proper service, especially not use slaves' bodies as private property. He cites Lombard law as a positive example and criticizes Roman law for being inadequate.

The logic is correct. Masters should not violate their slaves' wives; slaves should have marriage rights—these judgments are sound.

In this passage, he also moves forward: he acknowledges that "no matter how lowly, they too need to know shame." This means he at least admits that slaves have dignity, that they are not pure instruments.

But he still does not say it. He talks about what "masters should not do," about "laws protecting chastity," about which governments protect it better—but he never confronts the fundamental question: does one person have the right to possess another's body? He has reached "slaves also have shame"—which is a step forward from earlier passages—but he has not crossed the line.


## Ⅸ The Consequences of Slavery under Different Governments (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
The consequences of a large number of slaves differ under different governments. In despotic governments, a large number of slaves is no burden at all, because political slavery already exists in the state, so civil slavery is hardly felt—the freedom of the so-called free is little more than that of those without the title. These unfree persons—eunuchs, freedmen, slaves—control almost everything, and the difference in status between free and slave is very small. So in despotic governments, the number of slaves does not matter.

But in moderate governments, it matters greatly that slaves not be too many. Political freedom makes civil freedom precious; those deprived of it lose political freedom. They watch the whole society living in happiness while they are not members of it. They feel that security is established for others, not for them. They see their master's spirit rising daily while theirs sinks lower. Seeing others enjoy freedom while they cannot—this most deeply makes them feel that their condition differs little from that of cattle. Such people are the enemies of society; if they are many, they are dangerous.

In moderate governments, the state is troubled by slave rebellions—something very rare in despotic governments.

B：
In this passage, Montesquieu sees clearly.

He sees the real reason slavery is dangerous: not the number of slaves, but whether slaves can see others enjoying freedom while they cannot. In despotic governments, no one is free, so slaves do not feel deprived. But in moderate governments, freedom is visible and valued; slaves see daily what they cannot have—and this contrast itself is the most intense form of oppression.

The condition he describes—"watching others happy while not being a member of society"—is where the real danger lies. Rebellion does not come from the most oppressed, but from those who have seen freedom but cannot attain it.

This observation is sharp and accurate.


## Ⅹ Armed Slaves (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
Arming slaves is more dangerous in republics than in monarchies. In monarchies, the martial people and the nobility can effectively control armed slaves. In republics, armed slaves stand on equal footing with citizens, so mere citizenship cannot control them.

After the Goths conquered Spain and dispersed throughout the country, they quickly became weak. They made three important laws: they abolished the old custom forbidding marriage with Romans; they decreed that slaves freed by state funds must serve in wartime, on pain of re-enslavement; and every Goth, when going to war, had to bring one-tenth of his slaves and arm them. Compared with the slaves left behind, one-tenth was a small number. These slaves taken to war by their masters were not formed into separate units, but stayed within the army—in a sense, as if at home.

B：
This analysis is correct. Montesquieu is not discussing whether slavery is right; he is discussing the different risks of armed slaves under different governments. The Goths' approach was clever: they took slaves to war with their masters but did not form them into separate units. Without independent organization, slaves could not form their own political force. This is precise institutional analysis. No problem—direct affirmation.


## Ⅺ Martial Spirit and Armed Slaves (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
If the entire nation is composed of warriors, armed slaves are nothing to fear.

According to Germanic law, a slave who stole something stored in a place was punished the same as a free man; but if he took it by force, he only had to return what he took. For the Germans, anything based on courage and strength was not contemptible. They used slaves in war. In most republics, they tried to crush the courage of slaves; the confident Germans, on the contrary, sought to increase their boldness. The Germans never laid down their arms, so they had no fear of armed slaves—on the contrary, slaves were tools for plunder and gaining glory in war.

B：
This passage follows the same logic as the previous one: whether armed slaves are dangerous depends not on the slaves themselves, but on the rulers' organizational capacity. The Germans were a nation of warriors, never laid down their arms, and were confident in their ability to control the situation—so they not only did not fear armed slaves, but used them as tools for war. The difference in their punishment for theft versus robbery reflects the same martial tendency. This analysis is thorough. Direct affirmation.


## Ⅻ Clemency and Cruelty (Book XV, Chapter V)

A：
In moderate states, a large number of slaves may cause concern, but humane treatment can prevent this danger. As long as the master is not crueler than slavery itself, people can get used to anything—even slavery. The Athenians treated their slaves with great clemency, and while Sparta was torn apart by its slaves, Athens remained tranquil.

We see that the early Romans were not troubled by slaves. It was only after they lost all humane feeling toward slaves that civil wars comparable to the Punic Wars occurred.

People who work themselves treat their slaves more gently than those who do not work. The early Romans lived, worked, and ate with their slaves; they treated them with clemency and justice. Their harshest punishment was to make a slave carry a wooden fork past his neighbors. Morality was enough to maintain the loyalty of slaves; there was no need to resort to law.

But when the Romans became powerful, when slaves became tools of luxury and arrogance rather than companions, and morality had decayed, they had to resort to law—even very harsh law—otherwise the master's safety could not be ensured. These cruel masters lived among slaves as if among enemies.

Thus were enacted the *Senatus Consultum Silanianum* and other laws. These provided that if a master was killed, all slaves living in his house, and all within earshot, were to be executed. Those who concealed a suspect to save him were treated as murderers. A slave who killed his master even by order was still guilty; a slave who failed to prevent his master's suicide was also punished. If a master was killed on a journey, the slaves accompanying him and those who fled were executed. Even if proved innocent, the accused slaves were still punished by these laws. The purpose was to force slaves to respect their masters absolutely. These laws did not arise from the needs of civil administration, but from its defects and imperfections. They violated the principles of civil law and were not derived from its justice. They were founded on the principles of war—the difference being that the enemy was within. The *Senatus Consultum Silanianum* derived from a principle of the *ius gentium*: that a society, however imperfect, must preserve itself.

When magistrates are forced to make such cruel laws, the situation is already very bad, because it is already difficult to make slaves obey, so heavy penalties must be imposed for disobedience, or their loyalty must be suspected. A good legislator can prevent himself from becoming a terrible legislator. For if the slaves of Rome were to trust the law, the law must first trust the slaves.

B：
This passage is also completely sound.

He makes two comparisons—Athens vs. Sparta, early Rome vs. late Rome—to demonstrate a simple truth: treating slaves with clemency makes them less likely to become enemies; treating them with cruelty makes their masters live among enemies. The *Senatus Consultum Silanianum* was a product of this fear—treating slaves as potential enemies, using collective execution to force them to "respect" their masters.

His observations are accurate, his logic coherent. This analysis is solid.
