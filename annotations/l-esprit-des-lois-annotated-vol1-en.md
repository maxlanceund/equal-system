# Annotations on *The Spirit of the Laws* · Volume I

● Montesquieu  
○ Lanceund


## Ⅰ The Definition of Law and the Nature of Things

● In the broadest sense, law is the necessary relation arising from the nature of things. In this sense, all beings have their law: the Divinity has its law, the material world its law, the intelligences superior to man their law, the beasts their law, and man his law.

○ Montesquieu extracts "law" from theology and royal authority, anchoring it in "reason" and "nature." But "the nature of things" itself is not fixed. It is something that condenses under specific historical conditions. Law is not derived from nature ─ it is fabricated. Projecting human society's "law" onto all beings is an illegitimate leap from a finite interval to an infinite one.


## Ⅱ Excluding Blind Necessity

● Some say that everything we see in the world is produced by blind necessity ─ this claim is absurd. For what is more absurd than to say that intelligent beings arise from blind necessity?

○ Montesquieu excludes "blind necessity" here in order to secure the foundation of law. But he overlooks one possibility: order can condense spontaneously from chaos. Humankind was not born from rational design, but gradually formed everything ─ including "law" ─ through continuous trial and error.


## Ⅲ Primitive Reason and God

● There is, then, a primitive reason; laws are the relations between that primitive reason and the various beings, and the relations among those beings themselves. The Divinity has relations to the universe, and the laws according to which it created are those according to which it preserves.

○ Montesquieu says that even God acts according to law. But the question is: is law created by God, or does God also obey it? If created, then there was no law before creation. If obeyed, then law precedes God ─ who then made it? He is using one assumption to support another. Human law does not need God to guarantee it.


## Ⅳ The Laws of the Universe as a Premise

● As we see, the world of matter, moved by motion and lacking intelligence, always exists. Its motion must therefore have invariable laws. Creation, though appearing arbitrary, implies invariable laws, like the eternal destiny proclaimed by atheists. Without law, the world would cease to exist.

○ Montesquieu first says the universe has laws, then says God must obey them too. But "the universe must have laws" is a premise he simply assumes, never argues. If God is omnipotent, why could He not create arbitrarily? He is merely using one hypothesis to prove another.


## Ⅴ Just Relations Before Positive Law

● Before intelligent beings existed, they were possible; they could have relations, and thus possible laws. Before laws were made, relations of justice and injustice were possible. To say that there is no justice or injustice until positive law prescribes or prohibits certain actions is like saying that before a circle is drawn, all its radii are unequal.

○ Montesquieu claims that just relations existed before human law. The problem is: if there were no people, where were those "just relations" stored? Concepts like "gratitude," "harm," and "punishment" presuppose people. Without people, there is no gratitude, no justice. He projects human relations onto the universe and says they were always there. In fact, they grew out of human life itself ─ they were not placed there in advance.


## Ⅵ Plants and Beasts "Obeying"

● We do not know whether beasts are governed by general laws of motion or by particular causes. ... They have natural law because they are connected by shared feeling, but they are not connected through knowledge, so they have no positive law. Yet beasts do not consistently obey natural law; it is plants, which have neither knowledge nor feeling, that obey it better.

○ Montesquieu says plants and beasts obey natural law, therefore natural law must exist. But plants and beasts are not "obeying" anything. They are simply surviving. Hunting, reproducing, avoiding danger ─ these actions are not because they "know" the law, but because otherwise they would die. The word "obey" already presupposes a subject that knows the rule and chooses whether to follow it. Plants and beasts have no such subject. They simply run their survival programs. Montesquieu equates "behavior conforming to regularity" with "consciously obeying regularity" ─ two entirely different things.


## Ⅶ The Dual Nature of Man and the Triple Constraint

● As physical beings, humans are governed by invariable laws like other matter. As intelligent beings, they continually violate the laws set by God. ... Such a being may forget its creator at any moment; religion reminds them through laws. Such a being may forget itself at any moment; philosophers remind them through morality. They come into the world to live in society, but may forget others; legislators remind them through political and civil laws.

○ Montesquieu says that philosophers can remind people, legislators can constrain people, and God can also remind people. But the first two are real; God is not. He places something non-existent alongside real things, pretending they are equally effective. People do need law and reminders. But those things grew out of human life itself ─ they were not given by God.
